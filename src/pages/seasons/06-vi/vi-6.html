Constantinople III (680–681)

c. 680–6813 minPreviously: Constantinople II (553)

…we likewise declare that in him are two natural wills and two natural operations indivisibly, inconvertibly, inseparably, inconfusedly, according to the teaching of the holy Fathers.

Starring

A. Historical context
To win back the anti-Chalcedonians in the provinces under threat from Persia and then from the Arabs, Patriarch Sergius of Constantinople and Emperor Heraclius promoted the formula of "one activity" (energeia) in Christ (Monenergism, 630s). After protests led by Sophronius of Jerusalem, this became the formula of "one will" (thelēma), set out in the Ekthesis (638) (Monothelitism). Pope Honorius (625–638), in correspondence with Sergius, had spoken of "one will" in Christ. Opposition was led by Maximus the Confessor and by Rome. The Lateran Synod of 649 under Pope Martin I condemned Monothelitism. Emperor Constans II had Martin arrested and exiled to Cherson, where he died (655). Maximus was mutilated and exiled, and died in 662.
B. Participants
About 150 bishops at the final session (fewer at first). Emperor Constantine IV presided at many sessions. Papal legates brought the letter of Pope Agatho and a Roman synod. Patriarch George of Constantinople came to accept dyothelitism. Macarius of Antioch defended Monothelitism and was deposed.1
C. Political context
By 680 Egypt and Syria had been lost to the Arab caliphate, so concessions to win over the anti-Chalcedonians no longer had a political purpose. Constantine IV sought reconciliation with Rome.
D. Theological issues
Whether will and activity belong to the person (so one in Christ) or to the nature (so two in Christ). Whether two wills imply conflict within Christ.
E. Competing arguments
Monothelites: one subject must have one will; two wills would mean two willing subjects in conflict (Gethsemane being read as the human will following the divine without resistance). Dyothelites (Maximus, Agatho): will is a property of nature; if Christ has a complete human nature he has a human will; "what is not assumed is not healed", and that applies to the human will. In Gethsemane ("not my will but yours") the human will freely submits to the divine.
F. Decisions
A definition affirming two natural wills and two natural activities. Condemnation of Sergius, Pyrrhus, Paul and Peter of Constantinople, Cyrus of Alexandria, Macarius of Antioch, and Pope Honorius.
G. Reception
Accepted in Rome by Pope Leo II (682), who confirmed the condemnation of Honorius, and by the Greek Church. Its reception among the Maronites is a contested historical question (Vol. XIII ch. 5). Not received by Oriental Orthodox or the Church of the East, who were not parties to it.2
H. Consequences
The end of imperial attempts to reach Christological compromise. Doctrinal unity between Rome and Constantinople restored for a time. The condemnation of a pope by an ecumenical council became a major point in nineteenth-century debates on papal infallibility.
I. Primary sources

…we likewise declare that in him are two natural wills and two natural operations indivisibly, inconvertibly, inseparably, inconfusedly, according to the teaching of the holy Fathers. And these two natural wills are not contrary the one to the other (God forbid!) as the impious heretics assert, but his human will follows and that not as resisting and reluctant, but rather as subject to his divine and omnipotent will.

Constantinople III, Definition of Faith (Session 18, 16 September 681). Trans. Percival, NPNF II/14 (1900). Checked. Q33 · checked
The Session 13 sentence rejects the letters of Sergius and "Honorius some time Pope of Old Rome" as "foreign to the apostolic dogmas" (Q34, checked).
J. Historical controversies
The "Honorius question". Was Honorius condemned as a heretic, or for negligence in failing to suppress heresy? Leo II's confirmation speaks of his having permitted the faith to be stained, and Catholic apologists of 1870 stressed this. Did his letters intend a moral unity of will rather than a single faculty? Critics of papal infallibility at Vatican I (notably Döllinger and Hefele) argued that the condemnation was incompatible with the doctrine.3
K. Legacy
Completed the classical Christological settlement. Maximus the Confessor became a central figure in both Orthodox and Catholic theology.
