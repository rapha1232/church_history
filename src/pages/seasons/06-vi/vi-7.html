Nicaea II (787)

c. 7873 minPreviously: Constantinople III (680–681)

For the honour which is paid to the image passes on to that which the image represents, and he who reveres the image reveres in it the subject represented.

Starring

A. Historical context
Byzantine iconoclasm: imperial policy against religious images, conventionally dated from Leo III (from 726/730) and certainly pursued by Constantine V. Constantine V's council of Hieria (754) condemned images. After his death, Empress Irene, regent for her son Constantine VI, reversed this policy. A first attempt to hold a council in Constantinople (786) was broken up by iconoclast soldiers. Narrative in Vol. VIII ch. 3.
B. Participants
About 350 bishops, together with many monks. Presided over by Patriarch Tarasius, a former lay official. Legates of Pope Hadrian I attended, as did two monks claiming to represent the Eastern patriarchs under Islamic rule, whose credentials were questionable.
C. Political context
Irene's regency; the purge of iconoclast officers; Byzantine efforts to improve relations with Rome. Frankish exclusion from the process later fed Charlemagne's hostile response.
D. Theological issues
Whether images of Christ, Mary and the saints are idolatrous. Whether an image of Christ either divides his natures (by depicting only the humanity) or confuses them (by claiming to depict the divinity), as Hieria had argued. Whether images may be venerated.
E. Competing arguments
Iconoclasts: the prohibition of images in the Decalogue (Exod. 20:4); the only true image of Christ is the Eucharist; images cannot depict divinity. Iconophiles (John of Damascus, Patriarch Germanos, later Theodore the Studite and Nikephoros): the incarnation made the invisible God visible, so to refuse to depict Christ is to deny the incarnation. Images depict the person (hypostasis), not a nature. The veneration given to images passes to their prototype (Basil, Q10). Veneration (proskynēsis) is distinct from worship (latreia), which is due to God alone.
F. Decisions
A definition restoring images and their veneration. Twenty-two canons, including canon 7 requiring relics in the consecration of churches. Condemnation of Hieria.
G. Reception
Iconoclasm was renewed in Byzantium (815–843). The decisive restoration came on 11 March 843, still celebrated as the "Triumph of Orthodoxy" on the first Sunday of Lent. Rome accepted the council. The Franks, working from a defective Latin translation that seemed to require images to be adored like the Trinity, rejected it in the Opus Caroli regis (Libri Carolini) and at the Council of Frankfurt (794). The West came to count it as the seventh ecumenical council only gradually, by the late ninth century. Most Protestant traditions reject its decisions on images.
H. Consequences
It fixed the theology of the icon that is central to Orthodox identity. It exposed the difference in religious culture between Byzantium and the Frankish West. With the Filioque, it supplied an issue in Carolingian polemic against the "Greeks".

J. Historical controversies
When iconoclasm began, and how far Leo III was an active iconoclast. Leslie Brubaker and John Haldon argue that most narrative evidence for the 720s–730s comes from later iconophile writers and is unreliable.1 The representativeness of the "Eastern" delegates. The precise Frankish misunderstanding.2
K. Legacy
The last council recognised as ecumenical by both Catholic and Orthodox Churches. A pivotal moment in the history of Christian art.
