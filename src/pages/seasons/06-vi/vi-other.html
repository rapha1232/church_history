Other councils and synods of the period

Starring

These councils are not counted as ecumenical by the Eastern Orthodox. Several of them are central to the history of authority and division.

Antioch, “Dedication Council” (341)

About 90–97 Eastern bishops met for the dedication of Constantine's Golden Church. They produced several creeds avoiding homoousios and rejected Marcellus. Its canons, possibly originating from an earlier Antiochene (c. 328), entered Eastern and Western canon law. They include canon 9, on the system, and canons 4 and 12, on deposed bishops appealing to the emperor.1

Serdica (c. 343)

Summoned by Constans and Constantius to resolve the Athanasius affair. The Easterners refused to sit with Athanasius and Marcellus, met separately, and excommunicated Julius of Rome. The Westerners, under Ossius, issued canons, including canons 3–5 (Latin numbering varies) providing for a deposed bishop to appeal to the bishop of Rome, who might order a retrial by neighbouring bishops:

…let us, if it seem good to your charity, honour the memory of Peter the Apostle, and let those who gave judgment write to Julius, the bishop of Rome, so that, if necessary, the case may be retried by the bishops of the neighbouring provinces and let him appoint arbiters…

Serdica, canon 3 (Greek). Trans. Percival, NPNF II/14 (1900). Checked. Q25 · checked
Rome later cited these canons as "Nicene", a mistake exposed by the African bishops in the Apiarius affair (419). The Eastern Church received Serdica's canons through the Trullan council (692).2

Ephesus II (449)

Summoned as ecumenical and presided over by Dioscorus. It reinstated Eutyches and deposed Flavian, Domnus, Ibas and Theodoret. Annulled at Chalcedon. Leo's term latrocinium stuck. The Oriental Orthodox do not count it among the ecumenical councils, but they reject the condemnation of Dioscorus that followed it. See Vol. IV ch. 12.

Lateran Synod (649)

A Roman synod under Martin I, with substantial input from Maximus and Greek monks in Rome. It condemned and the imperial Typos (648). Its acts were composed in Greek and Latin. Recent research (Riedinger) shows they were drafted in advance, largely by Greek monks.3

Quinisext / Council in Trullo (691–692)

Convened by Justinian II to issue the disciplinary canons that the fifth and sixth councils had not issued (hence "fifth-sixth"). Its 102 canons form the backbone of Orthodox canon law. Several criticised Roman practices: canon 13 defends married priests living with their wives; canon 55 condemns fasting on Saturdays in Lent as practised in Rome; canon 36 re-enacts Constantinople 3 and Chalcedon 28. Pope Sergius I refused to sign, and an imperial attempt to arrest him was thwarted by Italian troops. Anastasius the Librarian (9th century) reports that Rome later accepted the canons only insofar as they did not contradict earlier canons, papal decrees and good custom.

Hieria (754)

About 338 bishops under Constantine V condemned images. No patriarchs were present: the see of Constantinople was vacant, and the others were under Islamic rule. Its definition survives because it was quoted for refutation at Nicaea II. Its decisions were repudiated in 787.

Frankfurt (794)

A Frankish synod under Charlemagne, with papal legates present. It condemned Spanish Adoptionism and rejected the "Greek synod" on images, meaning Nicaea II as known through a faulty translation. See Vol. VIII ch. 3.

Constantinople 861, 869–870 and 879–880 (the Photian councils)

861: with papal legates present, confirmed the deposition of Ignatius and the election of Photius. Pope Nicholas I repudiated his legates' actions. 869–870: under Basil I and in the presence of Hadrian II's legates, condemned Photius and restored Ignatius. Counted by the Catholic Church as the Eighth Ecumenical Council (Constantinople IV), a count that became established in Western canon law from the late eleventh century. 879–880: with the legates of John VIII, restored Photius, annulled the council of 869–870, and reaffirmed the creed without additions. Some Orthodox theologians call it the "Eighth Ecumenical Council", though it has not been formally proclaimed as such. See Vol. VIII ch. 6.

Constantinople 1341, 1347, 1351 (Hesychast councils)

Endorsed Gregory Palamas' distinction between God's unknowable essence and his uncreated against Barlaam of Calabria and Gregory Akindynos. They are authoritative in Orthodoxy, and some Orthodox writers call them a "ninth ecumenical council". See Vol. IX ch. 6.

Medieval Western councils relevant to East–West relations

Lateran IV (1215: canon 4 on Greek practices, and canon 5 on patriarchal order), Lyon II (1274) and Ferrara-Florence (1438–1445). See Vol. IX ch. 4. The full treatment of medieval Western councils belongs to Volume X.

Why it matters today
