Nicaea I (325)

c. 3253 min

Let the ancient customs in Egypt, Libya and Pentapolis prevail, that the Bishop of Alexandria have jurisdiction in all these, since the like is customary for the Bishop of Rome also.

Starring

A. Historical context
The dispute between Arius and Bishop Alexander of Alexandria (from c. 318–321) had divided the Eastern episcopate. Constantine had just become sole emperor (324) and wanted religious unity. Other issues were outstanding too: the date of Easter, and the Melitian schism in Egypt. Narrative in Vol. IV ch. 5–6.
B. Participants
Between about 250 and 300 bishops, overwhelmingly from the Greek East; tradition later fixed the number at 318. Leading figures: Alexander of Alexandria with his deacon Athanasius; Eustathius of Antioch; Eusebius of Caesarea; Eusebius of Nicomedia; Marcellus of Ancyra; Ossius of Córdoba. Pope Silvester did not attend and was represented by the presbyters Vitus and Vincentius. Bishops also came from Persia (a "John the Persian" is listed in some subscription lists), Armenia and Gothia.1
C. Political context
Constantine convened the council, paid for travel, opened it in person and took part in debate. Eusebius credits him with proposing or endorsing homoousios. The council's sentences of exile were enforced by imperial authority.
D. Theological issues
Whether the Son is eternal and of the same divine being as the Father, or a creature brought into existence by the Father's will. Which terms could express the answer without falling into modalism (Sabellianism).
E. Competing arguments
Arius and his sympathisers: only the Father is unbegotten and without beginning; to say the Son is "from the Father's substance" implies division or emanation in God; Scripture calls the Son "firstborn of all creation" (Col. 1:15) and "created" (Prov. 8:22, LXX). Alexander and his allies: Scripture calls the Son God, Word, Wisdom and Image, eternal with the Father (John 1:1; Heb. 1:3); a creature cannot reveal or save; "Father" implies an eternal Son. Many bishops were uneasy with non-scriptural terminology but more uneasy with Arius' statements.
F. Decisions
The Creed with anathemas; twenty canons (including canon 6 on Alexandria, Rome and Antioch, canon 7 on Jerusalem, and canon 19 on followers of Paul of Samosata); a settlement of the Melitian schism; a decision for a common Easter. Arius was exiled with Theonas and Secundus.
G. Reception
Accepted by all historic Churches: Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, the Church of the East (adopted at the Persian synod of 410), and the main Protestant confessions. In the decades after 325, however, its creed was contested and frequently bypassed. Its authority was secured only by the 360s–380s (Vol. IV ch. 7).
H. Consequences
Short term: renewed conflict, the rehabilitation of Arius (who died in 336, before he could be readmitted to communion), and the exile of Athanasius. Long term: the precedent of imperially summoned councils issuing binding creeds, and the use of non-scriptural technical language in dogma.
I. Primary sources
The Creed (quoted in full, Vol. IV ch. 6). Canon 6:

Let the ancient customs in Egypt, Libya and Pentapolis prevail, that the Bishop of Alexandria have jurisdiction in all these, since the like is customary for the Bishop of Rome also.

Nicaea, canon 6. Trans. Percival, NPNF II/14 (1900). The opening clause was checked; the remainder follows the same translation and was checked for sense. Q24 · partly checked
J. Historical controversies
The number of participants; how far Constantine shaped the creed; whether homoousios was proposed by Ossius or the Western delegation; the authenticity of the Arabic "canons of Nicaea", which are later compilations (Misconceptions). Canon 6 is read differently. Catholic commentators often see it as recognising a pre-existing Roman authority used as a model. Orthodox and many historians read it as recognising regional supra-metropolitan authority, with Rome cited as a parallel.2
K. Legacy
The defining moment of Trinitarian orthodoxy and the model of a general council. Its 1700th anniversary was marked jointly by Pope Leo XIV, Ecumenical Patriarch Bartholomew and other Christian leaders at İznik on 28 November 2025. Their joint declaration of 29 November called the council "a providential event of unity".3
