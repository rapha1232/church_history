The narrative of the fourth- and fifth-century councils is given in Volume IV. To avoid repetition, the entries for Nicaea, Constantinople I, Ephesus and Chalcedon summarise that narrative and concentrate on the analytical headings.

What made a council “ecumenical”?

Oikoumenē meant "the inhabited world", in practice the Roman Empire. An "ecumenical" synod was in the first instance one summoned by the emperor for the whole empire. Being summoned as ecumenical did not guarantee recognition. The council of 449 was summoned as ecumenical and was rejected. Constantinople 381 was a regional Eastern council that was later accepted as ecumenical. What made a council's authority endure was its reception: acceptance by the Churches, and in particular by the five major sees and, in Western and later Catholic theology, by the bishop of Rome. How reception works, and whose acceptance is decisive, remains a point of difference between the traditions.
