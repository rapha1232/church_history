Chalcedon (451)

c. 4513 minPreviously: Ephesus (431)

For the Fathers rightly granted privileges to the throne of old Rome, because it was the royal city.

Starring

A. Historical context
The condemnation of Eutyches (448), Leo's Tome (449), the "Robber Council" of Ephesus (449), the death of Theodosius II (450). Narrative in Vol. IV ch. 12.
B. Participants
About 350–370 bishops, almost all Eastern; tradition says 630. Roman legates Paschasinus of Lilybaeum, Lucentius of Asculum and the presbyter Boniface. About 19 high imperial officials directed proceedings. Dioscorus attended the first session as a defendant.1
C. Political context
Marcian and Pulcheria wanted to reverse 449 and restore unity, and pressed for a written definition. Marcian attended Session 6 in person. Imperial commissioners managed the agenda, the order of business and the drafting.
D. Theological issues
How to affirm both the single subject (Cyril) and the completeness and distinction of divinity and humanity (Leo, the Antiochenes). Whether "in two natures" was compatible with Cyril.
E. Competing arguments
Dioscorus and his supporters: Cyril's "one incarnate nature"; "from two natures" is acceptable, "in two natures" is Nestorian; Ephesus canon 7 forbids a new definition. Leo and the Easterners loyal to Flavian: the Tome's "each form does what is proper to it"; to deny two natures after the union is Eutychian. Many bishops, including some of Dioscorus' former supporters, wanted Cyril without Eutyches. Passages of the Tome were compared with Cyril's writings before it was approved.
F. Decisions
The Definition of Faith (Q20). Deposition of Dioscorus. Rehabilitation of Theodoret and Ibas, after they anathematised Nestorius. Jerusalem raised to a patriarchate. Twenty-seven canons plus canons 28–30. The definition reaffirmed the creeds of 325 and 381, Cyril's Second Letter and the Letter to John, and Leo's Tome.
G. Reception
Rejected by most of Egypt and much of Syria, and by Armenia (after 506/555). Accepted by Rome, which rejected canon 28, and by the Greek Church. Not part of the Church of the East's tradition. Accepted as ecumenical by Catholic, Eastern Orthodox and most Protestant Churches.2
H. Consequences
The division between Chalcedonian and non-Chalcedonian Churches (Vol. V ch. 2). Two further councils (553, 680–81) tried to reconcile the two. Canon 28 strained Rome–Constantinople relations (Vol. VII ch. 3).
I. Primary sources
Definition (Q20); acclamation "Peter has spoken thus through Leo" (Q19); canon 28:

For the Fathers rightly granted privileges to the throne of old Rome, because it was the royal city. And the One Hundred and Fifty most religious Bishops, actuated by the same consideration, gave equal privileges (ἴσα πρεσβεῖα) to the most holy throne of New Rome, justly judging that the city which is honoured with the Sovereignty and the Senate, and enjoys equal privileges with the old imperial Rome, should in ecclesiastical matters also be magnified as she is, and rank next after her…

Chalcedon, canon 28. Trans. Percival, NPNF II/14 (1900). Checked. Q21 · checked
Canon 9 (on appeals):

And if a bishop or clergyman should have a difference with the of the province, let him have recourse to the of the Diocese, or to the throne of the Imperial City of Constantinople, and there let it be tried.

Chalcedon, canon 9 (final clause). Trans. Percival, NPNF II/14. Checked. Q22 · checked
J. Historical controversies
"In" or "from" two natures in the original text. Whether canon 28 was validly passed (in the legates' absence) and what "equal privileges" meant. Whether Leo's confirmation (Letter 114, March 453) was required for the council's validity or was an act of reception. How far the outcome was imposed by the state. Whether the Tome was "received" on its own authority or after examination (Q19 discussion).3
K. Legacy
The Chalcedonian Definition remains the reference point for Christology in Catholic, Orthodox and most Protestant theology. It is also the most important single cause of division among the ancient Churches, and the subject of the most productive modern dialogues (Vol. V ch. 5).
