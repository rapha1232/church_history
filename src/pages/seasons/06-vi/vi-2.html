Constantinople I (381)

c. 3814 minPreviously: Nicaea I (325)

The council that met in Constantinople from May to July 381 was summoned by Theodosius I and consisted of around 150 Eastern bishops. No Western bishops and no papal legates attended.

Starring

A. Historical context
The end of Homoian imperial policy after Valens' death (378). Theodosius' law Cunctos populos (380). A disputed succession at Antioch (Meletius vs Paulinus). The need to secure the Nicene faith in the capital, where Gregory of Nazianzus had led a small Nicene congregation. Narrative in Vol. IV ch. 9.
B. Participants
About 150 Eastern bishops (36 "Macedonians" attended but withdrew). No Western bishops and no Roman legates. Presidents: Meletius of Antioch (d. during the council), Gregory of Nazianzus (resigned), Nectarius. Egyptian bishops arrived late and objected to Gregory's appointment.1
C. Political context
Summoned by Theodosius. Its decisions were enforced by imperial law (CTh 16.1.3, 30 July 381, naming bishops in communion with whom orthodoxy was to be judged).
D. Theological issues
The divinity of the Holy Spirit; the remaining non-Nicene positions; Apollinarianism.
E. Competing arguments
The Pneumatomachians argued that Scripture does not call the Spirit "God" and that the Spirit is a ministering power. The Cappadocians argued from baptism in the threefold name, from the Spirit's work of sanctification and deification, and from worship. Only God can make us divine, and what is worshipped with the Father and Son must share their being.
F. Decisions
A doctrinal "tome" (lost). Canons 1–4 (Percival's numbering lists seven, but canons 5–7 are probably from a later synod of 382 or later): condemnation of heresies; limitation of bishops to their own civil "dioceses"; canon 3 on Constantinople's rank; invalidation of the ordination of Maximus the Cynic. The creed attributed to the council (see the debate in Vol. IV ch. 9).
G. Reception
Initially an Eastern synod. Its ecumenical standing was established when Chalcedon (451) read its creed alongside Nicaea's. Rome accepted its doctrine but not canon 3 until much later. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) recognised the Latin patriarch of Constantinople in second place; the Council of Florence (1439) listed the patriarchal order with Constantinople second. Accepted as ecumenical by Catholic, Orthodox and Oriental Orthodox Churches, and recognised by the Church of the East.2
H. Consequences
Completion of the doctrine of the Trinity in conciliar form; elevation of Constantinople; the beginning of Alexandria–Constantinople rivalry, visible at Ephesus (431) and in 449–451.
I. Primary sources
The creed's article on the Spirit (Vol. IV ch. 9, Q11) and canon 3 (Q12).
J. Historical controversies
The origin of the creed; the authenticity and numbering of canons 5–7; whether the council intended canon 3 as a slight to Alexandria rather than Rome.3
K. Legacy
The Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed is the most widely shared text in Christianity. Its original text, without the Filioque, became central to East–West controversy (Vol. VIII ch. 5).

In depth

Why Constantinople I is counted as ecumenical

The council that met in Constantinople from May to July 381 was summoned by Theodosius I and consisted of around 150 Eastern bishops. No Western bishops and no papal legates attended. Meletius of Antioch presided until his death during the council; Gregory of Nazianzus then presided briefly, faced opposition, and resigned the see of Constantinople. Nectarius, a layman not yet baptized, was chosen in his place.

The council reaffirmed Nicaea, condemned the Macedonians or Pneumatomachi ('fighters against the Spirit'), who denied the full divinity of the Holy Spirit, and condemned Apollinarius. Its canons included canon 3, giving Constantinople second place in honour after Rome 'because it is New Rome', a canon Rome did not accept for centuries (VII.3).

The creed now called the Nicene Creed (see the full text) is first attested in the acts of Chalcedon in 451, where it was read as the creed of the 150 fathers. No acts of 381 survive. Scholars debate whether the council composed it, adapted an existing baptismal creed, or approved it in some other form. Its new section on the Spirit avoids calling the Spirit or 'God' outright, perhaps to win over moderates, but says that he is 'worshipped and glorified together with the Father and the Son'.

The council's ecumenical status came later. Rome accepted its creed in the sixth century but long rejected canon 3. Its authority rests on : Chalcedon read its creed alongside that of Nicaea, and later councils counted it second in the list.

Why it matters today
