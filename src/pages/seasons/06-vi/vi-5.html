Constantinople II (553)

c. 5534 minPreviously: Chalcedon (451)

If anyone defends the impious Theodore of Mopsuestia, who has said that the Word of God is one person, but that another person is Christ…

Starring

A. Historical context
Justinian sought to reconcile the anti-Chalcedonians without abandoning Chalcedon. Anti-Chalcedonians objected especially that Chalcedon had rehabilitated Theodoret and Ibas and failed to condemn Theodore of Mopsuestia. In 543/544 Justinian issued an edict condemning these "Three Chapters": the person and works of Theodore, certain writings of Theodoret against Cyril, and a letter of Ibas to Mari the Persian. He also condemned Origenist teachings, partly in response to disputes in Palestinian monasteries.
B. Participants
About 150–165 bishops, almost all Eastern, under Patriarch Eutychius of Constantinople. Pope Vigilius was in Constantinople, having been effectively detained there since 547, but refused to attend.1
C. Political context
The council's agenda was Justinian's. The treatment of Vigilius is the period's most striking case of imperial pressure on a pope. In 551 Vigilius took refuge in the church of St Euphemia at Chalcedon, and the council later struck his name from the diptychs. He issued a Constitutum (May 553) refusing to condemn the dead who had died in communion with the Church. After the council he reversed himself and accepted its decisions (December 553 and a second Constitutum, February 554). He died on his way back to Rome in 555.2
D. Theological issues
Whether one could condemn persons who had died in the Church's communion. Whether condemning the Three Chapters undermined Chalcedon. How to interpret Chalcedon in a Cyrillian sense: "Neo-Chalcedonianism", which used the formula "one of the Trinity suffered in the flesh" and allowed "one incarnate nature" if correctly understood.
E. Competing arguments
For condemnation: Theodore's Christology was the source of Nestorius' error, and removing it would show the anti-Chalcedonians that Chalcedon was not Nestorian. Against (most Western bishops, especially in Africa and northern Italy): Chalcedon had accepted Ibas and Theodoret; condemning them impugned Chalcedon; condemning the dead was unprecedented.
F. Decisions
Fourteen anathemas (the "Capitula") defining Chalcedon in Cyrillian terms and condemning the Three Chapters; condemnation of Origen, whose name appears in anathema 11. The fifteen anathemas against Origenism usually attached to this council were probably adopted by the bishops before the council formally opened.
G. Reception
Accepted by the Greek Church and, after Vigilius' acceptance, by Rome. The bishops of northern Italy (Aquileia, Milan) broke communion with Rome in the "Schism of the Three Chapters", which lasted in Aquileia until about 698–700. The council did not reconcile the anti-Chalcedonians. The Church of the East rejected it as a condemnation of its revered Interpreter.3
H. Consequences
Theological clarification of Chalcedon in a Cyrillian direction, which is standard in Byzantine theology. Further alienation of the Church of the East. Damage to papal prestige in the West.

J. Historical controversies
Whether the anti-Origenist canons belong to the council. How far later Byzantine summaries reshape the acta (the Greek acta survive only in part; the Latin version may have been edited to favour Vigilius). Whether Vigilius' reversal was free.
K. Legacy
Defines the Byzantine reading of Chalcedon. A test case in debates about the relationship of pope and council.

In depth

Constantinople II (553): Justinian and the Three Chapters

Justinian wanted to win back the majority in Egypt and Syria. He thought that condemning three figures revered by the Antiochene tradition would show that Chalcedon was not '': Theodore of Mopsuestia (d. 428), the writings of Theodoret of Cyrrhus against Cyril, and a letter of Ibas of Edessa. His edict of about 544 condemning these '' alarmed Western bishops, since Chalcedon had accepted Theodoret and Ibas.

Pope Vigilius was brought to Constantinople in 547. He condemned the Three Chapters in 548 (the Iudicatum), withdrew under Western protest, refused to attend the council, and issued a Constitutum that spared the persons of Theodoret and Ibas. He was put under heavy pressure, and in 553–554 he finally accepted the council's decisions.

The council met in May and June 553 with about 150 bishops, mostly Eastern. Its fourteen anathemas condemned the Three Chapters and interpreted Chalcedon in the light of Cyril: Christ's one is the eternal Word, 'one of the Trinity'. Anathemas against Origenist teachings are associated with the council, but whether the council itself adopted them is debated.

The miaphysites were not won back. In the West, the bishops of northern Italy broke communion with Rome over the council; this 'Three Chapters schism' in Aquileia lasted until about 700.
