The Church of the East

Starring

The Church of the East is the historic Church of Syriac-speaking Christians in Mesopotamia and Persia, outside the Roman Empire. It is often called "the Church". That name was applied by its opponents and has been rejected by the Church itself, and it gives a misleading account of the Church's origins. The Church of the East did not begin as a breakaway from the Council of Ephesus. It already existed, and was already organised separately, before Nestorius was born.

Origins in Mesopotamia

Christianity reached northern Mesopotamia and Adiabene (around Erbil) by the second century. Tradition attributes the mission to the apostle Thomas and to Addai (Thaddaeus) and his disciple Mari. These traditions are attested in later sources such as the Doctrine of Addai (c. 400), and their details cannot be confirmed. The first secure evidence comes from the third century. Bishops are attested in the Persian Empire, which the Sasanian dynasty ruled from 224. Romans deported from Syria by Shapur I (in the 250s–260s) brought many Christians into Persia.1

Christians in Persia were a minority under a Zoroastrian monarchy. After Constantine they could be suspected of sympathy with Rome. Under Shapur II (c. 340–379), Persian Christians suffered severe persecution, recorded in the Syriac Acts of the Persian Martyrs.

Becoming a self-governing Church: 410, 424, 486

The Church of the East's constitution was laid down in a series of synods whose acts survive in the Synodicon Orientale, edited by J.-B. Chabot (1902).2

  • 410, Seleucia-Ctesiphon (Synod of Isaac). Held with the permission of Shah Yazdegerd I and in the presence of the Roman envoy Marutha of Maypherqat. It adopted the Creed and canons of Nicaea and recognised the bishop of Seleucia-Ctesiphon as head ("") of the Persian Church, with metropolitans under him.
  • 420, Synod of Yahballaha I. Accepted further Western canons.
  • 424, Markabta (Synod of Dadisho'). Declared that the Catholicos could not be judged by the "Western" (Roman-empire) bishops, and that any complaint against him must be settled within the Persian Church. This was a declaration of jurisdictional independence, not a doctrinal schism. It came before the Council of Ephesus.
  • 486, Synod of Acacius. Adopted a Christological statement affirming two natures in one Christ and rejecting "mixture". It also permitted clergy, including bishops, to marry. An earlier at Beth Lapat (484) under Barsauma of Nisibis had moved in the same direction, though its acts were later set aside.

The theological orientation of the Church of the East was shaped less by Nestorius than by Theodore of Mopsuestia (d. 428), whose biblical commentaries were translated into Syriac at the "School of the Persians" in Edessa. When the emperor Zeno closed that school in 489, its teachers moved across the border to Nisibis. The School of Nisibis became the intellectual centre of the Church. Theodore was venerated there as "the Interpreter". Nestorius, too, is commemorated in the Church's liturgy as a Greek doctor. He was never its founder, and the Church did not take its theology from him.3

The definitive Christological formulation came in the early seventh century from Babai the Great (d. 628): two natures (kyānē), two qnōmē, one parṣōpā (person) of Christ. On the meaning of qnōmā, see Key terms. The Church of the East did not accept the Council of Ephesus (431), which had condemned Nestorius. Nor did it accept Constantinople II (553), which condemned Theodore. It therefore stands outside the conciliar tradition of the Byzantine and Latin Churches from 431 onwards, while professing the faith of Nicaea.

Expansion across Asia

From the fifth to the fourteenth century the Church of the East was geographically the most extensive Church in the world.

  • India. The Saint Thomas Christians of the Malabar coast received bishops from Persia. Copper-plate grants from Kollam (Quilon), datable to around 849, and "Persian crosses" with Pahlavi inscriptions document an established community. Their own tradition of evangelisation by the apostle Thomas in AD 52 cannot be independently verified.
  • Central Asia. sees existed at Merv and Herat by the sixth century, and Christian communities among Sogdians, Turks and later the Uyghurs. Hundreds of Syriac-Turkic gravestones (13th–14th c.) survive from the Chüy valley in Kyrgyzstan.
  • China. The monk Aluoben (Alopen) reached the Tang capital Chang'an in 635. The Chinese-Syriac stele erected in 781 (now in the Beilin Museum, Xi'an) records the mission's history and the "Luminous Teaching" (Jingjiao). The stele was rediscovered c. 1623–25. Its authenticity was once doubted by some European writers but is now accepted.4 The mission declined after the anti-foreign persecution of 845. It reappeared under the Mongol Yuan dynasty (1271–1368).
  • Under Islam. After the Arab conquests the Catholicos moved, under Timothy I (780–823), to Baghdad. Syriac Christian scholars were central to the translation of Greek philosophy and science into Arabic. Timothy's letters mention metropolitans for Turks and for China (Beth Sinaye).
  • Mongol period. Two Uyghur or Öngüt monks travelled from Khanbaliq (Beijing) westwards. One, Markos, became Catholicos Yahballaha III (1281–1317). The other, Rabban Sauma, travelled as an envoy to Constantinople, Rome, Paris and Bordeaux in 1287–88, met Pope Nicholas IV, and received communion from him. His Syriac travel account survives.5

Decline and later divisions

The conversion of the Ilkhanate to Islam (1295), the Black Death and the campaigns of Timur (late 14th century) devastated the Church outside its Mesopotamian and Kurdish mountain heartlands. In 1552, a dispute over hereditary patriarchal succession led a party to elect Yohannan Sulaqa, who went to Rome and entered communion with Pope Julius III (1553). From this and later unions descends the Chaldean Catholic Church, an Eastern Catholic Church whose patriarchate was consolidated in its present form in 1830. The historic hereditary line continued as the Assyrian Church of the East, which formally adopted that name in 1976. A dispute in the 1960s, partly over adopting the Gregorian calendar and hereditary succession, produced the Ancient Church of the East (1968), based in Baghdad. The Saint Thomas Christians of India also divided after Portuguese latinisation at the Synod of Diamper (1599). See ch. 2.6

Key takeaways

  • The Church of the East's independence was jurisdictional (410–424) before it was doctrinal, and it was shaped by the Persian political context.
  • Its theology descends from Theodore of Mopsuestia and the School of Nisibis. "Nestorian" is a polemical misnomer.
  • In 1994 the Catholic Church and the Assyrian Church of the East declared that they share the same faith in Christ (ch. 5).

Why it matters today

The Assyrian Church of the East and its daughter churches still pray in Syriac; their 1994 agreement with Rome ended a 1,500-year dispute.
