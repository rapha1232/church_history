The prefix matters. Mia (μία) means "one" in the sense of a single united reality. Monos (μόνος) suggests "one alone". The Oriental Orthodox Churches use "" and reject "monophysite". The distinction is not modern special pleading. Severus of Antioch wrote at length against the Eutychian Sergius the Grammarian and against the Julianists, who held that Christ's body was incorruptible before the resurrection. In doing so he defended the reality of Christ's human nature, activity and suffering.1

Why did the division happen?

Historians weigh several factors. Their relative importance remains debated.

  • Theology and terminology. The parties used physis in different senses (Key terms). Each heard the other's formula as heresy: "two natures" as division, "one nature" as Eutychian confusion. Modern dialogues have concluded the disagreement was largely verbal, while acknowledging real differences in emphasis.
  • Loyalty to Cyril and Alexandria. In Egypt, Chalcedon's deposition of Dioscorus was felt as an attack on Cyril's see and legacy.
  • Imperial coercion. Forcible installation of Chalcedonian bishops, sometimes with troops, hardened resistance and associated Chalcedon with imperial power. Hence the name "" ("imperial") for the Chalcedonians of the East.
  • Language and regional identity. An older theory saw the schisms as expressions of Coptic and Syrian "nationalism" against Greek rule. A. H. M. Jones and W. H. C. Frend argued about this in the 1950s–70s, and most historians now consider it anachronistic. Anti-Chalcedonian leaders such as Severus and Timothy Aelurus wrote in Greek and thought of themselves as the true Church of the empire. Language and region did, however, shape how communities later remembered the councils.2
  • Jurisdiction. Chalcedon's canon 28 and its rearrangement of the Antiochene and Jerusalem jurisdictions gave ecclesiastical-political grievances a doctrinal expression.
  • The Arab conquests froze the division by ending imperial power to enforce or negotiate union.

In depth

One nature, two natures: why the words still matter

Cyril of Alexandria often used the phrase 'one incarnate nature (mia ) of God the Word'. He meant that Christ is one living reality, the eternal Word who became fully human, not two subjects joined together. The phrase, which Cyril believed came from Athanasius, actually originated with Apollinarius.

Eutyches, an archimandrite of Constantinople, went further, saying that after the union Christ's humanity was not 'of one substance with us'. He was condemned in 448 and again at Chalcedon in 451, whose definition confessed Christ 'in two natures, without confusion, without change, without division, without separation'.

Churches that rejected Chalcedon, the Coptic, Syriac, Armenian and Ethiopian traditions among them, also rejected Eutyches. They held that 'in two natures' risked dividing Christ, as Nestorius had seemed to. Scholars now usually call them 'miaphysite' rather than 'monophysite', a term their opponents used to imply Eutychianism. The Church of the East, which stressed the two natures most strongly, is called ''.

Part of the problem was language: physis, and meant different things to different writers, and Greek, Syriac and Coptic terms did not map neatly onto each other. Politics mattered too: rivalry between Alexandria and Constantinople, and imperial pressure. Since the 1960s official dialogues have concluded that the Chalcedonian and non-Chalcedonian churches confess the same faith in different words.

Why it matters today

Recognising that different words can express the same faith is the method behind today’s ecumenical agreements.
