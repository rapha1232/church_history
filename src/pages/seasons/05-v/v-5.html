Modern Christological dialogues

c. 1964–20264 minPreviously: How the families of Churches formed

Starring

From the 1960s, theologians of the separated Eastern Churches and of the Catholic Church re-examined the fifth-century disputes. They found substantial agreement in faith behind the opposed formulas. The dialogues did not restore communion, because questions of authority, the of councils and the status of persons anathematised by one side remain open. But they changed the official stance of several Churches towards the others.

In pictures1 images · tap to enlarge

Eastern Orthodox and Oriental Orthodox

  • Aarhus (1964), Bristol (1967), Geneva (1970), Addis Ababa (1971). Unofficial consultations, held in connection with the World Council of Churches, concluded that the two families share the same Christological faith.
  • Official Joint Commission. Its First Agreed Statement (Anba Bishoy monastery, Egypt, 1989) and Second Agreed Statement (Chambésy, 1990) declared both families faithful to the same Orthodox Christology. They proposed that the anathemas pronounced against each other's councils and fathers be lifted. As of 2026 the Churches have not implemented that proposal by a common act of reception.1

Catholic and Oriental Orthodox

  • 10 May 1973. Pope Paul VI and Coptic Pope Shenouda III signed a common declaration of faith in Christ.
  • 23 June 1984. Pope John Paul II and Syriac Orthodox Ignatius Zakka I Iwas did the same. Their declaration also permitted limited pastoral sacramental sharing in cases of need.
  • 13 December 1996. John Paul II and Armenian Karekin I.
  • Since 2003. An official Joint International Commission has met regularly. Its Executive Committee met in Rome on 12–13 May 2026 to assess twenty years of dialogue.2

Catholic and Assyrian Church of the East

On 11 November 1994 Pope John Paul II and Catholicos-Patriarch Mar Dinkha IV signed a Common Christological Declaration. It stated that the Catholic title "Mother of God" and the Assyrian "Mother of Christ our God and Saviour" express the same faith:

We both recognize the legitimacy and rightness of these expressions of the same faith…

Common Christological Declaration between the Catholic Church and the Assyrian Church of the East, 11 November 1994 (vatican.va). Short quotation from a copyrighted text. Q38 · checked

The declaration attributes past divisions largely to misunderstanding. It does not establish full communion. In 2001 the Holy See recognised the validity of the ancient Anaphora of Addai and Mari, a Eucharistic prayer that lacks an explicit institution narrative, in guidelines for Eucharistic sharing between the Chaldean Catholic and Assyrian Churches. A joint commission continues to meet; its seventeenth plenary was held in Rome on 27–28 October 2025.3

What the dialogues did and did not do

  • Did: establish that the Christological formulas of 431 and 451 need not be read as contradictory confessions.
  • Did not: settle the status of the councils themselves, or of figures venerated by one side and anathematised by the other (Dioscorus and Severus; Leo; Nestorius and Theodore), or questions of church authority.

In depth

How the old Christological divisions were revisited

Unofficial consultations between Eastern Orthodox and Oriental Orthodox theologians began at Aarhus in 1964. The official joint commission's agreed statements of 1989 and 1990 (Chambésy) declared that both families had 'always loyally guarded the same authentic Orthodox Christological faith', despite using different terms. Neither side has yet formally lifted all historic anathemas.

In 1973 Pope Paul VI and the Coptic Pope Shenouda III signed a common declaration confessing Christ as 'perfect God with respect to his divinity, perfect man with respect to his humanity', united 'without mingling, without commixtion, without confusion'. Similar declarations followed with the Syriac Orthodox Patriarch (1984) and the Armenian Catholicos (1996).

In 1994 Pope John Paul II and Mar Dinkha IV of the Assyrian Church of the East signed a Common Christological Declaration. Each side recognized the other's expressions of faith in Christ as legitimate, while acknowledging that the Assyrians do not use the title 'Mother of God'.

In 2001 Rome went further and recognized the validity of the celebrated with the ancient Anaphora of Addai and Mari, which has no explicit words of institution. A joint international commission between the Catholic Church and the Oriental Orthodox churches has met annually since 2003.

Why it matters today

Dialogues since the 1970s have produced common statements on Christ between churches divided since the fifth century.
