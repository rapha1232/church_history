Chalcedon’s aftermath and the Oriental Orthodox Churches

Starring

Chalcedon did not produce an immediate, clean split. It produced more than a century of contested . In Egypt and much of Syria, large parts of the Church regarded the council as having betrayed Cyril. Emperors alternately enforced Chalcedon, tried to set it aside, and sought compromise formulas. Separate hierarchies, and therefore separate Churches, emerged gradually, mainly between the 510s and the 570s.

Resistance and compromise (451–518)

  • Egypt. After Dioscorus' deposition the Proterius was installed at Alexandria. He was lynched in 457. The anti-Chalcedonian Timothy Aelurus was then consecrated, and Alexandria had rival bishops for long periods. Palestine saw a monastic revolt in 451–453.
  • The Henotikon (482). The emperor Zeno, with Acacius of Constantinople, issued an "instrument of union". It affirmed Nicaea, Constantinople and Ephesus and Cyril's Twelve Anathemas, condemned Nestorius and Eutyches, and passed over Chalcedon in silence, condemning anyone who held otherwise "whether at Chalcedon or at any whatever". It satisfied neither side fully.1
  • The Acacian schism (484–519). Pope Felix III excommunicated Acacius for this compromise, opening the first prolonged break in communion between Rome and Constantinople. It ended only in 519, when the emperor Justin I and John II accepted the Formula of Hormisdas. That document affirmed Chalcedon and the Tome and struck Acacius and his successors from the . Its claims for the Roman see are discussed in Vol. VII ch. 4.
  • Severus of Antioch. Patriarch of Antioch from 512, Severus was the most important theologian of the anti-Chalcedonian movement. He was deposed under Justin I in 518. A systematic Cyrillian, he rejected Eutyches as firmly as Nestorius. He affirmed that Christ's humanity is complete and consubstantial with us, but insisted that after the union one should speak of "one incarnate nature" and "one composite ", and that "two natures" after the union implied division.2

Separate hierarchies (518–c. 580)

From 518 Justin I and Justinian (527–565) removed anti-Chalcedonian bishops in Syria. Justinian's wife Theodora (d. 548) protected exiled anti-Chalcedonian leaders in the palace. The critical step was the consecration of new bishops independent of the imperial Church. Around 542/543, at the request of the Ghassanid Arab ruler al-Harith and with Theodora's support, the monk Jacob Baradaeus was consecrated bishop of Edessa. Travelling in disguise, he is said to have ordained dozens of bishops and thousands of clergy before his death in 578.3 Opponents called his Church "Jacobite", a name the Syriac Orthodox Church does not use for itself.

Justinian also tried theological reconciliation. He promoted "Neo-Chalcedonian" theology, which read Chalcedon through Cyril, and condemned the "" (writings of Theodore, Theodoret and Ibas). That condemnation was the business of Constantinople II (553). It failed to win back the anti-Chalcedonians, and it caused schism in the West (see Vol. VI). The seventh-century attempts at union through Monenergism and likewise failed (Constantinople III). The Arab conquests of the 630s–640s then removed Egypt and Syria from imperial control, and the separation became permanent.

The Oriental Orthodox Churches

The modern Oriental Orthodox communion consists of six Churches in full communion with one another. They accept the first three councils (Nicaea, Constantinople, Ephesus) and reject Chalcedon and later councils.

Coptic Orthodox Church

The majority Church of Egypt, continuing the line of Alexandrian popes through Dioscorus and Timothy Aelurus. Its head is the Pope of Alexandria and Patriarch of the See of St Mark. Coptic (the latest stage of Egyptian) was its liturgical and literary language. Arabic became dominant in daily use from about the 10th–13th centuries.4

Syriac Orthodox Church of Antioch

Descends from Severus and Jacob Baradaeus. Its patriarch, who uses the title of Antioch, has resided at various places, including Mor Hananyo monastery near Mardin, Homs and, since 1959, Damascus. Its liturgy is in Syriac.

Armenian Apostolic Church

Armenia's royal conversion under Tiridates and Gregory the Illuminator is traditionally dated 301. Many historians prefer c. 314. Armenia sent no bishops to Chalcedon: in 451 it was fighting Persia at the battle of Avarayr. Armenian synods at Dvin in 506 and c. 555 aligned the Church with the and then rejected Chalcedon, partly in reaction to Persian-sponsored Church of the East influence. The exact content and dates of these synods are debated.5 Its head is the of All Armenians at Etchmiadzin. There is also a Catholicosate of Cilicia, now at Antelias in Lebanon.

Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church

King Ezana of Aksum adopted Christianity in the mid-fourth century; his coinage shifts from pagan symbols to the cross. Frumentius was consecrated bishop by Athanasius. For sixteen centuries Ethiopia's (the abuna) was an Egyptian monk sent from Alexandria. The Church gained in stages: an Ethiopian archbishop in 1948–51 and a patriarch in 1959. Tewahedo means "made one", a reference to the union of natures in Christ.6

Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church

Separated from the Ethiopian Church after Eritrea's independence (1993). Its autocephaly was granted by the Coptic Orthodox Church in the 1990s, and its first patriarch was enthroned in 1998. The Ethiopian Church did not initially recognise the separation. In the 2000s the Eritrean government removed Patriarch Antonios, a removal the Coptic Church and others did not regard as canonical.

Malankara Orthodox Syrian Church

One branch of the Saint Thomas Christians of Kerala. After Portuguese latinisation (Synod of Diamper, 1599), the Coonan Cross Oath (1653) rejected Jesuit authority. Bishop Gregorios Abdul Jaleel of Jerusalem arrived in 1665 and brought West Syriac (Syriac Orthodox) liturgy and allegiance. A Catholicate was established in India in 1912. Disputes over whether the Church is autocephalous or under the Syriac Orthodox Patriarch of Antioch have divided the community. That side is now the Jacobite Syrian Christian Church, which is part of the Syriac Orthodox Church. The dispute has repeatedly gone to the Supreme Court of India.7

The Saint Thomas Christians today are divided among several Churches: the Syro-Malabar and Syro-Malankara Catholic Churches, the Malankara Orthodox and Jacobite Syrian Churches, the Mar Thoma Syrian Church (reformed in the 19th century under Anglican influence), and others. The Chaldean Syrian Church in Thrissur belongs to the Assyrian Church of the East.

Not every Christian community in these regions is Oriental Orthodox. Egypt and Syria also have Greek Orthodox (Chalcedonian, historically "", from the Syriac for "imperial") patriarchates, and Eastern Catholic Churches. The Maronite Church of Lebanon emerged from the monastic community of St Maron in the Chalcedonian milieu of northern Syria. Its history in the seventh century, including the question of Monothelitism, is disputed. It will be treated fully in Volume XIII.

Why it matters today

Copts, Armenians, Ethiopians, Syriacs and Malankara Christians form the Oriental Orthodox family, millions strong and often under pressure.
