Nicaea did not end the controversy. For a generation afterwards, its creed was rarely invoked. The struggle that followed was as much about people, councils and imperial favour as about doctrine.

Athanasius

Athanasius had attended Nicaea as Alexander's deacon and became bishop of Alexandria in 328. He was exiled five times: by Constantine in 335, after the Council of Tyre found against him on disciplinary charges; and again in 339, 356, 362 and 365. He spent about seventeen of his forty-five years as bishop away from his see.1 His theological case rested on salvation. Only one who is truly God can unite humanity to God:

For He was made man that we might be made God; and He manifested Himself by a body that we might receive the idea of the unseen Father.

Athanasius, On the Incarnation 54.3, c. 330s (date disputed). Trans. Archibald Robertson, NPNF II/4 (1892). Q08 · checked

This theme of , salvation as participation in divine life, remained central to Eastern Christian theology (Vol. IX ch. 6).

The theological parties

After Constantine's death (337) the empire was divided among his sons. Constantius II, sole ruler from 350 to 361, favoured a broad, non-Nicene settlement. The positions that emerged are conventionally grouped as follows. These labels are modern simplifications.

GroupFormulaMain figures
Niceneshomoousios, "of the same substance"Athanasius, Marcellus of Ancyra (whose own views were suspect to many), Western bishops
"Homoiousians"homoiousios, "of like substance"Basil of Ancyra, George of Laodicea
Homoianshomoios, "like [the Father]", with substance language bannedAcacius of Caesarea; the imperial creeds of 359–360
"Anomoeans" / Heterousiansanomoios, "unlike"Aetius, Eunomius

The Councils of Antioch (341) and Serdica (c. 343) showed the developing East–West divide. Western bishops, led by Julius of Rome, defended Athanasius and Marcellus. Many Eastern bishops saw Marcellus as a modalist who denied the Son's distinct existence, and saw Western support for him as proof that "Nicene" meant "Sabellian". At Serdica the two delegations met separately and excommunicated each other (Vol. VI, other councils).

Under Constantius, the twin councils of Ariminum (Rimini) and Seleucia (359), followed by Constantinople (360), imposed a creed on almost the whole episcopate. Jerome, looking back, wrote that the world "groaned and was astonished to find itself Arian".2 For a time, Homoian Christianity was the empire's official faith. Through the mission of Ulfila, it became the faith of the Goths and later of the Vandals, Burgundians and Lombards. That fact shaped Western politics until the conversion of the Visigothic king Reccared in 589 (Vol. VIII ch. 5).

In depth

Exiles, councils and creeds: the generation after Nicaea

Athanasius became bishop of Alexandria in 328 and held the see until his death in 373, though he spent about seventeen of those years in five exiles. The Council of Tyre deposed him in 335 and Constantine sent him to Trier. He was exiled again in 339, spent 356–362 hiding among the monks of Egypt, and was briefly driven out under Julian and again under Valens.

Nicaea had not ended the debate. Many eastern bishops disliked 'of one substance' (), fearing it blurred the distinction of Father and Son. Arius himself died in Constantinople in 336. The emperor Constantius II (337–361) sought a broad formula, and councils produced creed after creed.

By the 350s three main positions had emerged. Homoians said only that the Son was 'like' (homoios) the Father, avoiding the language of substance; this was the emperor's preferred settlement. Homoiousians, led by Basil of Ancyra, said the Son was 'like in substance' to the Father. A radical group, the Anomoeans or Heterousians around Aetius and Eunomius, called the Son 'unlike' the Father.

In 359 the twin councils of Rimini and Seleucia were pressed into accepting a homoian creed. Jerome later wrote that 'the whole world groaned and was astonished to find itself Arian'. Yet the pressure pushed homoiousians and Nicenes towards each other, preparing the settlement reached under Theodosius in 381.
