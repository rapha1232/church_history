The road to Nicaea and the Creed of 325

Starring

The council met at Nicaea in Bithynia (modern İznik), near the imperial residence at Nicomedia. It opened in early summer 325, traditionally 20 May, with Constantine presiding over at least the opening sessions. No minutes survive. What we know comes from the creed, twenty canons, a synodal letter to the Egyptian Church, letters of Constantine, and accounts by participants (Eusebius of Caesarea, Eustathius of Antioch, Athanasius) and by later historians.

How many bishops?

Eusebius, who was there, wrote that "the number of bishops exceeded two hundred and fifty".1 Athanasius later spoke of about 300. The figure of 318, which became canonical, appears from the 360s onwards (in Hilary of Poitiers and others) and was linked to the 318 servants of Abraham in Genesis 14:14. Socrates repeats it in the fifth century.2 Modern estimates usually fall between 250 and 300. Only a handful came from the Latin West: Ossius, two Roman presbyters representing Bishop Silvester, and a few others.

The Creed

We believe in one God, the Father Almighty, maker of all things visible and invisible; and in one Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of God, the only-begotten of his Father, of the substance of the Father, God of God, Light of Light, very God of very God, begotten, not made, being of one substance (ὁμοούσιον, consubstantialem) with the Father. By whom all things were made, both which be in heaven and in earth. Who for us men and for our salvation came down [from heaven] and was incarnate and was made man. He suffered and the third day he rose again, and ascended into heaven. And he shall come again to judge both the quick and the dead. And [we believe] in the Holy Ghost. And whosoever shall say that there was a time when the Son of God was not, or that before he was begotten he was not, or that he was made of things that were not, or that he is of a different substance or essence [from the Father] or that he is a creature, or subject to change or conversion — all that so say, the Catholic and Apostolic Church anathematizes them.

The Creed of Nicaea (325), as preserved in the acts of Ephesus and Chalcedon and in Eusebius, Athanasius, Socrates and Theodoret. Trans. H. R. Percival, NPNF II/14 (1900). Checked. Q05 · checked

Three features deserve attention:

  • It is not the creed most Christians recite today. The "Nicene Creed" used in liturgy is the longer text associated with Constantinople (381) (ch. 9). The 325 creed ends abruptly with "and in the Holy Spirit", followed by anathemas.
  • "Of the substance (ousia) of the Father" and "of one substance" (homoousios) were the decisive additions. was not a biblical word, had been associated with Gnostic and modalist theologies, and had been rejected at Antioch in 268 in the case of Paul of Samosata. Its adoption was controversial from the start. Eusebius of Caesarea's letter to his church shows him accepting it only after it was explained in a sense he could live with.3
  • The anathemas equate and ("of a different substance or essence"; Greek ex heteras hypostaseōs ē ousias). This was a source of later confusion. When the Cappadocians later distinguished the two terms (ch. 8), they had to explain away this wording.

Only two bishops, Theonas of Marmarica and Secundus of Ptolemais, refused to sign and were exiled with Arius. Eusebius of Nicomedia and Theognis of Nicaea signed the creed but not the condemnation of Arius, and were exiled shortly afterwards. Accounts of this differ. Socrates names five initial objectors.4

Other business

The council also:

  • decided to celebrate Easter on a common Sunday independent of the Jewish calendar. The detailed method of calculation was not fixed by the council, and the Alexandrian and Roman reckonings continued to differ for centuries.
  • settled the Melitian schism in Egypt.
  • issued twenty canons. Canon 6, recognising the authority of Alexandria, Rome and Antioch over their regions, became the founding text for later arguments about church authority (Vol. VII ch. 2).

It did not discuss or decide the canon of Scripture. That widespread modern claim has no basis in any ancient source (Misconceptions).

Why it matters today

The Nicene Creed is still recited by Catholics, Orthodox and most Protestants; the council’s 1,700th anniversary was marked in 2025.
