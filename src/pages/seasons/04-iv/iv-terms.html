Starring

Much of the history in Volumes IV–VI turns on a handful of Greek words whose meanings were still being fixed during the controversies themselves. The same word could mean different things to different writers, and translation into Latin and Syriac added further shifts. The table summarises how each word was eventually used. The notes after it explain the most important ambiguities.

Notes on the ambiguities

  • Ousia vs hypostasis. In 325 these were synonyms. Nicaea's condemns those who say the Son is "of another or ". The Cappadocians separated them in the 360s–370s (ch. 8).
  • Physis in Cyril. Cyril of Alexandria often used physis almost as a synonym for hypostasis, meaning a concrete, individual reality. His "one incarnate nature of God the Word" therefore meant "one concrete subject, the Word, now incarnate". It did not mean that the humanity had been absorbed. Chalcedon used physis in the more abstract sense of "nature as a set of properties". For this reason, modern dialogues have concluded that the Oriental Orthodox "one nature" and the "two natures" can express the same faith (Vol. V ch. 2).
  • Qnōmā. The Church of the East confesses in Christ "two natures, two qnōmē, one parṣōpā". This sounds like "two hypostases", which Chalcedon rejected. Syriac scholars such as Sebastian Brock have argued that qnōmā here denotes the individuated nature (a particular instance of a nature), not an independent subject. If so, the formula does not mean "two persons".1
  • Theotokos is a statement about Christ before it is a statement about Mary. The controversy of 428–431 was about whether the one born of Mary is identical with the eternal Word.

Why it matters today

Many modern disagreements turn out to be about words; these terms explain why churches with the same faith once condemned each other.
