The Donatist controversy

Starring

The first church dispute that Constantine was asked to settle was not about the Trinity but about holiness and authority. In 311 or 312, Caecilian was consecrated bishop of Carthage. His opponents claimed that one of his consecrators, Felix of Abthugni, had been a traditor who surrendered scriptures in the persecution. They argued that sacraments administered by such men were invalid, and that a Church tolerating them had ceased to be the true Church. A council of Numidian bishops elected a rival bishop, Maiorinus. He was soon succeeded by Donatus, whose name the movement took.1

Both sides appealed to the emperor. Constantine referred the case to a under Bishop Miltiades of Rome (October 313), then to a larger council at Arles (1 August 314). Both found for Caecilian. Felix was later cleared by a civil inquiry. Arles also ruled that baptism given in the name of the Trinity was not to be repeated, even if given by heretics. This marked a divergence from the earlier African practice associated with Cyprian. After further appeals Constantine tried coercion (317–321), failed, and settled for toleration.

The schism lasted more than a century. For much of the fourth century Donatists were probably the majority Church in Numidia. Augustine of Hippo became their most important opponent, developing a theology in which the Church is a "mixed body" of saints and sinners. In this theology the validity of a sacrament depends on Christ, not on the holiness of the minister. After an imperial conference at Carthage in 411 the Donatists were outlawed, and Augustine came, with misgivings, to justify state coercion against them. Recent scholarship stresses the violence on both sides and the deep regional roots of the dispute.2

Why it matters

  • It set the pattern of rival Christian parties appealing to the emperor, and of the emperor acting through councils.
  • Augustine's answers on the Church and the sacraments became foundational in Western theology, and were invoked again at the Reformation.
  • It showed that "schism" (a break over order and communion) could be as divisive as "heresy" (a dispute over doctrine). See the glossary entries and .

In depth

How the Donatist dispute shaped Western theology

The dispute began in Carthage around 311–312 when Caecilian was consecrated bishop. His opponents claimed that one of his consecrators, Felix of Abthugni, had been a traditor, someone who handed over the Scriptures during the persecution, and that the consecration was therefore invalid. They elected a rival, Majorinus, soon followed by Donatus, who gave the movement its name.

The dispute came to Constantine, who referred it to a synod at Rome (313) and then a larger council at Arles (314). Both decided for Caecilian. An inquiry in 315 cleared Felix. Constantine briefly tried coercion (317–321), then gave up. The Donatists became the majority church in much of Numidia for a century, with their own bishops in most towns. Bands of rural militants called circumcellions were associated with them, though their nature is debated.

Behind the quarrel lay a real theological question inherited from Cyprian: does the validity of a sacrament depend on the holiness of the minister and the purity of the Church? The Donatists said yes and rebaptized converts. Augustine, bishop of Hippo from 395, answered that Christ is the true minister of the sacraments, so a baptism given by an unworthy priest, or even outside the Church, is real, though it does not bear fruit until the person returns to unity. This is the root of the later Western teaching that sacraments work ex opere operato, 'by the work performed'.

After a conference at Carthage in 411 the imperial government outlawed Donatism. Augustine came to justify state coercion with the text 'compel them to come in' (Luke 14:23), an argument later used to support persecution of heretics.

Why it matters today

The Donatist dispute shaped teaching that a sacrament’s validity does not depend on the minister’s holiness, and it still informs responses to scandal.
