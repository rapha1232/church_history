Constantine’s conversion

Starring

Constantine was acclaimed emperor by his father's army at York in 306. In 312 he invaded Italy against Maxentius, who held Rome, and defeated him at the Milvian Bridge on 28 October.1 Two Christian authors, both close to the events, describe a divine sign before the battle. Their accounts differ in ways that matter.

Constantine was directed in a dream to cause the heavenly sign to be delineated on the shields of his soldiers, and so to proceed to battle. He did as he had been commanded, and he marked on their shields the letter X, with a perpendicular line drawn through it and turned round thus at the top, being the cipher of Christ.

Lactantius, On the Deaths of the Persecutors 44.5, written c. 314–315. Trans. William Fletcher, ANF 7 (1886). Q01 · checked

He said that about noon, when the day was already beginning to decline, he saw with his own eyes the trophy of a cross of light in the heavens, above the sun, and bearing the inscription, Conquer by this.

Eusebius, Life of Constantine 1.28, written after Constantine’s death in 337. Trans. E. C. Richardson, NPNF II/1 (1890). Q02 · checked

Lactantius, writing within about three years, describes a dream on the eve of battle and a monogram painted on shields. Eusebius, writing a quarter-century later, describes a daytime vision seen by the whole army, followed by a dream and the making of the labarum standard. He says Constantine told him this himself "long after" and confirmed it with oaths. Notably, Eusebius did not mention the vision in his Ecclesiastical History, which he revised in the 310s and 320s.2

Scholarly debate: what happened in 312?

Historians broadly agree that by late 312 Constantine publicly identified his victory with the Christian God. They disagree about the nature and timing of his personal religious change.

  • Early, sincere conversion. Timothy Barnes argues that Constantine was a committed Christian from 312 and pursued an increasingly Christian policy, including restrictions on sacrifice.3
  • Gradual or syncretic development. Others stress continued solar imagery (Sol Invictus on coins until the early 320s) and the ambiguous wording of the Arch of Constantine (315), which credits victory to "the prompting of the divinity" (instinctu divinitatis). They see a monotheist who moved towards Christianity over time.4
  • Policy of consensus. H. A. Drake reads Constantine's policy as aiming at a broad, inclusive monotheism that left room for non-Christians, with harsher measures mostly coming later.5
  • Natural phenomena. Some have proposed that a solar halo explains the vision Eusebius describes. This is a speculative attempt to explain a later account, not established fact.

Constantine was baptised only on his deathbed in May 337, by Eusebius of Nicomedia. Deferring baptism was common at the time and does not by itself show insincerity.

Imperial patronage

Constantine's practical support for the Church is well documented:

  • restoration of confiscated property;
  • exemption of clergy from burdensome civic duties (from 313);
  • the right of bishops to arbitrate civil cases (episcopalis audientia);
  • Sunday as a legal day of rest (321);
  • lavish building programmes: the Lateran basilica in Rome, St Peter's on the Vatican hill, and the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem (dedicated 335).6

Above all, Constantine treated church unity as a concern of the state. When Christians disputed, he summoned bishops to councils, paid their travel through the public post, and enforced their decisions with exile. This model of the emperor as convener and enforcer, though not formally the judge of doctrine, shaped every council in this volume.

The idea that Constantine "made Christianity the state religion" or "decided the content of the Bible" is a modern misconception. See Common misconceptions. Christianity became the official religion only under Theodosius I, half a century later (ch. 9).
