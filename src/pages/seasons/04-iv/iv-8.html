The Cappadocians and the language of the Trinity

Starring

The resolution of the fourth-century debates owed most to three theologians from Cappadocia in central Anatolia: Basil of Caesarea (d. 378 or 379), his brother Gregory of Nyssa (d. c. 395), and their friend Gregory of Nazianzus (d. c. 390). Their writings are extensive and survive well.

The Council of Alexandria (362) and the problem of words

After Julian's accession (361), exiled bishops returned. Athanasius held a at Alexandria in 362 whose Tome to the Antiochenes made a crucial concession. It accepted that those who spoke of three could be orthodox, provided they did not mean three different substances, and that those who spoke of one hypostasis could be orthodox, provided they did not mean to deny the distinction of persons.1 The dispute had partly been about vocabulary. In Greek, ousia and hypostasis could both mean "reality" or "substance". In Latin, substantia was the natural translation of hypostasis, so Latins heard "three hypostases" as "three substances", that is, three gods.

The Cappadocian distinction

Basil and the Gregories established the formula that became standard in the East:

one ousia (essence, substance) · three hypostaseis (concrete subsistences, "persons")

The Father, Son and Spirit share one divine nature completely. They are distinguished not by degree of divinity but by their relations of origin: the Father is unbegotten, the Son begotten, the Spirit proceeding. For a detailed comparison of terms, see Key Greek terms. Latin theology came to express the same structure as una substantia, tres personae. The overlap and mismatch between hypostasis and persona (originally a "role" or "face") remained a source of misunderstanding.

The Holy Spirit

By the 360s a new question had emerged. Some who accepted the Son's full divinity denied it to the Holy Spirit; opponents called them Pneumatomachoi, "Spirit-fighters", later identified with "Macedonians". Basil's On the Holy Spirit (c. 375) argued from the Church's worship, specifically the doxology giving glory to the Father "with the Son together with the Holy Spirit", that the Spirit shares the same honour. Characteristically, he avoided calling the Spirit "God" or homoousios outright. Gregory of Nazianzus, in his Fifth Theological Oration (380), did both.2 Basil's treatise also contains an image argument that the iconophiles of the eighth century would later quote:

…because the honour paid to the image passes on to the prototype.

Basil, On the Holy Spirit 18.45. Trans. Blomfield Jackson, NPNF II/8 (1895). In context Basil refers to the emperor’s image and, by analogy, to the Son as image of the Father; he is not discussing religious pictures. Q10 · checked

Apollinarius and the humanity of Christ

Apollinarius of Laodicea, a staunch Nicene, taught that in Christ the divine Word took the place of the human mind (nous). Gregory of Nazianzus' reply gave the classic soteriological principle that would govern all later Christology:

For that which He has not assumed He has not healed; but that which is united to His Godhead is also saved.

Gregory of Nazianzus, Letter 101 (to Cledonius), c. 382. Trans. C. G. Browne and J. E. Swallow, NPNF II/7 (1894). Q09 · checked

Apollinarianism was condemned in Rome and at Constantinople in 381. The question it raised, how to affirm one subject in Christ without diminishing either his divinity or his humanity, opened the controversies of the next century (ch. 11).

In depth

Three Cappadocians and the words for God

Basil of Caesarea (c. 330–379), his younger brother Gregory of Nyssa (c. 335–395) and their friend Gregory of Nazianzus (c. 329–390) came from Christian families in Cappadocia, in central Asia Minor. Their sister Macrina, head of a household community of ascetics, shaped Basil's and Gregory's spiritual lives. Gregory of Nyssa's Life of Macrina records her influence.

Their key contribution was to distinguish two words that Nicaea's anathemas had treated as synonyms: (essence or substance) and hypostasis (concrete subsistence). God is one ousia in three hypostases. The Father, Son and Spirit share one divine nature and act inseparably, yet are distinguished by their relations: the Father is unbegotten, the Son begotten, the Spirit proceeding.

Basil's On the Holy Spirit (375) defended giving glory to the Spirit 'with' the Father and the Son. Gregory of Nazianzus's five Theological Orations, preached in Constantinople in 380, set out the doctrine so memorably that the Eastern Church calls him 'the Theologian'. The Council of Constantinople in 381, over which he briefly presided, confirmed the Nicene faith and enlarged the creed's article on the Spirit.

Against Apollinarius, who denied that Christ had a human mind, Gregory of Nazianzus wrote the principle that would guide later debates: 'What has not been assumed has not been healed' (Letter 101).
