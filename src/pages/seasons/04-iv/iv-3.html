The so-called Edict of Milan (313)

Starring

In February 313 Constantine and Licinius, who ruled the Balkans and would soon take the East, met at Milan. Licinius married Constantine's half-sister, and the two agreed a common religious policy. What is called the "Edict of Milan" was not an edict and was not issued at Milan. The surviving text is a letter of instruction to provincial governors that Licinius published in the East after defeating Maximinus Daia. Lactantius gives the Latin version as posted at Nicomedia in June 313; Eusebius gives a Greek version.1

[We resolved] that the Christians and all others should have liberty to follow that mode of religion which to each of them appeared best.

Lactantius, On the Deaths of the Persecutors 48.2. Trans. William Fletcher, ANF 7 (1886). Opening words supplied in brackets. Q03 · checked

The document is notable for its general principle of religious freedom, which extends explicitly to "all others", and for ordering that confiscated church property be restored to the Christian communities at state expense where necessary. It did not establish Christianity, end pagan worship or privilege the Church over other cults. Those developments came gradually over the next eighty years.

Key takeaways

  • Not an edict, not from Milan: a Licinian letter of 313 recording a policy agreed at Milan.2
  • It granted toleration to all religions and restitution to Christians. It did not make Christianity the state religion.

In depth

What the 313 agreement did and did not do

The name 'Edict of Milan' is misleading in three ways. It was not issued at Milan: Constantine and Licinius met there in February 313, and what survives is the letter Licinius posted at Nicomedia on 13 June 313 after defeating Maximinus Daia. It was not strictly an edict but a letter of instruction to provincial governors. And it was not the first toleration: Galerius had already ended the persecution in April 311 (IV.1).

What was new was the scope. Galerius' edict tolerated Christians grudgingly, asking them to pray for the emperor. The 313 letter grants 'to the Christians and to all others' the free choice of religion, says that no religion should lose any of its honour, and orders the return of confiscated Christian property, with compensation from the treasury for buyers. Two versions survive, in Latin in Lactantius and in Greek in Eusebius; the differences are small.

The letter did not make Christianity the state religion. That came only with Theodosius in 380 (IV.9). But it marked a turning point: within a few years Constantine was granting clergy exemptions from civic duties, funding churches, and summoning bishops to resolve the Donatist dispute. The language of religious freedom in the letter would be quoted again in very different circumstances in the modern period.
