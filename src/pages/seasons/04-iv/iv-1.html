The Great Persecution and its end (303–311)

Starring

By 300, Christians were a substantial minority in the Roman Empire, more numerous in the Greek-speaking East and North Africa than in the Latin West. Precise figures are impossible. Estimates that are often cited (around 10 per cent of the population, perhaps five to six million people) come from modelling rather than counts, and should be treated as orders of magnitude.1

Persecution before 303 had been intermittent and mostly local. The empire-wide measures of Decius (250) and Valerian (257–260) were the main exceptions. After Valerian's capture by the Persians, his son Gallienus restored confiscated property, and the Church enjoyed some forty years of de facto toleration. In this period churches were built openly, Christians served at court and in the army, and bishops held property recognised by the state.2

The four edicts

Beginning in February 303, the tetrarchic government of Diocletian and his co-emperors issued a series of measures:

  1. February 303: churches were to be destroyed, scriptures surrendered and burned, and Christians of rank stripped of privileges. The church at Nicomedia, visible from the palace, was demolished on 23 February.
  2. Later in 303: clergy were to be imprisoned.
  3. Late 303: imprisoned clergy were to be released if they sacrificed, and compelled to if they refused.
  4. Early 304: a general order for all inhabitants to sacrifice, attested mainly for the East.

Our main narrative sources are Lactantius, a Christian rhetorician at Nicomedia, and Eusebius of Caesarea, who witnessed the persecution in Palestine. Both wrote as partisans, after the persecutors had fallen.3 Enforcement was uneven. In the West, where Constantius (Constantine's father) governed Gaul and Britain, it seems to have gone little further than destroying some buildings. In Egypt, Palestine and the eastern provinces under Galerius and later Maximinus Daia, it was severe and prolonged.

The persecution left lasting divisions inside the Church as well as martyrs. Christians who had handed over scriptures (traditores) or sacrificed (lapsi) had to be dealt with when it ended. In North Africa this question produced the Donatist schism, and in Egypt the Melitian schism, which the Council of Nicaea later addressed.

Galerius’ edict of toleration (311)

The persecution was ended not by Constantine but by its chief architect. Mortally ill, Galerius issued an edict in April 311, in his own name and those of his co-emperors. It conceded that the attempt to bring Christians back to ancestral religion had failed. It permitted them to "exist again" and rebuild their meeting places, provided they did nothing contrary to public order, and asked them to pray for the emperors' safety.4 Galerius died days later. In the East, Maximinus Daia resumed pressure on Christians until his defeat by Licinius in 313.

Key takeaways

  • Legal toleration of Christianity (311) came before Constantine's patronage and was not his invention.
  • How severe the "Great Persecution" was depended greatly on region and ruler.
  • Its aftermath (what to do with those who had lapsed) shaped church divisions for a century.

In depth

The Great Persecution: edicts, enforcement and survivors

On 23 February 303 Diocletian's soldiers destroyed the new church at Nicomedia, and the next day the first edict was posted. It ordered churches destroyed, scriptures surrendered and burned, and Christians stripped of rank and legal privileges. Later edicts ordered the arrest of clergy, released them if they sacrificed, and in 304 required everyone to sacrifice to the gods.

Enforcement varied greatly. In the West, Constantius I (Constantine's father) did little more than demolish some buildings. In the East, under Galerius and later Maximinus Daia, there were executions, mutilations and condemnations to the mines. Eusebius, an eyewitness in Palestine, recorded the Martyrs of Palestine; Lactantius, who had taught rhetoric at Nicomedia, wrote On the Deaths of the Persecutors.

The persecution left lasting divisions. Clergy who had handed over scriptures were called traditores ('those who handed over'). In North Africa, disputes over whether bishops ordained by such men were valid gave rise to the Donatist schism, which divided the African church for more than a century.

In April 311 the dying Galerius issued an edict of toleration: Christians might 'exist again and hold their meetings', provided they prayed for the emperor. Maximinus resumed persecution in the East until his defeat by Licinius in 313. The agreement of Constantine and Licinius at Milan that year confirmed toleration for all.

Why it matters today

Diocletian’s persecution still frames the memory of the martyrs and the ideal of a Church that does not depend on state power.
