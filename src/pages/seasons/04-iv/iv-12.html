From Eutyches to Chalcedon (448–451)

Eutyches

Eutyches was an elderly archimandrite in Constantinople, a passionate Cyrillian with influence at court. In November 448 he was accused before the "home " of Bishop Flavian of Constantinople. When questioned, he confessed that Christ was "of two natures before the union, but after the union one nature". He was reluctant to say that Christ was consubstantial with us in his humanity. The synod deposed him. His exact views remain debated. He may have been more confused than heretical. But "Eutychianism", the belief that Christ's humanity was absorbed into or mixed with his divinity, became the name for a position that all later parties, including the opponents of Chalcedon, condemned.1

Leo's Tome and Ephesus II (449)

Eutyches appealed to Pope Leo I and to Dioscorus, Cyril's successor at Alexandria. Leo replied with a dogmatic letter to Flavian dated 13 June 449, the "Tome". It set out Western Christology in balanced antitheses:

For each form does what is proper to it with the co-operation of the other; that is the Word performing what appertains to the Word, and the flesh carrying out what appertains to the flesh.

Leo I, Letter 28 (the Tome) 4, dated 13 June 449. Trans. C. L. Feltoe, NPNF II/12 (1895). Q18 · checked

Theodosius II called a council to Ephesus in August 449, presided over by Dioscorus. The Tome was not read. Eutyches was rehabilitated. Flavian and other leading "two-nature" bishops, including Domnus of Antioch, Ibas of Edessa and Theodoret, were deposed. Flavian died soon after. Later accounts say he died from beatings at the council; the evidence is partisan and the details are disputed. Leo, whose legate Hilarus escaped, denounced the assembly. In a letter to the empress Pulcheria he called it a latrocinium, a "robber council", and the name has stuck.2

Chalcedon (451)

On 28 July 450 Theodosius II died after a fall from his horse. His sister Pulcheria married the officer Marcian, who became emperor and reversed his predecessor's church policy. A new council was summoned. It met at Chalcedon, across the Bosporus from the capital, from 8 October to around 1 November 451. About 350–370 bishops took part, almost all from the East. Later tradition gives 630. Roman legates (Paschasinus, Lucentius and the priest Boniface) took a leading place, and imperial officials directed proceedings.3

  • Session 1. The council reviewed Ephesus II and condemned its proceedings.
  • Session 3. Dioscorus was deposed, formally for disciplinary reasons (refusing to appear when summoned), not for heresy.
  • Leo's Tome was read and acclaimed. Bishops from Illyricum and Palestine were concerned about some passages, and the commissioners had those passages checked against Cyril's writings before the Tome was accepted.

This is the faith of the fathers, this is the faith of the Apostles. So we all believe, thus the orthodox believe. to him who does not thus believe. Peter has spoken thus through Leo. So taught the Apostles. Piously and truly did Leo teach, so taught Cyril.

Acclamations after the reading of the Tome, Chalcedon, Session 2 (in Percival’s numbering; Session 3 in some editions). Trans. Percival, NPNF II/14 (1900). Q19 · checked

The acclamation "Peter has spoken through Leo" is cited by Catholic writers as evidence of recognised papal teaching authority. Orthodox and many other scholars point to its immediate qualification, "so taught Cyril", and to the council's own examination of the Tome as showing that the bishops judged Leo's letter by its agreement with tradition rather than accepting it on authority. Both readings appeal to the same text.

The emperor insisted, against the wishes of many bishops, on a new definition of faith. It was agreed at Session 5 on 22 October and proclaimed in Marcian's presence on 25 October. Its central clause:

Following the holy Fathers we teach with one voice that the Son [of God] and our Lord Jesus Christ is to be confessed as one and the same [Person], that he is perfect in Godhead and perfect in manhood, very God and very man, of a reasonable soul and [human] body consisting, consubstantial with the Father as touching his Godhead, and consubstantial with us as touching his manhood; made in all things like unto us, sin only excepted; begotten of his Father before the worlds according to his Godhead; but in these last days for us men and for our salvation born [into the world] of the Virgin Mary, the Mother of God according to his manhood. This one and the same Jesus Christ, the only-begotten Son [of God] must be confessed to be in two natures, unconfusedly, immutably, indivisibly, inseparably [united], and that without the distinction of natures being taken away by such union, but rather the peculiar property of each nature being preserved and being united in one Person and subsistence, not separated or divided into two persons, but one and the same Son and only-begotten, God the Word, our Lord Jesus Christ…

Chalcedon, Definition of Faith (22–25 October 451). Trans. Percival, NPNF II/14 (1900). Checked. Q20 · checked

The phrase "in two natures" (en dyo physesin) is the reading of the Latin version and of the version known to later Greek writers. Some argued that the original draft had read "out of two natures" (ek dyo physeōn), a formula Dioscorus could have accepted, and that it was changed at the insistence of the Roman legates and the emperor. The four adverbs, often called the "Chalcedonian fences", exclude both confusion of the natures ("unconfusedly, immutably", against Eutyches) and division ("indivisibly, inseparably", against Nestorius).

The Chalcedonians regarded this as a synthesis of Cyril and Leo. Its opponents saw it as a betrayal of Cyril and a return to Nestorius under a new formula. The consequences, including the separation of the Oriental Orthodox Churches, are the subject of Volume V ch. 2. The council's canons, especially the disputed canon 28 on Constantinople, are treated in Volume VI and Volume VII.

Why it matters today

The Chalcedonian definition is still the dividing line between Eastern and Oriental Orthodox, now largely reconciled in theological agreements.
