Theodosius, 380–381, and the Nicene settlement

Cunctos populos (380)

On 27 or 28 February 380, at Thessalonica, Theodosius, with his co-emperors Gratian and Valentinian II, issued the law later placed at the head of the religious book of the Theodosian Code. It commanded all peoples under imperial rule to follow the religion that "the divine apostle Peter transmitted to the Romans". It identified that faith by naming two living bishops, Damasus of Rome and Peter of Alexandria, as its exemplars, and declared that only those who held it might be called "Catholic Christians"; others were "demented and insane" heretics, liable to divine and imperial punishment.1

This law, not anything done by Constantine, is the best candidate for the moment Nicene Christianity became the official religion of the Roman state. Later laws excluded non-Nicenes from church buildings (381) and prohibited public pagan sacrifice and temple cult (391–392).2 Enforcement was uneven. Pagan cults, Jewish communities and dissident Christian groups persisted, but the legal framework of a Christian empire was in place.

The Council of Constantinople (381)

Theodosius summoned about 150 Eastern bishops to his capital in May 381. No Western bishop attended, and Rome was not represented. The council was presided over first by Meletius of Antioch, who died during it, then by Gregory of Nazianzus. Gregory resigned amid disputes over the Antiochene succession and over his own appointment as bishop of Constantinople. Nectarius, an unbaptised senator, was elected in his place.

The council reaffirmed Nicaea and condemned the Homoians, Anomoeans, and Apollinarians. It is associated with an expanded creed, the "Niceno-Constantinopolitan" Creed, whose article on the Spirit reads:

And we believe in the Holy Ghost, the Lord and Giver-of-Life, who proceeds from the Father, who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified, who spoke by the prophets.

Scholarly debate: did the council of 381 compose this creed?

The text is first attested in the acts of Chalcedon (451), where it was read as the creed "of the 150 fathers". No document from 381 itself preserves it. J. N. D. Kelly reviewed the theories. One is that the council endorsed an existing local creed, perhaps from Jerusalem or Constantinople; another is that the creed formed part of the council's lost "tome". He concluded that the council probably did adopt it, in connection with the baptism and installation of Nectarius.

What is certain is that from 451 this creed became the common creed of the Greek and Latin Churches. With the later Western addition of the , it is the creed recited in most churches today. The Oriental Orthodox Churches also use it. The Church of the East has its own historic creed, but it too recognises the councils of 325 and 381.3

The council's canon 3 had long-term consequences for church politics:

The bishop of Constantinople however shall have the prerogative of honour next after the bishop of Rome, because Constantinople is new Rome.

Constantinople I, canon 3 (381), in the form read into the record at Chalcedon, Session 16. Trans. Percival, NPNF II/14 (1900). Socrates (HE 5.8) paraphrases it in the same sense. Q12 · checked

Rome did not accept the canons of 381 for centuries. Its objection was to the principle that a see's rank depended on its city's political status rather than its apostolic foundation. See Vol. VII ch. 3.

Why it matters today

The 381 creed is the one used in the liturgy, and the Filioque later added to it still divides East and West.
