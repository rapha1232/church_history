Arius and the outbreak of the controversy

c. 318–3253 minPreviously: The Donatist controversy

Arius was a respected presbyter in charge of the Baucalis church in Alexandria.

Starring

Arius was a respected presbyter in charge of the Baucalis church in Alexandria. Around 318–321 (the exact date is disputed) he came into conflict with his bishop, Alexander, over how to describe the relation of the Son (the divine Word, Logos) to God the Father.1

Arius' own words survive only in a few letters and in fragments of his poem Thalia quoted by hostile writers. His letter to Eusebius, bishop of Nicomedia, his most powerful supporter, preserves his position in his own voice:

We are persecuted, because we say that the Son has a beginning, but that God is without beginning. This is the cause of our persecution, and likewise, because we say that He is of the non-existent.

Arius, Letter to Eusebius of Nicomedia, preserved in Theodoret, Ecclesiastical History 1.4. Trans. Blomfield Jackson, NPNF II/3 (1892). Q04 · checked

Arius' concern was to protect the uniqueness and indivisibility of God the Father. For him the Son was the first and highest of God's works, the agent of creation, and "perfect God" in a derived sense. But he was brought into being by the Father's will. There was a time "when he was not", and he was not eternal in the way the Father is. Alexander, and later Athanasius, held that this made the Son a creature. A creature, they argued, could not reveal God or save humanity. If the Father is eternally Father, he must eternally have a Son.

Scholarly debate: what was “Arianism”?

Since the 1970s, scholarship has substantially revised the traditional picture.

  • Not a single system. R. P. C. Hanson and others showed that "Arianism" as a coherent party is largely a construction of its opponents, especially Athanasius. Arius was not the founder of a school, and most later "Arians" did not regard him as their teacher.2
  • A widespread theology. Rowan Williams situates Arius within Alexandrian philosophical theology. He argues that many bishops shared a broadly "subordinationist" view in which the Son is divine but ranked below the Father. To them Arius' clear-cut statements appeared not shocking but over-precise.3
  • A dispute about method. Lewis Ayres describes the century after 318 as a search for adequate terms. In that search, positions were refined and sometimes reversed, and the eventual "pro-Nicene" consensus of the 360s–380s was not simply a return to 325.4

Hence modern specialists often avoid "Arian" as an umbrella term. Where they use it, it is shorthand for a range of non-Nicene theologies. This edition follows that practice.

Alexander condemned Arius at an Egyptian . Arius found support from Eusebius of Nicomedia and from Eusebius of Caesarea, among others, and the dispute spread through the eastern episcopate. When Constantine defeated Licinius and took control of the East in 324, he found the Church there divided. He first sent his adviser Ossius (Hosius) of Córdoba with a letter treating the question as a trivial dispute over words. Ossius found otherwise. A synod at Antioch in early 325 condemned Arius' position and provisionally excommunicated Eusebius of Caesarea. The emperor then summoned a general council.5
