Two traditions

Once the full divinity of the Son was settled, the question became how God and a full human being are united in the one Christ. Two broad traditions had developed. Modern textbooks call them "Alexandrian" and "Antiochene", labels that oversimplify real diversity within each.1

The Theotokos dispute

Nestorius, a monk-preacher from Antioch, became bishop of Constantinople in April 428. Socrates, a lay historian in the capital who was not hostile to him, records how the controversy began:

This Anastasius preaching one day in the church said, ‘Let no one call Mary Theotocos: for Mary was but a woman; and it is impossible that God should be born of a woman.’ These words created a great sensation…

Socrates Scholasticus, Ecclesiastical History 7.32. Trans. A. C. Zenos, NPNF II/2 (1890). Q13 · checked

Nestorius backed his chaplain. He proposed Christotokos ("bearer of Christ") as more accurate, or accepted only if paired with anthropotokos ("bearer of a human being"). For his opponents, this implied that the one born of Mary was not God the Word himself but a man joined to him: in effect, two sons.

Cyril's campaign

Cyril of Alexandria (bishop 412–444) responded in letters to Egyptian monks, to Nestorius and to the imperial court, and secured the support of Celestine of Rome. A Roman in August 430 condemned Nestorius. Cyril's Third Letter to Nestorius (November 430) attached twelve anathemas for Nestorius to sign:

If anyone will not confess that the Emmanuel is very God, and that therefore the Holy Virgin is the Mother of God (Theotokos), inasmuch as in the flesh she bore the Word of God made flesh [as it is written, “The Word was made flesh”]: let him be .

Cyril of Alexandria, Twelve Anathemas, no. 1, appended to his Third Letter to Nestorius (430). Trans. Percival, NPNF II/14 (1900). Q14 · checked

The anathemas, with their language of "natural" or "hypostatic union" and the claim that "the Word of God suffered in the flesh", alarmed Antiochene theologians, including John of Antioch and Theodoret. To them, they seemed Apollinarian.2

Ephesus, 431

Theodosius II summoned a council to Ephesus for Pentecost 431. What followed was less a single council than rival assemblies:

  • 22 June. Cyril opened the council before John of Antioch and the Syrian bishops arrived, despite the protests of the imperial commissioner Candidian and of some 68 bishops. Nestorius refused to attend. In one day the assembly examined his teaching and deposed him.

    …we have come, with many tears, to this sorrowful sentence against him, namely, that our Lord Jesus Christ, whom he has blasphemed, decrees by the holy Synod that Nestorius be excluded from the episcopal dignity, and from all priestly communion.

    Council of Ephesus, Decree against Nestorius (22 June 431). Trans. Percival, NPNF II/14. Q15 · checked
  • 26 June. John of Antioch arrived and held a counter-synod of some 43 bishops that deposed Cyril and Memnon of Ephesus.
  • July. The Roman legates arrived and endorsed Cyril's council.
  • Afterwards. The emperor at first confirmed both sets of depositions and arrested Cyril, Memnon and Nestorius. Eventually he accepted Cyril's council. Contemporary sources suggest Cyril's agents were active at court and gave generous "blessings" (gifts). Nestorius was sent back to his monastery near Antioch and later exiled to Egypt, where he died after 450.3

Canon 7 of the council (passed at a later session) forbade composing "a different faith" from that of Nicaea. It would be cited against every subsequent attempt at a new definition, including Chalcedon's (Vol. VI, Ephesus).

The Formula of Reunion (433)

Under imperial pressure, Cyril and John were reconciled in 433. John accepted the deposition of Nestorius. Cyril accepted a confession of faith drafted by the Antiochenes, probably by Theodoret, which spoke of two natures:

…of the same substance with his Father according to his Divinity, and of the same substance with us according to his humanity; for there became a union of two natures. Wherefore we confess one Christ, one Son, one Lord. According to this understanding of this unmixed union, we confess the holy Virgin to be Mother of God…

The Formula of Reunion (433), quoted in Cyril’s letter to John of Antioch (Laetentur caeli). Trans. Percival, NPNF II/14. Q17 · checked

Many of Cyril's own supporters thought he had conceded too much. His followers' reading of his work, and especially his formula "one incarnate nature of God the Word" (mia physis tou theou logou sesarkōmenē), which he believed came from Athanasius but actually derived from Apollinarius, would become the rallying point after Chalcedon (Vol. V ch. 2).4

Why it matters today

Calling Mary “Theotokos” is still common to Catholics and Orthodox; the debate shaped the separation of the Church of the East.
