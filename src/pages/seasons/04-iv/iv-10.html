The rise of monasticism

Starring

As Christianity became the religion of the powerful, a movement of ascetic withdrawal grew up at its edges. It became one of its defining institutions. Monasticism had antecedents: celibate "sons and daughters of the covenant" (bnay qyāmā) in Syriac Christianity, ascetic virgins and widows in cities, and the Jewish Therapeutae described by Philo. But the fourth century saw it take recognisable forms.

  • Anchoritic (solitary) life. The Life of Antony, attributed to Athanasius and written c. 357–362, presented Antony of Egypt (c. 251–356) as the model hermit. Its Latin translations spread the ideal to the West and helped prompt Augustine's conversion (Confessions 8.6). How much the Life reflects Athanasius' own agenda, for example by showing Antony as a loyal ally of the bishop, is debated.1
  • Coenobitic (communal) life. Pachomius (c. 292–348) founded communal monasteries in Upper Egypt from the 320s, with a written rule, common work and obedience to a superior. By his death they formed a federation of several thousand monks and nuns.2
  • Semi-anchoritic settlements at Nitria, Kellia and Scetis (Wadi al-Natrun), whose sayings were collected in the Apophthegmata Patrum.
  • Basil of Caesarea integrated ascetic communities with the life of the local Church, including care for the poor and sick, through his Asketikon. It remains the basis of Byzantine-rite monasticism.
  • Syrian asceticism developed spectacular forms, such as Symeon the Stylite (d. 459) on his pillar near Antioch, and strong links with Syriac poetry and theology (Ephrem, d. 373).
  • In the West, Martin of Tours, John Cassian (Marseille, c. 415), Lérins and later Benedict of Nursia (6th c.) adapted Eastern models.

Monks became a force in church politics. Egyptian monks under Shenoute and others were active against pagan temples. The archimandrite Eutyches in Constantinople, the Syrian monk Barsauma at Ephesus in 449, and Palestinian monastic resistance after 451 all show that the controversies of the fifth century were fought in monasteries as well as councils. Canon 4 of Chalcedon accordingly placed monks under the supervision of the local bishop.3

In depth

From the desert to the Rule of Benedict

Monasticism took two early forms in Egypt. Antony (c. 251–356) went into the desert as a solitary; Athanasius' Life of Antony (c. 360) made him famous across the empire and inspired many, including, according to his Confessions, Augustine. Pachomius (c. 292–346) founded communal (cenobitic) monasteries in Upper Egypt from about 320, with a written rule, common work and obedience to a superior. Between these lay the semi-eremitic settlements of Nitria and Scetis, whose teaching survives in the Sayings of the Desert Fathers (Apophthegmata Patrum).

In Asia Minor, Basil of Caesarea gave monasticism a place inside the Church's life: his rules emphasised community, service to the poor and obedience to Scripture, and Basil's monastery included a hospital. Evagrius of Pontus systematised desert teaching on prayer and on the eight 'evil thoughts', the ancestor of the seven deadly sins. In Syria, more extreme forms appeared, such as Symeon the Stylite on his pillar (from c. 423).

John Cassian brought Egyptian teaching to Marseilles around 415 and wrote the Institutes and Conferences. Martin of Tours founded monasteries in Gaul, and in Ireland monasteries became the main structure of the Church. The Rule of Benedict of Nursia (c. 530s), drawing on an earlier anonymous Rule of the Master, combined prayer, work and reading in a balanced community under an abbot. Through Carolingian sponsorship it became the standard rule of Western monasticism.

Monks had a large role in later controversies, as supporters of Cyril at Ephesus, of Eutyches and his opponents, and of icons in the eighth century. Bishops increasingly came from monasteries, especially in the East, where bishops must be celibate.

Why it matters today

Monasteries and the Rule of life they produced still shape spirituality, retreats and the daily office in many churches.
