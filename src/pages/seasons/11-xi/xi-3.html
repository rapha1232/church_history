Zwingli, Calvin and the Reformed tradition

Starring

Huldrych Zwingli (1484–1531) began preaching through Matthew's Gospel at Zurich in 1519. After public disputations in 1523, the city council abolished the Mass (1525), removed images and dissolved monasteries. Zwingli held that the Lord's Supper is a memorial in which Christ is spiritually present to faith, and that "is" in "This is my body" means "signifies". At the Marburg Colloquy (October 1529), Luther and Zwingli agreed on fourteen of fifteen articles but not on the Supper. Zwingli was killed at the battle of Kappel (1531). Heinrich Bullinger continued his work.1

John Calvin (1509–1564), a French humanist lawyer, published the Institutes of the Christian Religion in 1536 and expanded it until the final Latin edition of 1559.

Our wisdom, in so far as it ought to be deemed true and solid wisdom, consists almost entirely of two parts: the knowledge of God and of ourselves.

Calvin, Institutes 1.1.1 (1559). Trans. Henry Beveridge (1845). The second half was checked against the CCEL text; the opening follows the same translation. Q70 · partly checked

Calvin was minister at Geneva in 1536–38 and again from 1541 to his death. There he established a consistory of ministers and elders to oversee morals, and an academy (1559) that trained pastors for France and beyond. Geneva also executed the anti-Trinitarian Michael Servetus (1553), with Calvin's support for the prosecution.2 Calvin's eucharistic theology taught that believers truly feed on Christ's body by the Spirit's power. It sought a middle path and led to agreement with Zurich (Consensus Tigurinus, 1549).3

Spread and confessions

The Reformed tradition was international, and was shaped by confessions rather than by one founder.

  • France: the Gallican Confession (1559). Huguenots faced the Wars of Religion and the St Bartholomew's Day massacre (1572). The Edict of Nantes (1598) granted them toleration until its revocation in 1685.
  • Scotland: John Knox's reformation produced the Scots Confession (1560) and a presbyterian church.
  • The Netherlands: the Belgic Confession (1561).
  • The Palatinate: the Heidelberg Catechism (1563).
  • Switzerland: the Second Helvetic Confession (1566).
  • Hungary, Poland and Transylvania also had substantial Reformed churches.

The international of Dort (1618–19) rejected the Remonstrants (followers of Arminius) and defined five points on predestination and grace, later summarised (in English, from the 20th century) by the acronym "TULIP". The Westminster Confession (1646) became the standard of English-speaking Presbyterianism.4

In depth

The shape of the Reformed tradition

In Zurich Huldrych Zwingli began preaching through the Gospel of Matthew in 1519. Public disputations in 1523 led the city council to adopt the reform; images were removed and the Mass was replaced by a simple Lord's Supper in 1525. The Marburg Colloquy (1529) between Luther and Zwingli agreed on fourteen of fifteen articles but not on the presence of Christ in the Supper. Zwingli was killed at the battle of Kappel in 1531; Heinrich Bullinger succeeded him.

John Calvin, a French humanist trained in law, published the first edition of the Institutes of the Christian Religion in 1536, expanding it until the final edition of 1559. He settled in Geneva in 1536, was expelled in 1538, and returned in 1541 with his Ecclesiastical Ordinances, which set up four offices (pastors, doctors, elders, deacons) and a consistory to oversee morals. The execution of the antitrinitarian Michael Servetus in 1553, with the support of other Swiss churches, remains the most criticised episode of his career.

Calvin and Bullinger reached a common statement on the Lord's Supper, the Consensus Tigurinus (1549), which helped unite Swiss Protestantism. The Reformed movement then spread through confessions written for each national church: the French Confession (1559), the Scots Confession (1560), the Belgic Confession (1561), the Heidelberg Catechism (1563), the Second Helvetic Confession (1566), and later the Canons of Dort (1619) and the Westminster Confession (1647). Full texts of several of these are in this edition's texts library.

Reformed theology is often reduced to predestination. In fact its distinctive features include the sovereignty of God's grace, a strong emphasis on the covenant, the regulative principle in worship (only what Scripture commands), a spiritual presence of Christ in the Supper, and church discipline by elected elders.

Why it matters today

Reformed and Presbyterian churches, and the idea of a covenanted community, influenced democracy and education worldwide.
