Martin Luther and the Lutheran Reformation

Starring

Martin Luther (1483–1546), an Augustinian friar and professor of Bible at Wittenberg, came to understand the "righteousness of God" (Romans 1:17) as a gift received by faith, not a standard to be achieved. He later described this as a sudden discovery. Historians debate whether it occurred c. 1513–15 or c. 1518–19.1

The Ninety-Five Theses (1517)

On 31 October 1517 Luther sent his Disputation on the Power of Indulgences to Archbishop Albrecht. The famous posting on the door of the Castle Church is first reported after his death, by Melanchthon. Its historicity was challenged by Erwin Iserloh (1961) and is still debated.2

Dominus et magister noster Iesus Christus dicendo ‘Penitentiam agite &c.’ omnem vitam fidelium penitentiam esse voluit.

“Our Lord and Master Jesus Christ, in saying ‘Repent’, willed the whole life of the faithful to be one of repentance.”

Luther, Ninety-Five Theses (1517), thesis 1. Latin from the standard text; translation by this edition, compared in sense with the English at luther.de. Q66 · not re-checked

Hominem predicant, qui statim ut iactus nummus in cistam tinnierit evolare dicunt animam.

“They preach human doctrine who say that as soon as the coin clinks in the chest, the soul flies out [of purgatory].”

Thesis 27. Latin from the standard text; translation by this edition. Q67 · not re-checked

The break (1518–1521)

  • 1519, Leipzig. In debate with Johann Eck, Luther said that popes and councils could err, and defended some teachings of Hus.
  • 1520. Three programmatic treatises: To the Christian Nobility (the priesthood of all believers), The Babylonian Captivity of the Church (reducing the sacraments to baptism, the Lord's Supper and, provisionally, penance), and The Freedom of a Christian. Pope Leo X's bull Exsurge Domine (15 June 1520) condemned 41 propositions; Luther burned it.
  • 3 January 1521. Excommunication (Decet Romanum Pontificem).
  • April 1521, Diet of Worms. Luther refused to recant unless convinced "by Scripture or by clear reason". The words "Here I stand, I can do no other" appear in early printed versions, but their authenticity is uncertain. The Edict of Worms outlawed him. Frederick the Wise had him hidden at the Wartburg, where he translated the New Testament into German (September 1522; the complete Bible appeared in 1534).

Building a church

Luther opposed both radical reformers and the Peasants' War (1524–25). His harsh tract against the rebels damaged his reputation among the poor.3 Lutheran reform was carried out by princes and cities through church orders, visitations and Luther's catechisms (1529). Philip Melanchthon drafted the Augsburg Confession, presented to Charles V on 25 June 1530. It remains the defining Lutheran confession. The "Protestants" took their name from the Protestation at Speyer (1529). After war (the Schmalkaldic War, 1546–47), the Peace of Augsburg (1555) recognised Lutheranism in the Empire on the principle later summarised as cuius regio, eius religio: the ruler decides the territory's religion. Reformed and Anabaptist Christians were excluded. Inner-Lutheran disputes were settled in the Formula of Concord (1577), and the Book of Concord (1580) collected the confessions. Lutheranism became the established religion of Scandinavia.4

Luther's late writings against the Jews (1543), with their call to burn synagogues, are a grave stain on his legacy. Lutheran Churches have formally repudiated them, for example the Evangelical Lutheran Church in America in 1994. They are discussed in Part IV study H.5
