The English Reformation

Starring

The English Reformation began as an act of state. Henry VIII sought the annulment of his marriage to Catherine of Aragon, and Clement VII, under pressure from Catherine's nephew Charles V, would not grant it. Henry broke with Rome.1

  • Henry VIII. The Act of (1534) made the king Supreme Head of the Church of England. Thomas More and John Fisher were executed (1535). The monasteries were dissolved (1536–1540). The English Bible was authorised (the Great Bible, 1539), drawing heavily on William Tyndale, who had been executed in 1536 near Brussels. Henry's own doctrine remained largely Catholic (the Six Articles, 1539).
  • Edward VI (1547–53). Archbishop Thomas Cranmer introduced the Book of Common Prayer (1549; a more Protestant revision in 1552) and the Forty-Two Articles (1553).
  • Mary I (1553–58) restored Catholicism. About 280–290 Protestants were burned, including Cranmer (1556), commemorated in John Foxe's Acts and Monuments (1563).
  • Elizabeth I. The Elizabethan Settlement (1559) restored royal supremacy (as "Supreme Governor") and a revised Prayer Book. The Thirty-Nine Articles (1563; confirmed by Parliament in 1571) defined doctrine. Catholic priests were executed as traitors after the papal excommunication of Elizabeth (1570): about 180 Catholics in her reign, by common estimates.2

We are accounted righteous before God, only for the merit of our Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ by Faith, and not for our own works or deservings…

Thirty-Nine Articles, Article XI (1571). Text checked against the Church of England website. Q71 · checked

Article VI holds that "Holy Scripture containeth all things necessary to salvation". Article XXI says that general councils "may err, and sometimes have erred".3

Later developments:

  • Puritans sought further reform. Some separated, among them the first English Baptists: John Smyth in Amsterdam (1609) and Thomas Helwys in London (1612).
  • Civil war and Restoration. The civil wars brought presbyterian and independent church orders (1640s–50s). The Restoration (1660) and the 1662 Prayer Book re-established episcopacy and ejected about 2,000 nonconforming ministers.
  • Anglican identity. Its distinctive identity, called "via media" by later writers such as Newman, was articulated by Richard Hooker (Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity, 1590s). It became a worldwide Communion through colonial and missionary expansion (Part III).

Revisionist historians (Christopher Haigh, Eamon Duffy) argue that the English Reformation was imposed from above on a largely Catholic population. Others (A. G. Dickens) emphasised popular Protestant enthusiasm.4

In depth

Why the English Reformation is distinctive

Henry VIII's break with Rome was driven by his wish to annul his marriage to Catherine of Aragon. A series of statutes cut off appeals and payments to Rome, and the Act of Supremacy (1534) declared the king Supreme Head of the Church of England. The monasteries were dissolved between 1536 and 1540, and their lands sold. Henry's doctrinal position remained largely Catholic; the Six Articles (1539) affirmed and clerical celibacy.

Under Edward VI (1547–1553), Archbishop Thomas Cranmer moved the Church in a Protestant direction with the Book of Common Prayer (1549, more Reformed in 1552) and the Forty-Two Articles (1553). Mary I (1553–1558) restored papal authority; about 280 Protestants were burned, including Cranmer.

Elizabeth I's settlement (1559) restored the royal supremacy (now as 'Supreme Governor') and a revised Prayer Book. The Thirty-Nine Articles (1563, English text 1571) set out a moderately Reformed doctrine (see the full text). The Church kept bishops, cathedrals, vestments and a liturgical calendar, which left it open to pressure from who wanted further reform and from Catholics, who after the papal excommunication of Elizabeth in 1570 were treated as potential traitors.

Richard Hooker's Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity (1594–1597) defended the settlement through Scripture, tradition and reason. The idea that Anglicanism is a 'middle way' (via media) between Rome and Geneva is largely a later, especially nineteenth-century, interpretation; the Elizabethan church saw itself as part of the Reformed Protestant world.

Why it matters today

The Anglican Communion of about 85 million members grew from this settlement and still debates its identity.
