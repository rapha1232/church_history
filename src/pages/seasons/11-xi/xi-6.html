The Reformation disputes are often summarised in the later formula of the "solas": sola scriptura, sola fide, sola gratia (and in 20th-century usage, solus Christus and soli Deo gloria). The list as such is not a sixteenth-century formula.1

Justification

Lutheran (Augsburg Confession, 1530)

Also they teach that men can not be justified [obtain forgiveness of sins and righteousness] before God by their own powers, merits, or works; but are justified freely [of grace] for Christ's sake through faith, when they believe that they are received into favor, and their sins forgiven for Christ's sake, who by his death hath satisfied for our sins. This faith doth God impute for righteousness before him.

Augsburg Confession, Art. IV. English in Philip Schaff, Creeds of Christendom, vol. 3 (1877). Checked against CCEL. Q68 · checked

Catholic (Council of Trent, 1547)

If any one saith, that by faith alone the impious is justified; in such wise as to mean, that nothing else is required to co-operate in order to the obtaining the grace of Justification, and that it is not in any way necessary, that he be prepared and disposed by the movement of his own will; let him be .

Trent, Session 6 (13 January 1547), canon 9. Trans. J. Waterworth (1848), as at papalencyclicals.net. Checked. Q69 · checked

Note what Trent condemns. It does not reject justification by grace, which its decree affirms is unmerited (ch. 8). It rejects "faith alone" understood as excluding human cooperation and inner renewal. Lutherans spoke of righteousness imputed to the believer. Trent spoke of righteousness infused, with justification including sanctification. The Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification (signed by the Lutheran World Federation and the Catholic Church at Augsburg, 31 October 1999; later affirmed by Methodists in 2006, by the World Communion of Reformed Churches in 2017 and by the Anglican Communion) states a "consensus on basic truths". It declares that the sixteenth-century condemnations do not apply to the teaching it presents, while acknowledging remaining differences.2

The Lord's Supper

PositionHeld bySummary
TransubstantiationCatholic (Lateran IV, 1215; Trent, Session 13, 1551)The substance of bread and wine is changed into Christ's body and blood; the appearances (accidents) remain. The Mass is a true propitiatory sacrifice, one with Calvary.
Sacramental unionLutheranChrist's true body and blood are present "in, with and under" the bread and wine and received by all communicants. Rejects transubstantiation and the Mass as sacrifice. ("Consubstantiation" is a label used by critics, not by Lutheran confessions.)
Spiritual real presenceCalvin; Reformed confessions; much Anglican teachingBelievers truly receive Christ's body by faith through the Spirit, lifted up to heaven where Christ is.

Other issues

  • Scripture and tradition. The Reformers held Scripture to be the sole infallible rule. Trent (Session 4, 1546) received Scripture and unwritten apostolic traditions "with equal affection of piety", and defined the canon including the books (II.9).
  • Sacraments. Catholics hold seven. Lutherans and Reformed hold two (baptism and the Supper), with penance or absolution valued by Lutherans. speak of "ordinances".
  • Baptism. Infant baptism was retained by Catholics, Lutherans, Reformed and Anglicans, and rejected by Anabaptists and later Baptists.
  • Predestination. All parties drew on Augustine. Reformed orthodoxy (Dort) taught unconditional election. Lutherans affirmed election to salvation but resisted double predestination. Arminians and later Methodists stressed resistible grace. Within Catholicism, Dominicans and Jesuits disputed grace and free will (the de auxiliis controversy, 1597–1607), as did the Jansenists later.
  • Ministry and authority. The priesthood of all believers; ordained ministry as office rather than sacrament (except for Anglicans and some Lutherans); rejection of papal authority; varied views of episcopacy.
  • Saints, Mary and purgatory. The Reformers rejected the invocation of saints and purgatory, while generally keeping Mary's title and, in Luther's case, her perpetual virginity.
