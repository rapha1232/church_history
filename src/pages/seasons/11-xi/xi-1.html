Background: indulgences, humanism, printing and politics

c. 1450–15173 min

An indulgence was a remission of the temporal punishment still due for sins whose guilt had been forgiven in confession. It did not forgive sins as such, though popular preaching often blurred the distinction.

Starring

  • Indulgences. In Catholic theology an is a remission of the temporal punishment due to sins already forgiven. It draws on the "treasury of merits" of Christ and the saints, and can be applied to souls in purgatory "by way of suffrage" (Sixtus IV, 1476). In practice, indulgences were sold to fund projects. The St Peter's indulgence of 1515 was preached in Germany by the Dominican Johann Tetzel. Part of its proceeds repaid Archbishop Albrecht of Mainz's debts to the Fugger bank for papal dispensations.1
  • Humanism. The humanist call to return "to the sources" (ad fontes) produced critical editions of classical and Christian texts. Lorenzo Valla exposed the Donation of Constantine (1440). Erasmus published the first printed Greek New Testament (1516), with a new Latin translation and notes. His satire of clerical abuse and his "philosophy of Christ" prepared many readers for reform, though he did not join the Reformation himself and broke with Luther over free will (1524–25).2
  • Printing. Gutenberg's movable type (c. 1450s) made rapid, cheap circulation of pamphlets possible. Luther became the most published author of his age, and historians speak of the Reformation as a "media event" (Andrew Pettegree).3
  • Politics. The Holy Roman Empire was a patchwork of princes and free cities. The emperor Charles V (from 1519) also ruled Spain, the Netherlands and much of Italy, and was preoccupied with France and the Ottomans, who besieged Vienna in 1529. Princes and city councils could therefore protect reformers and control church property.4

In depth

Indulgences: the theology behind the controversy

An indulgence was a remission of the temporal punishment still due for sins whose guilt had been forgiven in confession. It did not forgive sins as such, though popular preaching often blurred the distinction. The theology rested on the idea of a 'treasury of merits' of Christ and the saints, which the Church could apply to the faithful; Clement VI defined it in the bull Unigenitus (1343). Sixtus IV in 1476 extended indulgences explicitly to souls in purgatory 'by way of suffrage'.

The indulgence that provoked Luther was the one proclaimed in 1515 by Leo X for rebuilding St Peter's in Rome. In parts of Germany it was preached under Albrecht of Brandenburg, who had borrowed heavily from the Fugger bankers to pay for papal permission to hold three bishoprics at once; half the proceeds in his territories went to repay that debt. The Dominican Johann Tetzel preached it near Wittenberg in 1517, though not in electoral Saxony itself, where Frederick the Wise had banned it to protect his own relic collection.

Other conditions favoured a rapid spread of reform: printing, which made Luther the best-selling author in Europe within a few years; humanism, whose call 'back to the sources' produced Erasmus' Greek New Testament (1516); resentment of papal taxation in Germany; and the political structure of the Holy Roman Empire, where princes and city councils could protect preachers against the emperor.

Why it matters today
