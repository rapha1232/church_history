The Radical Reformation

c. 1521–16003 minPreviously: Zwingli, Calvin and the Reformed tradition

George H. Williams' term "Radical Reformation" (1962) groups very different movements that rejected both Catholic and magisterial Protestant church–state arrangements.

Starring

George H. Williams' term "Radical Reformation" (1962) groups very different movements that rejected both Catholic and magisterial Protestant church–state arrangements.1

  • Swiss Brethren. On 21 January 1525, in Zurich, Conrad Grebel baptised George Blaurock, and others followed: adult believers baptising each other in defiance of Zwingli and the council. Felix Manz was drowned by Zurich authorities in January 1527, the first Anabaptist executed by Protestants. The Schleitheim Confession (February 1527), drafted by Michael Sattler (burned in May 1527 by Catholic authorities), set out believers' baptism, church discipline (the ban), separation from the world, refusal of the sword and of oaths, and a congregationally chosen pastor.2
  • Apocalyptic radicals. Thomas Müntzer, executed after the Peasants' War in 1525. The Anabaptist kingdom at Münster (1534–35), which introduced polygamy and was crushed. Münster was used for centuries to discredit all .3
  • Menno Simons (1496–1561), a former priest, gathered the peaceable Dutch and North German Anabaptists, later called Mennonites. The Hutterites practised community of goods in Moravia. The Amish separated from the Swiss Brethren in 1693.
  • Spiritualists and anti-Trinitarians. Sebastian Franck and Caspar Schwenckfeld; Michael Servetus; Faustus Socinus and the Polish Brethren (the Racovian Catechism, 1605); the Unitarian church of Transylvania, under the Edict of Torda (1568).

Thousands of Anabaptists were executed by Catholic and Protestant authorities in the sixteenth century. The Martyrs Mirror (1660) preserved their memory.4 In 2010 the Lutheran World Federation formally asked Mennonites for forgiveness for Lutheran persecution of Anabaptists.5 The Anabaptist emphasis on voluntary church membership and religious liberty became influential in later Baptist, Quaker and free-church traditions.

In depth

The Radical Reformation in its variety

The first adult baptisms of the Reformation era took place in Zurich on 21 January 1525, when Conrad Grebel baptized George Blaurock, who then baptized others. Zwingli's former allies had concluded that the Church must consist only of believers who chose baptism. The Zurich council made rebaptism punishable, and Felix Manz was drowned in the Limmat in 1527, the first Anabaptist executed by Protestants.

The Schleitheim Confession (1527), drafted under Michael Sattler, set out seven articles: believers' baptism, the ban (church discipline), the breaking of bread among the baptized, separation from the world, pastors chosen by the congregation, refusal of the sword, and refusal of oaths. Sattler was executed that same year by Catholic authorities at Rottenburg.

A very different episode was the Anabaptist kingdom at Münster (1534–1535), where leaders seized the city, introduced community of goods and polygamy, and were crushed by a siege. Münster made 'Anabaptist' a byword for sedition. The former priest Menno Simons (d. 1561) regrouped the peaceful Dutch and North German Anabaptists, who became the Mennonites. The Hutterites in Moravia practised community of goods. The Amish separated from Swiss Mennonites in 1693.

The Radical Reformation also included Spiritualists, such as Caspar Schwenckfeld and Sebastian Franck, who downplayed outward sacraments, and Antitrinitarians, such as the Socinians of Poland (Racovian Catechism, 1605). All of them were persecuted by Catholics and Protestants alike; thousands of Anabaptists were executed in the sixteenth century. Their convictions about voluntary membership and the separation of church and state later passed into Baptist and free-church traditions.

Why it matters today

Anabaptist convictions about believers’ baptism, pacifism and church–state separation live on in Baptist, Mennonite and free churches.
