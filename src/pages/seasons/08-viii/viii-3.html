Starring

Byzantine , the official hostility to religious images, lasted with an interval from the 720s or 730s to 843. Its causes have been sought in several places :

  • Old Testament prohibitions, perhaps sharpened by contact with Islam and Judaism. Caliph Yazid II issued an edict against images in 721.
  • Military defeat, interpreted as divine punishment for idolatry.
  • Imperial suspicion of the power of monasteries and holy objects.
  • Theological concerns about depicting the divine.

Leslie Brubaker and John Haldon have argued that the iconophile sources exaggerate both the scale of persecution and the role of Leo III, and that coherent iconoclast policy began with Constantine V (741–775).1

The decisive theological defence came from John of Damascus (d. c. 750). He was a monk at Mar Saba near Jerusalem, writing in the caliphate, beyond the emperor's reach. His three Treatises on the Divine Images argued that the incarnation makes the invisible God depictable, that matter can mediate grace, and that veneration differs from worship. The council of Nicaea II (787) adopted these arguments. After a second iconoclasm (815–843), Empress Theodora restored the icons on 11 March 843.

The Western response

Rome consistently opposed iconoclasm. Gregory II, Gregory III (Roman of 731) and Hadrian I all did so. The Frankish court reacted differently. It received Nicaea II's acts in a Latin translation that appeared to require that images be given the same adoration (adoratio) as the Trinity. Theodulf of Orléans, in the name of Charlemagne, composed the Opus Caroli regis contra synodum (the "Libri Carolini", c. 790–793). It rejected both the iconoclast council of 754 and the "adoration" of images it attributed to 787. Images were permitted for decoration and instruction, but not to be venerated. The Council of Frankfurt (794) adopted this position. Pope Hadrian I defended Nicaea II in a reply.

The episode matters for three reasons:

  • It shows the Frankish Church asserting its own theological judgement against a council received in Rome and Constantinople.
  • The Opus Caroli also criticises the Greek profession that the Spirit proceeds "through the Son" and defends the , an early sign of the controversy to come.
  • It shows how far mistranslation was already shaping East–West perceptions.2

In depth

Iconoclasm: causes, arguments and outcome

Byzantine iconoclasm had two periods: about 730–787, under Leo III and especially Constantine V, and 815–843. Traditional dates for Leo's first measures (726 or 730) depend on later, hostile sources, and recent historians think his actions may have been more limited than once claimed. Explanations for the movement include reaction to military disasters seen as divine punishment, the influence of Islamic and Jewish criticism of images, and imperial efforts to control the Church and monasteries. None is decisive on its own.

The iconoclast case, formulated at the Council of Hieria (754), was Christological: an image of Christ either depicts only his humanity, dividing him like Nestorius, or claims to depict his divinity, which cannot be circumscribed and so confuses the natures. The only true image of Christ is the .

Defenders of icons, above all John of Damascus (writing in Muslim-ruled Palestine) and later Theodore the Studite and Nikephoros, answered that the incarnation itself made God visible, so to refuse to depict Christ is to deny that he became truly human. They distinguished honour or veneration given to images from the worship due to God alone, and argued that honour passes from the image to its prototype.

Nicaea II (787) adopted this position. A second iconoclast period followed military defeats, and the final restoration came in March 843 under the regent Theodora. It is still celebrated in the Orthodox Church as the Triumph of Orthodoxy on the first Sunday of Lent.

Why it matters today

Icons are central to Orthodox worship, and debates over art in churches still echo iconoclasm and its defence.
