Photius, Bulgaria and the ninth-century crisis

The "Photian schism" was the first East–West crisis in which papal claims, the and missionary rivalry all came together. Its story was later retold by both sides in polemical forms. Francis Dvornik's research (1948) overturned much of the traditional Western account.1

The events

  1. 858. Ignatius was forced out amid court politics. Photius, a lay scholar and senior civil servant, was ordained through all the clerical ranks in six days and made patriarch. Ignatius' supporters appealed to Rome.
  2. 861. A council in Constantinople, with papal legates, confirmed Ignatius' deposition. Pope Nicholas I (858–867) disowned his legates and, at a Roman of 863, declared Photius deposed. Nicholas held a strong theory of papal authority over the whole Church.
  3. 864–866. Khan Boris of Bulgaria was baptised under Byzantine sponsorship, but then turned to Rome for an independent Church. Nicholas sent bishops and a long letter of guidance, the Responsa ad consulta Bulgarorum (866). Latin missionaries in Bulgaria taught the Filioque and repeated chrismations administered by Greek priests.
  4. 867. Photius' Encyclical to the Eastern Patriarchs denounced the Latin practices in Bulgaria and, for the first time officially, the Filioque as heresy. A council in Constantinople declared Nicholas deposed. Weeks later the new emperor Basil I deposed Photius and restored Ignatius.
  5. 869–870. A council in Constantinople with Hadrian II's legates condemned Photius. (The Catholic Church counts it as the "Eighth Ecumenical Council".) In a separate session immediately afterwards, the emperor and the Eastern representatives assigned Bulgaria to Constantinople's jurisdiction, over the legates' protests.
  6. 877–880. On Ignatius' death (877), Photius returned. The council of 879–880, with John VIII's legates, recognised him, annulled 869–870 and reaffirmed the creed without additions.
  7. 886. Leo VI deposed Photius. Dvornik showed that the alleged "second Photian schism", a second papal excommunication of Photius, is a later legend. Photius died in communion with Rome.2

Photius' Mystagogy of the Holy Spirit (c. 885/6) gave the classic Eastern case against the Filioque. In it he describes the popes Leo III and John VIII as defenders of the unaltered creed.3

Cyril and Methodius

Meanwhile, the brothers Constantine (Cyril) and Methodius of Thessalonica were sent by Photius' government to Great Moravia (863). They created the Glagolitic alphabet and a Slavonic liturgy. Popes Hadrian II and John VIII approved the Slavonic liturgy against Frankish opposition. The Frankish clergy expelled Methodius' disciples after his death (885). They took the Slavonic tradition to Bulgaria, where it flourished, and from there it reached Serbia and Rus'. This mission, sponsored from Constantinople and approved by Rome, is now honoured by both Churches.

In depth

Photius, Pope Nicholas and the Bulgarian question

In 858 Emperor Michael III's government forced Patriarch Ignatius to resign and appointed Photius, a learned layman, who was ordained through every rank in a few days. Ignatius's supporters appealed to Rome. Pope Nicholas I declared Photius deposed in 863.

Meanwhile Khan Boris I of Bulgaria was baptized around 864, with Byzantine clergy. Seeking a church of his own, he turned to Rome in 866, and Latin missionaries arrived. Photius responded in 867 with an encyclical to the Eastern patriarchs attacking Latin practices in Bulgaria and, for the first time, the Western addition of the Filioque to the creed. A council in Constantinople declared Nicholas deposed.

A palace coup the same year removed Photius and restored Ignatius. The Council of 869–870 condemned Photius; the West later counted it as the Eighth Ecumenical Council. But in 870 Bulgaria placed itself under Constantinople. After Ignatius died in 877 Photius was restored, and the council of 879–880, attended by papal legates, reconciled him with Rome. The East regards this second council as authoritative.

In the same years Constantinople sent the brothers Constantine (Cyril) and Methodius to Moravia (863). Their Slavonic alphabet and liturgy, rejected in Moravia after Methodius's death, found a home in Bulgaria.
