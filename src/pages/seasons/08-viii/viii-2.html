The Franks, the papacy, and the coronation of 800

Starring

In the mid-eighth century the papacy turned from Constantinople to the Franks. The reasons were mainly practical:

  • Iconoclast emperors were unable or unwilling to defend Rome against the Lombards.
  • Leo III's government (c. 732–733) had transferred papal estates in Sicily and Calabria, and jurisdiction over Illyricum, southern Italy and Sicily, from Rome to Constantinople. The date and circumstances are debated.
  • The Lombards took Ravenna in 751, ending the Byzantine exarchate.

Pope Stephen II crossed the Alps in 753–754 and anointed the Frankish king Pippin at Saint-Denis. Pippin campaigned against the Lombards and gave the conquered territories to "St Peter" (the "Donation of Pippin", 756). This was the foundation of the Papal States, which lasted until 1870.1 The forged Donation of Constantine, which claimed that Constantine had granted the pope authority over the West, was probably composed in this period (mid- to late eighth century). Lorenzo Valla exposed it as a forgery in 1440.2

Christmas Day, 800

On 25 December 800 Pope Leo III crowned Charlemagne in St Peter's, and the Roman people acclaimed him emperor. In Constantinople the throne was held by Irene. She ruled in her own name from 797, the first woman to do so. Westerners could argue that the imperial office was vacant; Byzantines regarded the coronation as usurpation. After years of conflict, Michael I recognised Charlemagne as basileus in 812, but not as "emperor of the Romans", a title Byzantium reserved for itself.3

From now on there were two Christian empires. Each claimed to be Roman, and each had a Church closely tied to its ruler. The Carolingian Church was confident, reforming and theologically assertive. As the next chapters show, it became the main source of Western positions that Byzantium found objectionable, notably on images and on the . The papacy was caught between the two.4

In depth

The Donation of Constantine and the Carolingian Church

Pepin's donation of 754–756 gave the pope lands in central Italy captured from the Lombards, the core of the Papal States, which lasted until 1870. Around the same time someone, probably in Rome, forged the Donation of Constantine (Constitutum Constantini), which claimed that Constantine, cured of leprosy by Pope Sylvester, had granted the pope authority over Rome and the western provinces and imperial regalia. The forgery was widely accepted in the Middle Ages and was used in papal claims, though not always. Lorenzo Valla demonstrated its falsity in 1440 on linguistic grounds.

Charlemagne (king 768, emperor 800) treated the Church as part of his responsibility as ruler. He promoted the Roman liturgy, Benedictine monasticism, correct Latin texts and schools, an effort historians call the Carolingian renewal. Alcuin of York was his chief adviser on education.

The coronation of Christmas Day 800 was possible partly because the throne at Constantinople was held by a woman, Irene, whom Westerners could treat as leaving it vacant. Byzantium regarded the title as usurped and recognised Charlemagne only as 'emperor' (not 'of the Romans') in 812.

Theological independence followed. The Libri Carolini (c. 790–793) criticised Nicaea II on images, working from a poor Latin translation, and the of Frankfurt (794) rejected the council. Frankish theologians also defended the Filioque, and Charlemagne pressed Pope Leo III to add it to the creed. Leo refused and had the creed without it engraved on silver tablets in St Peter's.

Why it matters today

The coronation of 800 created the Western link between pope and emperor that shaped Europe’s church–state history.
