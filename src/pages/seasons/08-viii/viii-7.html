How the factors accumulated

c. 400–10502 minPreviously: Photius, Bulgaria and the ninth-century crisis

By the mid-eleventh century every factor discussed in this volume was in place. None was new. What changed was that they reinforced one another and that the conditions that had previously contained them weakened.

  • Distinct theological cultures. Latin Augustinian and Greek Cappadocian-Byzantine traditions developed with little contact. Each became fluent in its own terms and suspicious of the other's.
  • Distinct political frameworks. There were two empires, and the papacy had become a territorial power allied to the Western one. That made every ecclesiastical quarrel a political one.
  • Competing ecclesiologies. The papacy's sense of its universal authority was growing, and would grow sharply with the eleventh-century reform. Byzantium's model was pentarchic and synodal.1
  • Competing jurisdictions. These ran along a long frontier: Illyricum, Bulgaria, southern Italy and Sicily.
  • Customs as identity markers (ch. 4), and a doctrinal issue, the , that could be presented as heresy.
  • Precedents of rupture. The Acacian schism (484–519), the Monothelite crisis and the Photian conflict had already accustomed both sides to breaks in communion, and to their eventual repair.2

Until the eleventh century, breaks were followed by reconciliation. Whether the break of 1054 was different, and if so why, is the subject of Volume IX.

In depth

Estrangement rather than a single break

The idea that East and West drifted apart over centuries, rather than splitting at a single moment, was put most influentially by the Catholic theologian Yves Congar in After Nine Hundred Years (1959). He described a gradual 'estrangement' in which each side lost the habit of living with the other, so that differences that had once been tolerated became intolerable.

On this view the factors described in this volume reinforced one another: language (fewer people could read the other's theology); politics (the Frankish empire and the papal alliance with it); ecclesiology (Rome's growing claims of jurisdiction versus Eastern conciliar and patriarchal structures); theology (the Filioque and different styles of Trinitarian thought); and custom (, celibacy, fasting). None of them alone made schism inevitable.

Moments of open rupture, such as the Photian crisis of 863–867 or the mutual excommunications of 1054, were each followed by restored relations of a kind. What made later separation permanent, most historians agree, was the experience of the Crusades and especially the sack of Constantinople in 1204 (IX.3), which turned a quarrel among clergy into hostility among peoples.
