The Filioque

c. 400–20264 minPreviously: Customs, discipline and liturgy

The Creed of 381 says the Holy Spirit "proceeds from the Father" (John 15:26). The Latin Church came to add Filioque, "and [from] the Son".

Starring

The Creed of 381 says the Holy Spirit "proceeds from the Father" (John 15:26). The Latin Church came to add Filioque, "and [from] the Son". This one word became the most persistent doctrinal issue between East and West. Two questions have always been intertwined:

  • Is the doctrine true?
  • Did the Western Church have the right to add it to the common creed?

Origins of the doctrine and of the addition

  • Latin theology. Hilary of Poitiers, Ambrose and above all Augustine taught that the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son. Augustine added that the Spirit proceeds "principally" (principaliter) from the Father, who gives the Son the ability to breathe forth the Spirit.1
  • Greek theology. Greek fathers, including Gregory of Nyssa, Cyril of Alexandria and John of Damascus, could speak of the Spirit as coming "through the Son" or as "proper to the Son", while insisting that the Father is the sole cause (aitia) within the Trinity.
  • Maximus the Confessor. In his Letter to Marinus (c. 655), Maximus defended the Roman usage of his day. He explained that the Latins did not make the Son a cause of the Spirit, but meant the Spirit's coming forth through the Son. He suggested that difficulties arose from the limits of Latin vocabulary. This text is central to modern dialogue.
  • Spain. The phrase appears in creeds associated with the Third Council of Toledo (589), where the Visigothic king Reccared renounced Christianity. Its presence in the original text of that council has been questioned, but most scholars accept early Spanish use.
  • Frankish adoption. The Franks adopted the Spanish creed with the in their liturgy (late 8th century). The Council of Friuli (796/797) defended it. Frankish monks in Jerusalem were accused by Greek monks of heresy for singing it (808). Charlemagne's Council of Aachen (809) then asked Pope Leo III to approve the addition.
  • Rome's refusal and adoption. Leo III accepted the doctrine but refused to add it to the creed. According to the Liber Pontificalis, he had the creed engraved without the Filioque, in Greek and Latin, on two silver shields placed in St Peter's. Rome adopted the sung creed with the Filioque only in 1014, at the request of Emperor Henry II, for his coronation by Benedict VIII. The source is Berno of Reichenau.

The arguments

Eastern objections (from Photius onwards)

  • Canonical. Ephesus canon 7 forbids "a different faith" (Q16). A common creed may not be altered by one Church without an ecumenical council.
  • Theological. The Father is the sole source (the "monarchy" of the Father). Procession from two sources introduces two principles. Making procession a property shared by Father and Son confuses the persons, or leaves the Spirit subordinate.
  • Scriptural. John 15:26 says "from the Father". Texts on the Son "sending" the Spirit refer to his mission in time, not his eternal origin.

Western defence (from the Carolingians to Florence)

  • Scriptural. The Spirit is "the Spirit of the Son" (Gal. 4:6) and "receives from what is mine" (John 16:14). The Son's sending of the Spirit reflects his eternal relation.
  • Theological. The persons are distinguished only by opposed relations, so if the Spirit did not proceed from the Son, Son and Spirit would not be distinguished. Father and Son spirate "as one principle" (Lyon II, 1274; Florence, 1439), with the Father as source of the whole Godhead.
  • Canonical. The addition clarifies the creed and does not change its faith. Ephesus canon 7 forbids a contrary creed, not an explanatory word.

Modern clarifications

  • 1995. The Pontifical Council for Promoting Christian Unity's document The Greek and Latin Traditions regarding the Procession of the Holy Spirit distinguished the Greek ekporeusis, the origin of the Spirit from the Father as sole source, from the Latin processio, a broader term. It affirmed the Father's monarchy and the normative value of the Greek creed of 381.
  • 2003. The North American Orthodox–Catholic Theological Consultation, in The Filioque: A Church-Dividing Issue?, recommended that the Catholic Church use the original creed in catechetical and liturgical texts. It also proposed that the Filioque need not be church-dividing.
  • Liturgical practice. Popes have on several occasions recited the creed without the Filioque with Orthodox patriarchs, and Eastern Catholic Churches commonly omit it.
  • Since 2024. The question is on the agenda of the Joint International Commission (Vol. VII ch. 6).2

Why it matters today

The Filioque is still the most discussed doctrinal difference between Catholics and Orthodox, and some Western churches now omit it.
