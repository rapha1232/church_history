Much of the medieval polemic between Greeks and Latins concerned practice rather than doctrine. Lists of Latin "errors" circulated in Byzantium from the ninth century, and Latin counter-lists answered them.1 Some differences were long established and had been tolerated. Others became controversial only when other tensions made them signs of a different "faith".

In depth

Differences of practice and why they mattered

Many disputes between East and West concerned practice rather than doctrine, but in the Middle Ages practice was often seen as an expression of faith. The list compiled by both sides grew over time.

The Quinisext Council of 692 (in Trullo) issued canons meant to complete the fifth and sixth councils. Several criticised Roman customs directly: canon 13 defended married priests against the Roman requirement of continence; canon 55 forbade fasting on Saturdays in Lent, a Roman practice; canon 82 forbade depicting Christ as a lamb. Rome never accepted the council as a whole.

Other differences included the use of leavened bread in the East and unleavened bread (azymes) in the West, which became a major issue in the 1050s; Western clergy were clean-shaven while Eastern clergy wore beards; in the West only bishops confirmed, while in the East priests chrismated infants at baptism; the date and rules of Lent differed; and Eastern churches distributed Communion in both kinds to all, including infants.

In periods of good relations, writers on both sides accepted such diversity, as Pope Gregory I and Photius both did at times. In periods of conflict, lists of 'errors' circulated, mixing serious issues like the with trivial ones. Modern dialogue has largely returned to the earlier principle that diversity of custom need not break communion.

Why it matters today

Leavened or unleavened bread, married priests and fasting rules still mark the different practices of East and West.
