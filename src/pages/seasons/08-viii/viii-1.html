Two worlds: language and politics (c. 400–750)

Language

The Roman Church used Greek until the third century. By the late fourth century, Latin was its liturgical and theological language. Educated Westerners increasingly lacked Greek: Augustine read it only with difficulty, and Leo the Great could not read Eutyches' dossier without translators. From the late sixth century, knowledge of Latin also declined in Constantinople. Each side came to know the other's theology through translations, sometimes faulty ones, as the Frankish of Nicaea II shows. Terms that seemed equivalent were not: hypostasis and persona; ekporeusis and processio.1

Political separation

  • 476. The last Western emperor at Ravenna, Romulus Augustulus, was deposed by Odoacer. Constantinople's emperor remained, in law, the emperor of the West too. Western Christians increasingly lived under Germanic kings, many of them ("Arian") Christians until the sixth century.
  • 535–554. Justinian's wars reconquered Italy at catastrophic cost. Rome remained part of the empire. Popes needed imperial confirmation of their election, and several were coerced: Vigilius (553) and Martin I (653–655).
  • 568. The Lombards invaded Italy. Imperial control shrank to Ravenna, Rome, Naples, Sicily and the south.
  • The "Byzantine papacy" (537–752). Many popes of the late seventh and early eighth centuries were Greek- or Syriac-speaking, often refugees from the Arab conquests. Eastern feasts and practices entered Roman use, among them Marian feasts and the Agnus Dei under the Syrian pope Sergius I.2 The period shows that Rome was still culturally part of the Byzantine world well into the eighth century.
  • The Arab conquests (630s–710s). The conquest of Syria, Egypt, North Africa and Spain removed the Mediterranean's southern half from Christian rule. It reduced Alexandria, Antioch and Jerusalem to minority Churches under Islamic rule and left Rome and Constantinople as the two great sees of Christendom.

The historian Henri Pirenne argued that Islam's expansion, more than the Germanic invasions, broke the Mediterranean unity of the ancient world and turned the West towards the north. His thesis in its strong form is contested, but most historians accept the general shift it describes.3

In depth

When East and West stopped understanding each other

Until about 400 educated Westerners read Greek, and Rome's own liturgy and theology had been Greek-speaking until the third century. By the sixth century this had changed. Augustine read Greek only with difficulty; Gregory the Great, who had lived in Constantinople for years, never learned it. In the East, knowledge of Latin declined after Justinian, though Latin remained the language of law for a time.

Politically, the West broke into barbarian kingdoms after 476, while the East remained a Roman state with an emperor in Constantinople. Justinian's reconquest of Italy (535–554) restored imperial rule for a while, but the Lombard invasion from 568 left only scattered imperial territories, governed from the Exarchate of Ravenna.

From 537 to 752 the papacy lived in the 'Byzantine' period: popes needed imperial confirmation, and between 678 and 752 most were Greek or Syrian by origin. The relationship could be brutal. Pope Martin I was arrested in 653 for opposing , condemned in Constantinople and exiled to Crimea, where he died. Rome remained loyal to the empire in principle but increasingly had to fend for itself.

The decisive change came when Ravenna fell to the Lombards in 751. With no effective imperial protection, Pope Stephen II turned to the Frankish king Pepin. From then on the papacy looked north, and the two halves of Christendom developed their theology, liturgy and law with less and less contact.

Why it matters today

Language and politics drove East and West apart before doctrine did; the lesson still applies to divided Christians.
