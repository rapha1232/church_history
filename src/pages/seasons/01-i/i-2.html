Exile, the Persian period and the Second Temple

c. BC587–BC3323 minPreviously: Ancient Israel and the First Temple

Starring

The Babylonian exile (587/6–539 BC) was the formative crisis of ancient Judaism. Without land, king or Temple, the exiles preserved their identity through law, Sabbath, circumcision and the collection and editing of their traditions. Many scholars date much of the editing of the Torah, the historical books and the prophets to the exile and the Persian period that followed.

  • 539 BC. Cyrus the Great of Persia conquered Babylon. His policy of allowing deported peoples to return and restore their cults is reflected in the Cyrus Cylinder, which does not mention the Jews specifically, and in the Bible (Ezra 1; Isaiah 45:1, which calls Cyrus God's "anointed").1
  • c. 515 BC. The Second Temple was completed in Jerusalem. Judah became the small Persian province of Yehud.
  • 5th century BC. Ezra and Nehemiah carried out reforms, enforcing the law and separating from neighbouring peoples. The dates and the order of their missions are debated.
  • The diaspora. Many Jews stayed in Babylonia, the origin of a great centre of Jewish learning. Others settled in Egypt. The Aramaic papyri from Elephantine, a garrison on the Nile (5th century BC), show a Jewish community with its own temple, in correspondence with Jerusalem.2
  • The Samaritans worshipped the same God, held to the Torah, and had their own sanctuary on Mount Gerizim. The tension between them and Judeans forms the background to Gospel stories such as the parable of the Good Samaritan.3

By the end of the Persian period, the Torah (the Pentateuch) was very probably authoritative in Jerusalem. The collection of "Prophets" was taking shape. Other writings, the later "Writings", circulated without a fixed list. How the Hebrew canon was closed is discussed in Vol. II ch. 9.

In depth

How exile reshaped Israel's religion

The Babylonian deportations came in waves: the first in 597 BC, when King Jehoiachin and the Jerusalem elite were taken; the second after the destruction of the city and Temple in 587/586 BC; a smaller one around 582. Most of the population stayed in the land. What changed was the leadership class, which now lived in Babylonia without a temple, a king or a land.

That loss is the setting for much of the Hebrew Bible's final shaping. Historians widely date the editing of the books from Deuteronomy to Kings (the 'Deuteronomistic History') to this period: it reads the catastrophe as the consequence of covenant failure, not of the gods of Babylon being stronger. Prophets such as Ezekiel and the anonymous author of Isaiah 40–55 developed a sharper monotheism ('I am the Lord, and there is no other') and a hope of return.

Cyrus of Persia took Babylon in 539 BC. His policy of allowing deported peoples to restore local cults (known from the Cyrus Cylinder, which does not mention the Jews) fits the biblical account of a return and a rebuilt Temple, dedicated around 515 BC. Under Persian rule Judah (Yehud) was a small province. The work associated with Ezra and Nehemiah in the fifth century made the Torah the public law of the community, tightened rules on intermarriage and rebuilt Jerusalem's walls.

Two further developments mattered for Christianity later. First, a large Jewish population remained outside the land permanently, in Babylonia and Egypt (the Elephantine papyri show a Jewish garrison with its own temple on the Nile c. 400 BC): the was born. Second, the split with the Samaritans, who had their own temple on Mount Gerizim and their own form of the Torah, created the tension that the Gospels presuppose.

Why it matters today

The idea of a people defined by Scripture, synagogue and law rather than by king and Temple, which took shape in exile, is the template for Judaism and for the Church.
