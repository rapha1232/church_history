Judaism in the late Second Temple period (c. 200 BC – AD 70) was diverse. Historians sometimes speak of "Judaisms". What united Jews was worship of the one God, the Temple in Jerusalem, the Torah, and practices such as Sabbath, circumcision and food laws. They disagreed about how to interpret all of these.

The Jews had, for a great while, had three sects of philosophy peculiar to themselves…

Josephus, Jewish Antiquities 18.1.2 (c. AD 93–94). Trans. William Whiston (1737), as online at LacusCurtius/Penelope. Q42 · checked

Josephus, writing for Greek and Roman readers, described the parties as "philosophies":

GroupWhat the sources sayRelevance to the Gospels
PhariseesA lay movement devoted to precise interpretation of the Torah and to "traditions of the elders". They believed in resurrection and angels. Josephus says they were popular with the people. The rabbis who reshaped Judaism after AD 70 stood partly in their tradition.Jesus' most frequent debating partners. The Gospels' hostile portrait reflects later conflicts, and modern scholarship warns against reading it as a fair description of Pharisaism.
SadduceesAssociated with the priestly aristocracy and the Temple. They accepted only the written Torah as binding and rejected resurrection.Challenge Jesus over resurrection (Mark 12:18–27). The high priests involved in his arrest were of this milieu.
EssenesCommunal, ascetic groups described by Josephus, Philo and Pliny the Elder.Not mentioned in the New Testament.
"Fourth philosophy"Josephus' term for those who held that God alone was Israel's ruler and resisted Roman taxation (from AD 6, under Judas the Galilean). Later revolutionary groups (Zealots, Sicarii) are often connected with it.One of Jesus' disciples was "Simon the Zealot". The precise meaning of the label is debated.

The Dead Sea Scrolls

Between 1947 and 1956, about 900 manuscripts were found in caves near Qumran on the Dead Sea. They include the oldest surviving copies of the Hebrew Bible, every book except Esther being represented, and the rules and writings of a separatist community that called itself the Yahad ("the Union"). Most scholars identify that community with the , or a branch of them. A minority dispute both the identification and the link between the caves and the settlement. The Scrolls show that the scriptural text was still fluid. They also show a community expecting the end of the age, using a calendar opposed to the Temple's, and expecting more than one messianic figure (a royal and a priestly ).1

Messianic and apocalyptic hopes

There was no single "Jewish expectation of the Messiah". Texts of the period hope variously for:

  • a Davidic king who would defeat Israel's enemies (Psalms of Solomon 17);
  • a priestly messiah;
  • a prophet like Moses or a returning Elijah;
  • a heavenly "Son of Man" figure (Daniel 7; the Parables of Enoch, whose date is debated);
  • God's direct intervention without any messiah.

literature, such as 1 Enoch, Daniel and later 4 Ezra and 2 Baruch, spoke of revealed secrets, cosmic conflict, judgement and the resurrection of the dead. The early Christians used this language to interpret Jesus.

Synagogue and diaspora

Far more Jews lived outside Judea than in it: in Alexandria, Antioch, Asia Minor, Rome and Babylonia. Their gatherings2 (synagōgai, proseuchai) for reading and interpreting Scripture are attested by inscriptions, such as the Theodotus inscription from pre-70 Jerusalem, and by Philo and Josephus. Some Gentiles were attracted to Jewish worship and ethics without converting. Acts calls them "God-fearers". They were among the first hearers of the Christian mission. Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BC – c. AD 50) interpreted the Torah through Platonic philosophy and spoke of the divine Logos ("Word") as God's agent in creation. His method deeply influenced later Christian theology in Alexandria (Vol. III ch. 6).

Why it matters today

Pharisees, apocalyptic hopes and the Dead Sea Scrolls are the world the New Testament speaks into; reading them changes how Christians understand Jesus’ debates.
