Judea and Galilee in the time of Jesus

c. BC4–363 minPreviously: The Greco-Roman world

On Herod's death (4 BC), Augustus divided his kingdom among three sons.

Starring

On Herod's death (4 BC), Augustus divided his kingdom among three sons.1

  • Archelaus received Judea, Samaria and Idumea. He was deposed in AD 6, and Judea became a Roman province governed by a prefect of equestrian rank.
  • Herod Antipas ruled Galilee and Perea (4 BC – AD 39). This is the "Herod" of Jesus' ministry, who executed John the Baptist.
  • Philip received the north-eastern territories.

Pontius Pilate and the high priests

Pontius Pilate was prefect of Judea from c. AD 26 to 36. A dedicatory inscription found at Caesarea Maritima in 1961 confirms his name and title (praefectus Iudaeae). Philo and Josephus portray him as harsh and provocative.2 The high priest Caiaphas held office c. AD 18–36, an unusually long tenure suggesting close cooperation with Rome. An ossuary inscribed "Joseph son of Caiaphas", found in Jerusalem in 1990, is widely but not universally thought to be his. Under the prefects, the Jerusalem priestly aristocracy ran local affairs. Rome kept the power of execution.

The census of Quirinius: a dating problem

Luke 2:1–2 connects Jesus' birth with a census "when Quirinius was governor of Syria". Josephus dates Quirinius' census of Judea to AD 6, ten years after Herod's death. Matthew and Luke both place the birth under Herod, so before 4 BC. Proposed harmonisations include an earlier governorship of Quirinius or a different translation of Luke's Greek. None has won general acceptance. Most historians conclude that Luke's chronology is confused at this point.3 Jesus' birth is usually placed c. 6–4 BC. The AD/BC era itself was calculated by Dionysius Exiguus in the sixth century and is a few years off.

Galilee

Galilee was a largely Jewish region of villages and two new cities built by Antipas, Sepphoris and Tiberias, which the Gospels never mention Jesus visiting. Archaeology at Nazareth, Capernaum and Magdala (including a first-century synagogue found in 2009) shows observant Jewish village life: stone vessels for ritual purity and immersion pools. Scholars debate the economic situation of Galilean peasants and how far urbanisation strained it.

Key takeaways

  • Jesus lived in a Jewish world under indirect and then direct Roman rule, in which the Temple, the Torah and hope of God's intervention were live issues.
  • The figures in the Passion story (Pilate, Caiaphas, Antipas) are historically attested.
  • Exact dates for Jesus' birth cannot be fixed. The evidence points to c. 6–4 BC.

In depth

Rulers, priests and villages in Jesus' homeland

After Herod's death in 4 BC Augustus divided the kingdom among three sons. Archelaus received Judea and Samaria but was deposed in AD 6, after which Judea was governed by Roman prefects. Herod Antipas ruled Galilee and Perea until AD 39; he is the 'Herod' of Jesus' public life. Philip ruled territories to the north-east.

Pontius Pilate was prefect from 26 to 36. A dedicatory inscription found at Caesarea Maritima in 1961 names him as 'prefect of Judea'. The high priest Caiaphas held office from about 18 to 36, an unusually long tenure that suggests he cooperated closely with Rome. An ossuary inscribed 'Joseph son of Caiaphas', found in Jerusalem in 1990, may belong to his family.

Galilee was a region of farming and fishing villages with two new Hellenistic-style cities built by Antipas, Sepphoris and Tiberias. The Gospels never mention Jesus visiting either. Excavations at Capernaum, Nazareth and Magdala, where a first-century synagogue was found in 2009, have filled in the picture of village life.

Religious life centred on the Jerusalem Temple, its sacrifices and the great pilgrimage festivals, and on the Torah read in synagogues. , , and others disagreed over purity, the afterlife and how to live under foreign rule. Tensions with Rome would explode in the revolt of 66–70.

Why it matters today

Pilgrims still walk Galilee and Jerusalem; knowing the politics and poverty of Jesus’ world keeps his teaching from being read as timeless abstraction.
