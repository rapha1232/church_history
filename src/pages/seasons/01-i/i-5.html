The Greco-Roman world

c. BC31–1003 minPreviously: Second Temple Judaism: groups, scriptures, hopes

Augustus' victory at Actium (31 BC) brought the whole Mediterranean under one ruler.

Starring

Augustus' victory at Actium (31 BC) brought the whole Mediterranean under one ruler. The early Christian mission depended on what followed: the "Roman peace", roads and sea lanes, cities linked by trade, and Greek as a common language (the koinē) from Rome to Syria.1

Religion in the Roman world

  • Civic cult. Each city honoured its gods with sacrifices and festivals, believed to secure the community's well-being. Religion was a matter of practice more than belief. Refusing to take part could appear antisocial and dangerous, which is why Christians were called "atheists" (Vol. III ch. 2).
  • The imperial cult. Honours to Rome and the emperor, living or deified, especially in the eastern provinces. Its titles (saviour, son of god, lord, the "good news" of an emperor's birth) overlap with Christian language. Scholars debate how far early Christian texts deliberately contrasted Christ with the emperor.
  • Voluntary cults and "mysteries". Isis, Mithras, the Eleusinian and Dionysiac rites offered initiation and personal relationship with a deity. Older scholarship (the "History of Religions school") claimed that Christianity borrowed its sacraments from them. Modern scholars generally reject direct borrowing. Most Mithraic evidence, for example, is later than the New Testament.2
  • Toleration with limits. Judaism was generally tolerated as an ancestral religion. New cults could be suppressed if they seemed to threaten public order, as with the Bacchanalia in 186 BC.

Philosophy

Educated people looked to philosophy for answers about God, the soul and the good life. Each school left a mark on Christian thought:

  • Platonism taught a transcendent, unchanging First Principle, an intelligible world above the material one, and the immortality of the soul. "Middle Platonism" (1st c. BC – 2nd c. AD) shaped Philo, Justin, Clement and Origen. It also shaped the vocabulary of the fourth-century debates (Key terms).
  • Stoicism taught a rational order (logos) pervading the cosmos, natural law and the brotherhood of humanity. It influenced Paul's environment and later Christian ethics.
  • Epicureanism taught that the gods are indifferent, and that the soul dies with the body. It was the philosophy the early Christians most opposed.
  • Aristotle's logic and categories became important in theology later, from the fourth century.

How much Christianity was "Hellenised" was a famous question in older scholarship (Adolf von Harnack). It is now usually reframed: Judaism itself was already Hellenised by this period, and Christian writers used Greek philosophy critically, not passively.3

In depth

Roads, cities and gods: the world the first Christians travelled

The Roman Empire of the first century held perhaps 50 to 60 million people around the Mediterranean. Roman roads, sea lanes cleared of pirates and a long internal peace made travel easier than at any time before the modern era. In the eastern half of the empire, common (koine) Greek was spoken in every city. The New Testament was written in it.

Religion was woven into civic life. Each city honoured its gods with sacrifices and festivals, and the cult of the emperor expressed loyalty to Rome. Refusing these rites looked like disloyalty, which is why Christians, who would not take part, were called 'atheists'. Alongside civic cults, 'mystery' religions offered personal initiation and hope: the cults of Isis, of the Great Mother, of Mithras and of the Eleusinian goddesses.

Philosophy offered another kind of guidance. Stoics taught living according to reason and nature; Epicureans sought freedom from fear; Platonists looked to an unchanging divine reality beyond the visible world. Christian writers from Justin onwards borrowed their vocabulary while arguing that Christ fulfilled what the philosophers had sought.

Jews lived in many cities of the , with synagogues, the right to observe the Sabbath and exemption from civic sacrifice. Some Gentiles, the 'God-fearers', attended synagogues without converting. According to Acts, Paul's preaching often began in such synagogues and drew in these sympathisers. Private associations (collegia) were another familiar model: to outsiders, the house churches probably looked like one.

Why it matters today

Christian vocabulary for God, the soul and virtue was shaped by Greek philosophy, and the debate over how much faith should borrow from the surrounding culture continues.
