Origins: what the evidence allows

The biblical narrative runs from the patriarchs (Abraham, Isaac, Jacob), through the exodus from Egypt and the conquest of Canaan, to the united monarchy of Saul, David and Solomon. Historians and archaeologists generally conclude that this narrative was shaped centuries after the periods it describes.1

  • The patriarchs and the exodus cannot be confirmed from sources outside the Bible. No archaeological evidence matches the exodus as narrated, a migration of hundreds of thousands. Many scholars think that memories of smaller groups, perhaps including people who had been in Egypt, were woven into a national epic.
  • Israel as a people is first named outside the Bible on the Merneptah Stele (Egypt, c. 1208 BC). In the twelfth and eleventh centuries BC, small villages spread across the central hill country. Most archaeologists interpret these as the emergence of Israel largely from within Canaanite society, not by conquest from outside.2
  • David and Solomon. The Tel Dan inscription (9th century BC) refers to the "House of David", strong evidence that a dynasty traced itself to David. How large and powerful David's and Solomon's kingdom was is one of the most debated questions in biblical archaeology. "Minimalists" see a modest chiefdom. Others, citing finds such as Khirbet Qeiyafa, argue for a substantial early state.

Two kingdoms

From the ninth century the evidence becomes firmer. There were two kingdoms. In the north was Israel, with its capital at Samaria. Its kings Omri and Ahab appear in Assyrian and Moabite inscriptions; the Mesha Stele, c. 840 BC, refers to Omri. In the south was Judah, ruled from Jerusalem by the house of David. Assyria destroyed Israel in 722/720 BC and deported much of its elite. Judah survived as an Assyrian vassal. Under King Josiah (c. 640–609 BC), a reform centralised worship in the Jerusalem Temple. Many scholars connect this reform with a form of Deuteronomy.3

Religion

Inscriptions and archaeology show that religion in Israel and Judah was more varied than the biblical writers approved of. Inscriptions from Kuntillet 'Ajrud (c. 800 BC) invoke "Yahweh and his Asherah". Most scholars hold that strict monotheism, the belief that only one God exists, emerged gradually. It was articulated most clearly during and after the exile (e.g. Isaiah 40–55). This is a scholarly reconstruction. Traditional Jewish and Christian readings see the biblical faith as revealed from the beginning.

The fall of Jerusalem

Babylon under Nebuchadnezzar II took Jerusalem in 597 BC. After a revolt, it destroyed the city and the Temple Solomon was said to have built, in 587 or 586 BC. The exact year is disputed because ancient chronologies conflict. Babylonian administrative tablets (the "Jehoiachin ration tablets") confirm that Jehoiachin, the exiled king of Judah, received rations in Babylon.4

Why this matters for Christian history

  • Christianity inherited Israel's scriptures, its worship of one God, and its hope in a coming king of David's line ("", the anointed one).
  • The idea of a covenant people, the Temple and its sacrifices, and the prophetic critique of injustice all shaped how the first Christians understood Jesus.

Why it matters today

Jews and Christians still pray the Psalms of the Temple and argue over the land and city at the centre of this story; Jerusalem remains holy to Judaism, Christianity and Islam.
