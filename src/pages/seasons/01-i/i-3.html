Hellenism, the Maccabees and Roman conquest

Starring

Alexander the Great conquered the Persian Empire (334–323 BC). Judea passed first to the Ptolemies of Egypt (to c. 200 BC) and then to the Seleucids of Syria. Greek became the language of government and culture across the eastern Mediterranean. Jews everywhere had to decide how far to adopt Greek ways, and they decided differently.

The Septuagint

In Alexandria, the largest Jewish community outside Judea translated its scriptures into Greek. The Letter of Aristeas tells how seventy-two elders translated the Law for Ptolemy II (283–246 BC). Its details are legendary.1 Scholars agree, however, that the Pentateuch was translated in Alexandria in the third century BC, and the other books over the next two centuries. This Greek Bible, the (LXX), became the Old Testament of the early Church. The New Testament usually quotes it. Its contents, including books not later in the Hebrew canon such as Wisdom, Sirach and 1–2 Maccabees, underlie the difference between Catholic/Orthodox and Protestant Old Testaments (Vol. II ch. 9).

The Maccabean crisis

In 167 BC the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes, amid conflicts between Jerusalem factions, banned Jewish practices and set up a pagan cult in the Temple. A revolt led by the priestly family of Mattathias and his son Judas Maccabeus retook the Temple and rededicated it in 164 BC, the origin of the festival of Hanukkah. The main sources are 1 and 2 Maccabees and Josephus.2 The crisis produced two lasting ideas:

  • Martyrdom, as in the story of the mother and seven sons in 2 Maccabees 7, which later Christians read as a model.
  • Apocalyptic hope. The book of Daniel, which most scholars date in its final form to c. 167–164 BC, promises the resurrection of the dead (Daniel 12:2) and a coming kingdom of God.

Hasmoneans, Romans and Herod

The Maccabees' descendants, the Hasmoneans, ruled an independent kingdom (c. 140–63 BC). They combined the offices of king and high priest, which offended some pious Jews. In 63 BC the Roman general Pompey took Jerusalem. In 37 BC Rome installed Herod ("the Great") as client king. Herod ruled until 4 BC and rebuilt the Temple on a vast scale, as well as Caesarea Maritima, Masada and other fortresses.3 Jesus' birth "in the days of Herod the king" (Matthew 2:1) is therefore dated before 4 BC. In depth

The Septuagint, the Maccabees and the road to Herod

Greek-speaking Jews, above all in Alexandria, needed their scriptures in Greek. The Letter of Aristeas tells how seventy-two elders translated the Law for Ptolemy II (285–246 BC). The story is legendary, but the Torah was probably translated in Egypt in the third century BC, and the other books over the next two centuries. This 'Septuagint' became the Bible of the early Church: most quotations of the Old Testament in the New follow it.

In 167 BC the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes, backed by a Hellenizing party in Jerusalem, banned circumcision and Sabbath observance and set up a pagan altar in the Temple. The revolt led by the priest Mattathias and his son Judas Maccabeus retook the Temple and rededicated it in 164 BC, the origin of Hanukkah. 1 and 2 Maccabees, preserved in the Greek Bible, tell the story and give early evidence of belief in resurrection and in prayer for the dead (2 Maccabees 7 and 12).

The Maccabees' descendants, the Hasmoneans, ruled as high priests and later kings. Their dynasty was weakened by civil war, and in 63 BC the Roman general Pompey took Jerusalem and entered the Temple. Judea became a client state of Rome.

In 40 BC the Roman Senate named Herod, an Idumean, king of Judea. He held Jerusalem from 37 BC until his death in 4 BC. He was an able, ruthless ruler and a great builder: Caesarea Maritima, Masada, the Herodium, and a vast rebuilding of the Temple begun around 20 BC. This is the world into which Jesus was born.

Why it matters today

Hanukkah remembers the Maccabees, and Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include their books; the clash between faith and an imposed culture is a question believers still face.
