The First Vatican Council and the end of the Papal States

c. 1846–19293 minPreviously: Biblical criticism, liberal and conservative theology

Pius IX's pontificate (1846–1878) was the longest verifiable in papal history.

Starring

Pius IX's pontificate (1846–1878) was the longest verifiable in papal history. In it he defined the Immaculate Conception (Ineffabilis Deus, 1854) after consulting the bishops, issued the Syllabus (1864), and lost the Papal States to Italian unification.1 The trend of the period, called "" ("beyond the mountains", i.e. looking to Rome), strengthened papal authority against national churches (Gallicanism) and state control.

The First Vatican Council opened on 8 December 1869. About 700 bishops attended. Its decrees were:

  • Dei Filius (April 1870), on faith and reason;
  • Pastor aeternus (18 July 1870), which defined the pope's universal jurisdiction ("full and supreme power of jurisdiction over the whole Church") and the infallibility of his ex cathedra definitions on faith and morals, "of themselves, and not from the consent of the Church" (short phrases from the standard English translation).

The final vote was 533 to 2, after about 60 members of the minority, who considered a definition "inopportune", left Rome rather than vote against it.2 The minority included leading historians such as Hefele, who later accepted the decree. The Munich church historian Ignaz von Döllinger refused and was excommunicated (1871). The Old Catholic Churches (Union of Utrecht, 1889) emerged from those who rejected the decrees. They joined the Dutch Church of Utrecht, separated from Rome since 1724.3

The Franco-Prussian War forced the council's suspension. Italian troops entered Rome on 20 September 1870, and Pius IX considered himself "prisoner of the Vatican". The Roman Question was settled by the Lateran Treaty (1929), which created Vatican City.4 In Germany, Bismarck's Kulturkampf (1871–78) restricted the Catholic Church. Leo XIII (1878–1903) inaugurated a more engaged papacy. He revived Thomism (Aeterni Patris, 1879), wrote on labour (Rerum novarum, 1891), and declared Anglican orders "absolutely null and utterly void" (Apostolicae curae, 1896).5 For how Orthodox and Protestant Churches view papal infallibility, see IX.6 and the Part III matrix.

In depth

The council, the vote and the Old Catholics

Pius IX opened the First Vatican Council on 8 December 1869 with about 700 bishops. The constitution Dei Filius (April 1870) dealt with faith and reason, affirming that God can be known with certainty by natural reason and condemning rationalism and fideism.

The council then moved, against the wishes of a sizeable minority, to the question of papal and infallibility. Opponents included bishops who thought a definition inopportune, such as Dupanloup of Orléans, and some who rejected it on historical grounds. At a trial vote on 13 July, 451 voted placet, 88 non placet and 62 placet iuxta modum. About 60 of the minority left Rome rather than vote against the pope in public. On 18 July 1870 Pastor aeternus passed 533 to 2 (see the full text of the definition).

The next day the Franco-Prussian War began. French troops protecting Rome were withdrawn; Italian troops entered the city on 20 September 1870 and the Papal States came to an end. The council was suspended and never resumed. Popes regarded themselves as 'prisoners in the Vatican' until the Lateran Treaty with Italy in 1929 created Vatican City.

The church historian Ignaz von Döllinger refused to accept the decree and was excommunicated. Groups in Germany, Switzerland and Austria formed the Old Catholic Churches, which joined the older Church of Utrecht in the Union of Utrecht (1889). Since 1931 they have been in full communion with the Church of England.
