Biblical criticism, liberal and conservative theology

c. 1780–19102 minPreviously: New movements of the nineteenth century

Starring

Historical-critical study of the Bible developed from Spinoza, Richard Simon (a French Catholic priest, 1678) and Enlightenment scholars, and flourished in nineteenth-century German universities. Major landmarks:

  • the documentary hypothesis of the Pentateuch (Julius Wellhausen, 1878);
  • David Friedrich Strauss' Life of Jesus (1835), which treated the Gospel miracles as myth;
  • the "quest of the historical Jesus", surveyed and ended by Albert Schweitzer (1906).1

Responses spanned a spectrum.

  • Liberal Protestantism. Friedrich Schleiermacher (On Religion, 1799; The Christian Faith, 1821–22) grounded religion in the feeling of absolute dependence. Albrecht Ritschl and Adolf von Harnack (What Is Christianity?, 1900) stressed the ethical kingdom of God.2
  • Confessional and conservative responses. Revived Lutheran confessionalism; the Princeton theology of Charles Hodge and B. B. Warfield, with its doctrine of the inerrancy of Scripture; and the later American Fundamentals (1910–15), from which "fundamentalism" takes its name.
  • Catholic responses. Leo XIII's Providentissimus Deus (1893) cautiously addressed biblical study. The "Modernist crisis" followed. Pius X's Pascendi (1907) condemned Modernism, and the Anti-Modernist Oath (1910) was imposed on clergy. Pius XII's Divino afflante Spiritu (1943) later opened Catholic scholarship to historical methods.3
  • Orthodox theology engaged these questions mainly through Russian religious philosophy (Khomiakov, Soloviev) and later the "neo-patristic" movement (XV.6).

In depth

From Reimarus to the Fundamentals

Historical criticism of the Bible grew out of seventeenth-century questions (Spinoza, Richard Simon) about the authorship of the Pentateuch. In Germany, Lessing published fragments by Hermann Samuel Reimarus (1774–1778) arguing that the disciples invented the resurrection. David Friedrich Strauss's Life of Jesus (1835) treated the Gospel miracles as myth and cost him his career. Julius Wellhausen's Prolegomena (1878) gave the classic form of the documentary hypothesis, which saw the Pentateuch as composed from four sources (J, E, D, P), with the priestly law late rather than early.

Liberal Protestant theology responded by locating religion in experience or ethics. Friedrich Schleiermacher defined religion as the feeling of absolute dependence; Albrecht Ritschl and Adolf von Harnack (What is Christianity?, 1900) stressed the kingdom of God as an ethical community and the fatherhood of God, and saw later dogma as a Hellenisation of the Gospel.

Conservative Protestants defended the inspiration and authority of Scripture. At Princeton, Charles Hodge and B. B. Warfield formulated the doctrine of inerrancy in the original manuscripts. The Fundamentals (1910–1915), a series of essays, gave its name to the fundamentalist movement, and the fundamentalist-modernist controversy divided American Presbyterians and Baptists in the 1920s.

In the Catholic Church, scholars such as Alfred Loisy were condemned in the modernist crisis: Pius X's encyclical Pascendi (1907) and the anti-modernist oath (1910) restricted critical scholarship until Pius XII's Divino afflante Spiritu (1943) cautiously accepted historical methods.
