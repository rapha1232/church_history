Starring

Pietism in Lutheran Germany emphasised heartfelt faith, Bible study groups and practical charity. Its key figures were Philipp Jakob Spener (Pia desideria, 1675) and August Hermann Francke at Halle. Count Zinzendorf renewed the Moravian Church at Herrnhut (1727), which sent pioneering missionaries to the Caribbean (1732) and beyond.1

Methodism began among John and Charles Wesley and George Whitefield, Anglican clergy at Oxford. John Wesley's account of a meeting in Aldersgate Street, London, on 24 May 1738:

I felt my heart strangely warmed.

John Wesley, Journal, 24 May 1738. Standard text (public domain); not re-checked against an edition in this session. Q73 · not re-checked

Wesley preached in the open air across Britain and organised converts into societies, classes and a "connexion" of lay preachers. He taught Arminian theology, assurance and "Christian perfection". Charles Wesley wrote about 6,000 hymns. Wesley remained an Anglican priest, but his ordinations for America (1784) led to the Methodist Episcopal Church, organised at the Christmas Conference in Baltimore in December 1784. British Methodism separated from the Church of England after his death (1791).2

The Awakenings. In America the "Great Awakening" (1730s–40s) of Jonathan Edwards and Whitefield was followed by the Second Great Awakening (c. 1790s–1840s), with camp meetings and Charles Finney's "new measures". Baptists and Methodists grew rapidly, especially among the poor, on the frontier and among African Americans. Historians debate whether "Great Awakening" names a coherent event or a later construct (Jon Butler, 1982).3 , characterised by conversion, the Bible, the cross and activism (David Bebbington's quadrilateral), became the dominant Protestant culture of the English-speaking world.4

In depth

Heart religion: Pietism, Methodism and revival

Lutheran began with Philipp Jakob Spener's Pia Desideria (1675), which called for small groups for Bible study (collegia pietatis), a more active laity and a practical, heartfelt faith. August Hermann Francke turned Halle into a centre of education, an orphanage and missions. Count Nikolaus von Zinzendorf received Moravian refugees on his estate, which became Herrnhut (1722); a renewal there in 1727 launched one of the first Protestant missionary movements.

In England, John Wesley, an Anglican priest, was influenced by Moravians, and at Aldersgate in May 1738 'felt his heart strangely warmed'. With his brother Charles, whose hymns spread the movement's theology, and George Whitefield, he preached in the open air to working people. Wesley organised converts into societies, classes and bands, and used lay preachers. He taught an Arminian theology of universal grace and Christian perfection. Methodism separated from the Church of England after his death; in America it became a separate church in 1784.

In the American colonies the Great Awakening of the 1730s–1740s, associated with Jonathan Edwards and Whitefield, split Congregational and Presbyterian churches into supporters and critics of revival. A Second Great Awakening (c. 1790–1840) included camp meetings such as Cane Ridge, Kentucky (1801), and Charles Finney's 'new measures'. It made Methodists and Baptists the largest American denominations and fed reform movements including abolition and temperance.

Revivalism defined evangelical Protestantism: the emphasis on personal conversion, the Bible, the cross and active mission (David Bebbington's four marks of evangelicalism).
