Starring

The "conflict thesis", the idea that science and religion have always been at war, was popularised by J. W. Draper (1874) and A. D. White (1896). Historians of science now generally reject it as a generalisation.1 Medieval and early modern natural philosophy was largely practised by clerics. Many founders of modern science were devout: Kepler, Boyle and Newton, though Newton held anti-Trinitarian views. The Jesuits ran observatories and taught astronomy. Real conflicts did occur, and they need to be described precisely.

Galileo

  • 1543. Copernicus' De revolutionibus proposed a heliocentric system. It attracted little official opposition for decades.
  • 1610. Galileo's telescopic discoveries (the moons of Jupiter, the phases of Venus) supported Copernicanism, though they did not prove it.
  • 1616. Theologians of the Holy Office judged heliocentrism "formally heretical" (a consultants' opinion, not a papal definition). The Congregation of the Index suspended Copernicus' book "until corrected". Cardinal Bellarmine instructed Galileo not to hold or defend the doctrine.
  • 1632–33. Galileo published the Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems, which appeared to ridicule Urban VIII's position. He was tried by the Holy Office, found "vehemently suspect of heresy", made to abjure (22 June 1633), and kept under house arrest until his death (1642). He was not tortured, according to the trial records.
  • Later. Heliocentric books were removed from the Index in 1822 and 1835. In 1992 John Paul II, receiving a commission's report, acknowledged that theologians had erred in the case.

Historians debate the causes. Proposed factors include Counter-Reformation anxieties about biblical interpretation, the state of the scientific evidence (Tycho Brahe's system was a serious alternative), Galileo's personality and his patron's politics, and papal pride.2 Darwin's Origin of Species (1859) produced a range of responses, from accommodation (Asa Gray; Anglican and Catholic writers) to rejection. Organised "creationism" as an anti-evolution movement is largely a twentieth-century, mainly American, development (XV.8).

In depth

Galileo, and the 'conflict' story

Copernicus published his heliocentric system in 1543 with a dedication to Paul III, and for decades it raised little official concern. In 1616 the Congregation of the Index suspended his book 'until corrected' and declared heliocentrism contrary to Scripture, and Cardinal Bellarmine instructed Galileo not to hold or defend it. Bellarmine had written that if a true demonstration were found, Scripture would have to be reinterpreted, but that none had been given.

In 1632 Galileo published the Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems, with permission from the censors. It was read as a defence of Copernicanism and as mocking Urban VIII, formerly his friend, through the character Simplicio. In 1633 the Roman found him 'vehemently suspect of heresy', required him to abjure and sentenced him to house arrest, where he wrote his Two New Sciences. The famous words 'and yet it moves' are first reported more than a century later.

The ban on heliocentric books was eased in 1758 and dropped in 1822–1835. In 1992 John Paul II accepted the conclusions of a commission that the theologians who condemned Galileo had erred.

Historians of science now reject the 'conflict thesis' popularised in the nineteenth century by John Draper and Andrew Dickson White, which presented the history of science and religion as a constant war. Many early modern scientists, such as Kepler, Boyle and Newton, were devout, and the Galileo case involved scientific uncertainty, Counter-Reformation anxieties about private interpretation of Scripture, and personal and political factors.

Why it matters today

The Galileo affair is still the standard example in debates about faith and science; the Church acknowledged its error in 1992.
