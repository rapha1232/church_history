Starring

Christianity and slavery

Christian societies built and sustained the Atlantic slave trade, which forcibly transported about 12.5 million Africans between the 16th and 19th centuries (Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database estimates).1 Christian complicity took many forms:

  • Theologians justified slavery from the Bible (the "curse of Ham"; Pauline instructions to slaves). Nicholas V's bull Dum diversas (1452) authorised the Portuguese to reduce "Saracens and pagans" to perpetual servitude.
  • Churches and religious orders owned slaves. Jesuits in Maryland sold 272 enslaved people in 1838, an act Georgetown University has since acknowledged.
  • Southern US denominations split over slavery: the Southern Baptist Convention was formed in 1845, and Methodists divided in 1844.

Christians were also prominent in abolition:

  • Quakers (Philadelphia Yearly Meeting, 1758–76);
  • evangelical Anglicans such as William Wilberforce and the Clapham Sect, and former slaves such as Olaudah Equiano. Britain abolished the slave trade in 1807 and slavery in its colonies in 1833;
  • American abolitionists, many motivated by revivalism.

Gregory XVI condemned the slave trade in In supremo apostolatus (1839), though Catholic opinion on slavery itself remained divided. Enslaved and free Black Christians built their own churches and theologies of liberation from bondage. Richard Allen founded the African Methodist Episcopal Church in 1816, and the "invisible institution" of slave religion produced the spirituals.

The Protestant missionary movement

William Carey's Enquiry (1792) and the Baptist Missionary Society were followed by the London (1795), Church (1799) and American (1810) missionary societies. Missionaries translated the Bible into hundreds of languages. They founded schools and hospitals, and also sometimes served colonial interests. Historians now stress that African, Asian and Pacific evangelists and translators did most of the work. Examples include Samuel Ajayi Crowther (1809–1891), a freed slave who became the first African Anglican bishop in 1864, and the Liberian prophet William Wadé Harris (c. 1860–1929), whose preaching in Côte d'Ivoire produced tens of thousands of converts. The relationship between mission and empire is debated: collaboration, critique, or both (Brian Stanley; Lamin Sanneh's argument that vernacular translation empowered local cultures).2 The World Missionary Conference at Edinburgh (1910) marked the high point of Western mission confidence and the start of the ecumenical movement (XV.4).

The shift of Christianity's centre of gravity

In 1900 roughly two-thirds or more of Christians lived in Europe and North America. By 2020, according to the Pew Research Center, sub-Saharan Africa was home to the largest share of the world's Christians (31%), ahead of Europe (22%) (XV.8). The nineteenth century laid foundations for this shift through mission, migration and African and Asian initiative.

In depth

Missions, empire and the long fight against slavery

Spanish and Portuguese missions travelled with conquest. Some missionaries defended native peoples: the Dominican Bartolomé de las Casas denounced abuses, and in 1550–51 debated Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda at Valladolid over whether Indians could be justly subjected. Pope Paul III's bull Sublimis Deus (1537) declared that the Indians were rational beings who must not be enslaved.

The Atlantic slave trade carried some 12.5 million Africans across the ocean between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries. Most Christian churches tolerated or defended it, and many church institutions owned slaves. Opposition grew first among Quakers: the Germantown protest of 1688, John Woolman's campaigns, and the Society of Friends' exclusion of slaveholders in the 1770s.

Evangelicals such as William Wilberforce and the 'Clapham Sect' led the British campaign: the slave trade was abolished in 1807 and slavery in most British colonies in 1833. In the United States, Black churches such as the African Methodist Episcopal Church (1816) became centres of resistance; slavery ended there only with the Civil War and the Thirteenth Amendment (1865).

The Protestant missionary movement, launched in earnest by William Carey's journey to India (1793), spread across Africa, Asia and the Pacific in the nineteenth century, often alongside colonial rule. Yet African and Asian Christians were increasingly the main evangelists. Samuel Ajayi Crowther, a freed slave, became the first African Anglican bishop in 1864.

Why it matters today
