Enlightenment, toleration and revolution

c. 1648–19053 minPreviously: Science and the Galileo affair

Arguments for toleration grew from the experience of religious war.

Starring

  • Toleration. After the wars of religion, arguments for toleration came from several directions:
    • the Dutch Republic's practice, and Spinoza;
    • Roger Williams in Rhode Island (1636);
    • Locke's Letter Concerning Toleration (1689), which excluded Catholics and atheists;
    • the English Toleration Act (1689), for Protestant dissenters;
    • Pierre Bayle.
    Many of these arguments were themselves religious.1
  • The Enlightenment was not uniformly anti-Christian. Deists such as Toland and Tindal, and Voltaire ("écrasez l'infâme", directed against intolerant ecclesiastical power), criticised revealed religion. A "religious Enlightenment" (David Sorkin) of rational Christians also existed. Kant confined religion "within the bounds of reason alone" (1793), and Hume's critiques of miracles and design were influential.2
  • The American founding separated church and state at the federal level (First Amendment, 1791). State establishments lasted until 1833, in Massachusetts.
  • The French Revolution nationalised church property (1789). The Civil Constitution of the Clergy (1790) required clergy to take an oath. Pius VI condemned it, splitting the clergy into jurors and non-jurors. Dechristianisation followed in 1793–94, with closed churches, the Cult of Reason and the Supreme Being, and executions of priests, including the Carmelite nuns of Compiègne (1794). Napoleon's Concordat (1801) restored Catholic worship under state control. The 1905 law separated church and state in France (laïcité).3
  • Catholic responses. Gregory XVI's Mirari vos (1832) condemned "indifferentism" and freedom of conscience. Pius IX's Syllabus of Errors (1864) rejected the proposition that the pope should reconcile himself with "progress, liberalism and modern civilisation". Vatican II's Dignitatis humanae (1965) later affirmed religious freedom (XV.5). How these teachings fit together is debated among Catholic theologians (continuity or development).4

In depth

Toleration and the revolutions

Arguments for toleration grew from the experience of religious war. Sebastian Castellio protested against Servetus' execution; the Dutch Republic tolerated dissenters in practice; Roger Williams founded Rhode Island (1636) on liberty of conscience. John Locke's Letter Concerning Toleration (1689) argued that the state's role is limited to civil interests and that belief cannot be compelled, though he excluded atheists and Catholics. England's Toleration Act (1689) allowed Protestant dissenters to worship.

Enlightenment thinkers criticised revealed religion in different degrees. Deists accepted a creator and natural morality but rejected miracles and special revelation. Voltaire campaigned against clerical intolerance ('écrasez l'infâme'); Hume questioned miracles and natural theology; Kant redefined religion within the limits of reason. Many Enlightenment figures, especially in Britain, Germany and America, remained Christians.

The United States separated church and state at the federal level in the First Amendment (1791), though several states kept established churches into the early nineteenth century; Massachusetts was the last, in 1833.

The French Revolution nationalised church property (1789), imposed the Civil Constitution of the Clergy (1790), which split the clergy between jurors and non-jurors, and in 1793–1794 attempted 'dechristianisation', closing churches and promoting the Cult of Reason and the Cult of the Supreme Being. Thousands of priests were killed or deported. Napoleon's Concordat with Pius VII (1801) restored Catholic worship under state control. The experience shaped the nineteenth-century Catholic Church's deep suspicion of liberalism.
