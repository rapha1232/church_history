New movements of the nineteenth century

c. 1800–19103 minPreviously: Missions, colonialism, slavery and abolition

The nineteenth century, especially in the United States, produced several movements that drew on Christianity but redefined it.

Starring

  • Restorationism. The Stone–Campbell movement (Disciples of Christ, Churches of Christ) sought to restore New Testament Christianity beyond denominations.
  • Adventism. William Miller predicted Christ's return in 1843–44. After the "Great Disappointment" of 22 October 1844, one group, led by Ellen G. White, Joseph Bates and James White, adopted the Saturday Sabbath and the doctrine of a heavenly "investigative judgement" beginning in 1844. They organised as the Seventh-day Adventist Church in 1863. It emphasises health reform and education, and holds Ellen White's writings as a continuing (subordinate) prophetic authority.1
  • The Holiness movement and precursors of Pentecostalism. Methodist teaching on entire sanctification as a "second blessing" (Phoebe Palmer), the Keswick Convention (1875), and healing ministries prepared the ground for (XV.3).
  • The Oxford Movement (from 1833) recovered Catholic doctrine and practice within Anglicanism. Its leaders were Keble, Pusey and Newman. Newman became a Catholic in 1845 and wrote the Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine (1845).
  • Movements outside historic Christian orthodoxy. These include the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (1830), the Bible Student movement that became the Jehovah's Witnesses (from the 1870s), and Christian Science (1879). They identify as Christian but reject the Nicene doctrine of the Trinity or add new scriptures, and most historic Churches do not regard them as within Nicene Christianity. This edition records the classification without adjudicating it.2
  • Social Christianity. Christian socialism (F. D. Maurice), the Salvation Army (1865/1878), the Social Gospel in America (Walter Rauschenbusch), and Catholic social teaching beginning with Leo XIII's Rerum novarum (1891), on labour and capital.

In depth

Nineteenth-century new movements

The nineteenth century, especially in the United States, produced several movements that drew on Christianity but redefined it. Joseph Smith published the Book of Mormon in 1830 and founded the Church of Christ, later the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints; after Smith's murder in 1844 most members followed Brigham Young to Utah. The Millerites expected Christ's return in 1844; out of the 'Great Disappointment' came the Seventh-day Adventists (organised 1863), with Ellen G. White as prophetic voice. Charles Taze Russell's Bible Students (from the 1870s) became the Jehovah's Witnesses under that name in 1931. Mary Baker Eddy founded Christian Science (1879).

Other movements stayed within historic Christianity. The Restoration movement of Barton Stone and Alexander Campbell sought to restore New Testament Christianity without creeds, producing the Churches of Christ and the Disciples of Christ. In England the Oxford Movement (from 1833), led by John Keble, John Henry Newman and Edward Pusey, emphasised the Church of England's Catholic heritage; Newman became a Catholic in 1845. The Plymouth Brethren, through John Nelson Darby, developed dispensationalism. William and Catherine Booth founded the Salvation Army (named 1878).

Most Christian churches do not regard the Latter-day Saints, Jehovah's Witnesses or Christian Science as orthodox, chiefly because they reject or redefine the doctrine of the Trinity. The Latter-day Saints and Witnesses have become global movements with millions of members.
