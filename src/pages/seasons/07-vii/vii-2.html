Metropolitans, Nicaea and Serdica

Starring

By the fourth century, bishops were organised in provinces matching Roman civil provinces. Each was headed by the bishop of the provincial capital, the . Nicaea's canons 4–5 confirm this system. Canon 6 recognises that some sees exercised authority beyond a single province:

  • Alexandria over Egypt, Libya and Pentapolis "since the like is customary for the bishop of Rome also" (Q24);
  • Antioch and "other provinces" retaining their privileges;
  • Jerusalem (canon 7) honoured, without prejudice to its metropolitan, Caesarea.1

The canon grounds these powers in "ancient custom", not in apostolic foundation or in civil rank.

The canons of Serdica (c. 343) gave the bishop of Rome a defined appellate role: to order a retrial by neighbouring bishops (Q25). These canons are the earliest conciliar legislation conferring a specific jurisdiction on Rome beyond its own region. They were Western canons from a council the Easterners had boycotted. When Rome cited them as Nicene in the Apiarius affair (418–424), the African bishops checked copies from the East and found no such canon. They wrote to Pope Celestine (c. 424/5) asking him not to receive appeals from Africa.2

In the same period the bishops of Rome began issuing decretals, letters answering questions in the style of imperial rescripts. The first surviving example is Siricius' letter to Himerius of Tarragona (385). Damasus (366–384) and his successors increasingly presented Rome as the "Apostolic See" (sedes apostolica) whose authority derived from Peter.3

In depth

Serdica and the right of appeal to Rome

Nicaea's canon 6 confirmed the established rights of Alexandria over Egypt, Libya and Pentapolis, 'since this is customary for the bishop of Rome also', and of Antioch and other provinces. The canon presents these as regional customs. It does not set out a universal hierarchy, which is why both sides of later debates could cite it.

The Council of Serdica (modern Sofia, 343) was called to settle the case of Athanasius and other deposed Nicene bishops. The Eastern bishops withdrew and held a rival meeting, so the canons came from the Western majority. Canons 3–5 (numbering varies) allowed a bishop deposed by his province to appeal to the bishop of Rome, 'honouring the memory of the apostle Peter', who could order a retrial by neighbouring bishops.

These canons were later circulated in the West as if they were canons of Nicaea. In the case of Apiarius, a presbyter deposed in North Africa who appealed to Rome (418–424), Pope Zosimus cited them as Nicene. The African bishops checked copies from Constantinople and Alexandria, found nothing of the kind, and protested against Roman interference in local discipline.

The episode shows two things at once: Rome was already acting as a court of appeal for the West, and other churches did not regard that role as founded on Nicaea. Serdica's canons were later included in Eastern collections too, but Eastern canonists read them as applying within the Western church.
