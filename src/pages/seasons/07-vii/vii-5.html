Starring

From the mid-sixth century, Byzantine law and theology described the Church's government as resting on five patriarchates: Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch and Jerusalem, in that order. Modern historians call the idea the "".

  • Origins. The order combines Nicaea canon 6 (Rome, Alexandria, Antioch), Constantinople's elevation (381, 451) and Jerusalem's elevation (451). It is set out in Justinian's legislation (Novels 109, 123, 131).1
  • Theology. Theodore the Studite (d. 826) and the council of 869–870 (canon 21) treated the five as jointly responsible for the Church. Theodore, while appealing to the pope against the emperor, described the Church as a "five-headed" body. Peter III of Antioch, writing in 1054, compared the five patriarchs to the five senses of one body.2
  • Limits. The Pentarchy described the imperial Church. It ignored the Church of the East and the of Armenia. After the Arab conquests, three of the five patriarchs lived under Islamic rule. Alexandria and Antioch each had rival anti- patriarchs who commanded the larger communities.

Interpretations. Orthodox ecclesiology sees in the Pentarchy a model of collegial or conciliar government with a of honour, which passed to Constantinople when Rome broke communion. Catholic historians stress that the Pentarchy always placed Rome first and that Byzantine writers often recognised Rome's role as a court of appeal. Most historians see the Pentarchy as an idealised, imperial theory rather than a working constitution.

In depth

The Pentarchy as an idea and as a reality

'Pentarchy' (rule of five) is the idea that the Church is governed by the five patriarchs of Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch and Jerusalem. It was formulated in the sixth century, especially in Justinian's legislation (Novel 131 of 545 ranks Rome first and Constantinople second), and was used again in the iconoclast debates: Theodore the Studite argued that a council lacking the five patriarchs could not be ecumenical.

The ranking had grown step by step. Nicaea (325) recognised the old regional primacies; Constantinople (381) placed the new capital second; Chalcedon (451) gave Jerusalem patriarchal status and, in canon 28, extended Constantinople's jurisdiction, which Rome rejected. The Quinisext Council (692), canon 36, set out the full order of five.

In practice the system was never complete. After 451 the non-Chalcedonian majority in Egypt and much of Syria had its own patriarchs, and the Chalcedonian patriarchs there represented minorities. After the Arab conquests of the 630s–640s the three Eastern patriarchates lived under Muslim rule. The Church of the East, with its own catholicos in Persia, stood outside the scheme altogether.

The theory still mattered. Orthodox writers later used it to argue that the Church is governed collegially and that Rome's primacy was one of honour among equals; Latin writers argued that the other sees derived their authority from Peter. Today the Orthodox world is organised in autocephalous churches, several of them newer patriarchates (Moscow, Serbia, Romania, Bulgaria, Georgia).
