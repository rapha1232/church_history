The rise of Constantinople

Starring

Byzantium was a modest suffragan see of Heraclea in Thrace when Constantine refounded it as his capital (324–330). Within a century its bishop was the most powerful in the East. The steps can be traced:

  1. 381. Constantinople I, canon 3: "prerogative of honour next after the bishop of Rome, because Constantinople is new Rome" (Q12). The justification is political.
  2. c. 381–451. Constantinople's bishops in practice adjudicated disputes and consecrated bishops in Thrace, Asia and Pontus. John Chrysostom, for example, deposed bishops in Asia in 401–402. The "home " (synodos endēmousa) of bishops visiting the capital became a standing tribunal.
  3. 451. Chalcedon, canons 9 and 17, allowed appeals to "the throne of Constantinople" (Q22). Canon 28 confirmed "equal privileges" with Rome and gave Constantinople the right to ordain the metropolitans of the Pontic, Asian and Thracian dioceses (Q21).

Leo's rejection of canon 28

The Roman legates protested canon 28 at Session 16. Leo refused to confirm it. He wrote to Marcian, to Pulcheria and to Anatolius of Constantinople (Letters 104–106, May 452) that it violated the Nicene canons and the rights of Alexandria and Antioch:

Let it be enough for Anatolius that by the aid of your piety and by my favour and approval he has obtained the bishopric of so great a city. Let him not disdain a city which is royal, though he cannot make it an Apostolic See…

Leo I, Letter 104.3, to the emperor Marcian (22 May 452). Trans. Feltoe, NPNF II/12 (1895). Checked. Q23 · checked

The exchange captures two principles of ecclesiastical rank. Rome grounded rank in apostolic foundation, above all Peter's. Constantinople and much Eastern practice grounded it in a city's place in the empire, while acknowledging Rome's precedence. The East continued to apply canon 28, and the Quinisext council re-enacted it in 692 (canon 36).1 On the meaning of "equal privileges", see ch. 6.

“Ecumenical Patriarch”

From the late fifth or early sixth century the bishop of Constantinople was styled "ecumenical " in imperial documents and synodal acts. The title probably meant "patriarch of the imperial capital" rather than "universal bishop". When John IV "the Faster" used it in synodal acts (587/588 and 595), Pope Gregory I protested vigorously (ch. 4).2

Justinian's legislation gave the order of sees legal form. His Novel 131 (545) declared that the pope of Old Rome is "first of all priests" and the archbishop of Constantinople, New Rome, holds second place, following the canons of the councils.3

In depth

New Rome: how Constantinople became a great see

Constantine dedicated his new capital on 11 May 330. Its bishop at first ranked under the of Heraclea, but the city's political weight soon raised him. Canon 3 of the Council of Constantinople (381) gave its bishop 'the prerogative of honour after the bishop of Rome, because it is New Rome'.

Canon 28 of Chalcedon (451) went further. It gave Constantinople jurisdiction over the dioceses of Pontus, Asia and Thrace and 'equal privileges' with Old Rome, again because of its status as imperial city. Pope Leo I rejected the canon, arguing that the rank of sees rested on apostolic foundation, not politics. In practice it was applied in the East.

From the late fifth century the bishops of Constantinople used the title 'ecumenical patriarch'. Gregory the Great protested when John IV 'the Faster' used it around 595, calling it a proud title fit for no bishop. Constantinople understood it as describing the bishop of the imperial capital (the oikoumene), not universal jurisdiction.

Justinian's legislation (Novel 131, 545) ranked the five great sees: Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch and Jerusalem, a scheme later called the . The Council in Trullo (692) repeated canon 28.
