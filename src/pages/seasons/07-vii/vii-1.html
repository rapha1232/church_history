The early Church of Rome (c. 50–260)

Starring

Peter and Paul in Rome

Paul's letter to the Romans (c. 57) shows a Christian community already established in the capital, which he had not founded. Acts ends with Paul under house arrest in Rome. No New Testament text explicitly says that Peter went to Rome. 1 Peter 5:13 sends greetings from "Babylon", widely read as a code name for Rome. Early non-biblical evidence includes:

  • 1 Clement 5 (c. 96): presents Peter and Paul as recent "pillars" who suffered martyrdom, without stating where.
  • Ignatius, Romans 4.3 (c. 110): tells the Romans, "I do not command you as Peter and Paul did".
  • Dionysius of Corinth (c. 170): Peter and Paul taught in Italy and were martyred at the same time.
  • The Roman presbyter Gaius (c. 200): could show the "trophies" (tropaia) of the apostles on the Vatican hill and the Ostian Way. Both Dionysius and Gaius are quoted in Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 2.25.

Excavations under St Peter's (1940s–1950s) found a modest shrine of about 160, matching Gaius' "trophy". Whether bones found nearby are Peter's is not established.

Assessment. Most historians, including many Protestant and secular scholars, accept that Peter was in Rome and died there under Nero (c. 64–67). The claim that Peter was Rome's first bishop and governed it for twenty-five years is a later tradition. It is dogmatically significant for Catholic teaching () but is not documented in first-century sources.1

When did Rome have a single bishop?

1 Clement is sent in the name of "the Church of God which sojourns at Rome", not of a named bishop. Clement's name comes from later tradition. The letter speaks of "bishops and deacons" or "presbyters" in the plural. The Shepherd of Hermas (2nd c.) also refers to "the presbyters who preside over the Church". Many historians (for example Peter Lampe and Allen Brent) conclude that Rome was led by a group of presbyter-bishops until the mid-second century. On this view a single ruling bishop (monepiscopacy) is clearly attested from about the time of Anicetus (c. 155–166). Others argue for an earlier presiding figure within the college. The succession lists given by Irenaeus (c. 180) and Hegesippus show that by the late second century Rome's Church traced its continuity through a single line of names.2

Owing, dear brethren, to the sudden and successive calamitous events which have happened to ourselves, we feel that we have been somewhat tardy in turning our attention to the points respecting which you consulted us…

1 Clement 1.1 (Rome to Corinth, c. 96). Trans. Roberts and Donaldson, ANF 1 (1885). Checked. Q26 · checked

The letter intervenes in a dispute at Corinth, where presbyters had been deposed. Catholic writers have seen in it an early exercise of Roman responsibility for other Churches. Others note that it presents itself as fraternal counsel, not a judicial ruling, and that Corinth had apparently "consulted" Rome.

Ignatius and Irenaeus

Ignatius, who is also called Theophorus, to the Church which has obtained mercy … which also presides in the place of the region of the Romans … and which presides over love…

Ignatius of Antioch, To the Romans, salutation (c. 110). Trans. Roberts and Donaldson, ANF 1 (1885). Checked. Q27 · checked

Ignatius addresses Rome with uniquely elaborate praise and, unusually, names no bishop there. "Presiding in love" (prokathēmenē tēs agapēs) has been read as in charity or as presiding over the "love-feast" (the community of the Churches).

…by indicating that tradition derived from the apostles, of the very great, the very ancient, and universally known Church founded and organized at Rome by the two most glorious apostles, Peter and Paul … For it is a matter of necessity that every Church should agree with this Church, on account of its pre-eminent authority…

Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.3.2 (c. 180). Trans. Roberts and Rambaut, ANF 1 (1885). The original Greek is lost; the Latin reads ad hanc enim ecclesiam propter potentiorem principalitatem necesse est omnem convenire ecclesiam. Some editions read potiorem, as printed in the ANF bracket. English text checked; Latin given from the standard editions. Q28 · checked

This is the most debated sentence in early ecclesiology. Convenire can mean "agree with" or "resort to". Potentior principalitas may mean "more powerful origin" (apostolic foundation) or "superior authority". Irenaeus' argument is that the true apostolic tradition can be found in any apostolic Church through its succession of bishops. Rome is his example because "it would be very tedious, in such a volume as this, to reckon up the successions of all the Churches".

Victor and Stephen

  • Victor (c. 189–199) tried to excommunicate the Churches of Asia, which kept Easter on 14 Nisan (the Quartodecimans). Irenaeus and others rebuked him, and the Asian bishops did not comply. Victor's attempt shows a Roman claim to wide authority. The resistance shows that the claim was not accepted as binding.3
  • Stephen (254–257) clashed with Cyprian of Carthage and Firmilian of Caesarea over rebaptising those baptised by heretics. Firmilian's letter mocks Stephen for boasting of his succession from Peter.4 Cyprian rejected Rome's position:

    Nor ought custom, which had crept in among some, to prevent the truth from prevailing and conquering; for custom without truth is the antiquity of error.

    Cyprian, Ep. 73.9 (ANF numbering; Ep. 74 in modern editions), to Pompey, against Stephen’s letter (256). Trans. Ernest Wallis, ANF 5 (1886). Checked. Q30 · checked
Scholarly debate: Cyprian’s “primacy text”

Cyprian's treatise On the Unity of the Church, chapter 4, survives in two versions. One of them, the "Primacy Text", adds that "primacy is given to Peter" and refers to the "chair of Peter". The nineteenth-century ANF editor (A. C. Coxe) declared the additions "beyond all question spurious".5 The Jesuit scholar Maurice Bévenot argued in the 1930s–60s, and most scholars now accept, that both versions are Cyprian's. On this view he wrote the Primacy Text first and revised it during his quarrel with Stephen. Even in the Primacy Text, Cyprian's "chair of Peter" is a symbol of unity that every bishop shares, not a jurisdiction belonging to Rome alone.

Why it matters today

The Roman Church’s early prestige is the historical starting point for every claim made for and against the papacy.
