Starring

Leo I (440–461)

Leo the Great gave the most developed early statement of Roman . In his sermons on the anniversary of his consecration, Peter speaks and acts in his successor. The bishop of Rome is Peter's "unworthy heir" (indignus heres) and exercises the care of all the Churches. In a letter to his vicar at Thessalonica he described a graded structure culminating in Rome:

…that certain whose appointment is in the greater cities should undertake a fuller responsibility, through whom the care of the universal Church should converge towards Peter's one seat, and nothing anywhere should be separated from its Head.

Leo I, Letter 14.11, to Anastasius of Thessalonica (c. 446). Trans. Feltoe, NPNF II/12 (1895). Checked. Q31 · checked

Leo's claims were real and far-reaching. They were also exercised mainly in the West and in Illyricum. In the East his authority was recognised chiefly when it supported Eastern orthodoxy, as with the Tome, and resisted when it did not, as with canon 28.

Gelasius and the “two powers” (494)

During the Acacian schism, Pope Gelasius I wrote to the emperor Anastasius I. His letter became the foundational Western text on church and state:

Duo quippe sunt, imperator auguste, quibus principaliter mundus hic regitur: auctoritas sacrata pontificum et regalis potestas.

“There are two, august emperor, by which this world is chiefly ruled: the sacred authority of the pontiffs and the royal power.”

Gelasius I, Letter 12 (Famuli vestrae pietatis), to Anastasius I (494). Latin as in the standard edition (Thiel, Epistolae Romanorum Pontificum, 1868). Translation by this edition. The Latin was not checked against the edition during preparation; see register. Q40 · not re-checked

Gelasius distinguished auctoritas (moral authority) from potestas (executive power). He insisted that in sacred matters the emperor must submit to the bishops, "and above all to the bishop of that see which the Most High wished to be pre-eminent". Byzantine political theology, by contrast, developed the ideal of symphōnia (harmony) between priesthood and empire, set out in the preface to Justinian's Novel 6 (535).1

The Formula of Hormisdas (519)

To end the Acacian schism, Constantinople's John II (28 March 519), followed by most Eastern bishops, signed a formula drafted by Pope Hormisdas.2 It asserted that in the Apostolic See "the catholic religion has always been kept undefiled", alluding to Matthew 16:18. Catholic theologians, including at Vatican I, cited it as early Eastern acknowledgement of Roman doctrinal authority. Orthodox historians note that it was signed under imperial pressure, that Patriarch John added a statement of the unity of the sees of Old and New Rome, and that it did not prevent later Eastern resistance.

Gregory the Great (590–604)

Gregory, a former prefect of Rome and papal envoy to Constantinople, administered a Rome devastated by war and plague. He sent the mission to the English (597). His Pastoral Rule and Dialogues shaped medieval Christianity. He styled himself servus servorum Dei, "servant of the servants of God". He protested strongly against the title "ecumenical patriarch". When Eulogius of Alexandria applied a similar title to Gregory himself, he refused it:

Away with words that inflate vanity and wound charity. … For my honour is the honour of the universal Church. My honour is the solid vigour of my brethren.

Gregory I, Register 8.29 (NPNF 8.30), to Eulogius of Alexandria (598). Trans. James Barmby, NPNF II/12 (1895). Checked; the ellipsis omits intervening sentences. Q32 · checked

Orthodox writers cite Gregory's protest as evidence that the early Roman see rejected universal jurisdiction. Catholic writers note that Gregory also exercised appellate authority over Eastern bishops in several cases, wrote that he knew of no bishop not subject to the Apostolic See if a fault were found, and objected to the title because it seemed to deny other bishops their dignity, not because Rome lacked primacy.3

Why it matters today

Papal theory from Damasus to Gregory is the background to Vatican I and to the papacy’s current reforms of synodality.
