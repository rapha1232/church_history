Jesus of Nazareth: what historians can say

c. BC6–304 minPreviously: The sources for Jesus

Historical reconstruction of Jesus uses criteria to weigh the Gospel material.

Starring

Historical reconstruction of Jesus uses criteria to weigh the Gospel material. The best-known criteria are multiple attestation, embarrassment (what the early Church would not have invented), dissimilarity, coherence, and plausibility in a first-century Jewish setting. In recent decades several scholars have criticised these criteria as too mechanical and proposed approaches based on social memory. Three successive "quests" for the historical Jesus have produced very different portraits: an prophet (Albert Schweitzer; E. P. Sanders; Dale Allison), a wisdom teacher and social critic (the Jesus Seminar; J. D. Crossan), a prophet of Israel's restoration (N. T. Wright). There is nevertheless a wide area of agreement.1

Scholarly debate: the date of the crucifixion

All four Gospels place the crucifixion on a Friday at Passover. The Synoptics make the Last Supper a Passover meal (15 Nisan). John places the death on the day of preparation (14 Nisan), when the Passover lambs were slain. Astronomical calculations of the Jewish calendar allow 7 April AD 30 or 3 April AD 33 as the most likely dates, given Pilate's term (26–36) and Caiaphas' (18–36). Scholars are divided.

Christian doctrine affirms more than history can show: that Jesus was the eternal Son of God made flesh, without sin, and that his death was a sacrifice for the world. These doctrinal affirmations () and their development are the subject of Volumes III–VI.

In depth

How historians work on the life of Jesus

Modern scholarship has gone through phases usually called 'quests'. The nineteenth-century lives of Jesus tended to produce a liberal moral teacher; Albert Schweitzer (1906) showed that each author had found his own reflection, and argued instead for an apocalyptic prophet who expected the imminent end. Rudolf Bultmann's school doubted that much could be known. From the 1950s a 'new quest' tried again with stricter tools, and since the 1980s a 'third quest' has placed Jesus firmly inside Second Temple Judaism.

The tools are 'criteria of authenticity': multiple attestation (a saying found in independent sources), embarrassment (material the early Church would not have invented, such as Jesus' baptism by John), dissimilarity, and coherence with what is otherwise known. Many scholars now treat these as rough rules of thumb rather than proofs, and stress the overall pattern of memory over isolated sayings.

Points of broad agreement include: Jesus came from Nazareth in Galilee; he was baptized by John; he called disciples, including an inner group of twelve; he taught about the kingdom of God, often in parables; he had a reputation as a healer and exorcist; he shared meals with people considered outsiders; he caused a disturbance at the Temple; and he was crucified in Jerusalem under Pontius Pilate around AD 30 or 33.

The main disagreements concern how apocalyptic his message was, how he understood his own role (, prophet, Son of Man), and how far the Gospels' sayings reflect his words or later Christian reflection. These are historical questions; whether Jesus was also what Christians confess him to be lies outside what historical method can decide.

Why it matters today

Historians’ portraits of Jesus shape public debate and interfaith dialogue, and challenge believers to connect the Jesus of history with the Christ of faith.
