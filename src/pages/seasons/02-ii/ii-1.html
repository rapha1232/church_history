Christian sources

  • Paul's letters (c. 50–60) are the earliest surviving Christian writings. They say little about Jesus' life, but confirm his Jewish descent, his brother James, the Twelve, the Last Supper, his crucifixion, and early belief in his resurrection (1 Cor. 11:23–26; 15:3–8; Gal. 1:19).
  • The Gospels. Most scholars date Mark c. 65–75, Matthew and Luke c. 80–90, and John c. 90–110.1 All four were anonymous; the titles "according to Matthew" and so on are attested from the second century. The early tradition recorded by Papias (c. 110–130) links Mark to Peter:

    Mark having become the interpreter of Peter, wrote down accurately, though not in order, whatsoever he remembered of the things said or done by Christ.

    Papias, quoted in Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 3.39.15. Trans. A. C. McGiffert, NPNF II/1 (1890). Q46 · checked
    How much weight this tradition bears is debated.2
  • The Synoptic problem. Matthew, Mark and Luke share much material, often word for word. The majority explanation is the "Two-Source Hypothesis": Matthew and Luke independently used Mark and a lost collection of sayings ("Q"). Alternatives include the Farrer hypothesis (no Q; Luke used Matthew) and the Griesbach hypothesis (Mark is last).
  • John differs in chronology (a ministry of two to three years, a Temple action at the start), style and theology. Scholars debate how far it preserves independent historical tradition.
  • Non-canonical gospels, such as the Gospel of Thomas, a sayings collection preserved complete in Coptic and in Greek fragments, are occasionally argued to preserve early material. Most scholars date them later than, or dependent on, the canonical Gospels.

Non-Christian sources

Now there was about this time Jesus, a wise man, if it be lawful to call him a man… [And when] Pilate, at the suggestion of the principal men among us, had condemned him to the cross…

Josephus, Jewish Antiquities 18.3.3 (18.63–64), c. AD 93–94. Trans. Whiston. Segments checked; ellipsis omits disputed lines. Q43 · checked

The "Testimonium Flavianum" survives in all Greek manuscripts in a form that a Jewish writer is unlikely to have written ("he was the Christ"; he "appeared to them alive again the third day"). The majority view is that Josephus wrote a shorter, neutral notice that Christian copyists later expanded. A tenth-century Arabic version quoted by Agapius has been cited in support of this view. A minority regard the whole passage as interpolated.3 A second, almost universally accepted passage mentions "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ, whose name was James" (ch. 6).

Christus, from whom the name had its origin, suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus…

Tacitus, Annals 15.44 (c. AD 116). Trans. A. J. Church and W. J. Brodribb (1876). Checked in segments (Wikisource text). Tacitus calls Pilate “procurator”; his actual title was prefect. Q45 · checked

Pliny the Younger (c. 112) describes Christians in Bithynia meeting before dawn on a fixed day and singing "a hymn to Christ as to a god" (carmenque Christo quasi deo dicere, Ep. 10.96.7). Suetonius (c. 120) reports that Claudius expelled Jews from Rome for disturbances "at the instigation of Chrestus" (impulsore Chresto, Claudius 25.4). This is probably a garbled reference to disputes about Christ (compare Acts 18:2).4

Assessment

Virtually all historians of antiquity, whatever their religious views, agree that Jesus existed. They agree that he was a Jewish teacher from Galilee, baptised by John, with a following, and crucified under Pilate. The "Christ myth" theory, which denies his existence, has very little support among specialists.5

Why it matters today

Every claim about Jesus, from preaching to films to scholarship, depends on how the Gospels and other sources are weighed.
