Starring

Crucifixion was a Roman punishment for slaves, rebels and provincials, intended as public humiliation. The remains of a crucified man named Yehohanan, found in Jerusalem in 1968, with a nail still through the heel bone, show that a crucified Jew could receive burial. Jesus' execution under the charge "King of the Jews" indicates that Pilate treated him as a political threat. The Gospels1 assign varying degrees of responsibility to the Temple leadership. Their emphasis on Jewish guilt, especially Matthew 27:25, was later used to justify Christian hostility to Jews. Modern Catholic teaching explicitly rejects collective Jewish guilt (Nostra aetate 4, 1965), as do most other Churches.

The earliest resurrection testimony

For I delivered unto you first of all that which I also received, how that Christ died for our sins according to the scriptures; and that he was buried, and that he rose again the third day according to the scriptures: and that he was seen of Cephas, then of the twelve: after that, he was seen of above five hundred brethren at once … After that, he was seen of James; then of all the apostles.

Paul, 1 Corinthians 15:3–7 (written c. AD 53–55). King James Version (1611). Standard text. Q61 · not re-checked

Paul calls this a tradition he had "received". Most scholars date the formula to within a few years of the crucifixion, probably the 30s. Historians generally agree on three points: shortly after Jesus' death, several of his followers, and also his brother James and the persecutor Paul, became convinced that they had seen him alive. This conviction, expressed as "God raised him from the dead", was the founding claim of the movement. And the claim, in a Jewish context, implied the bodily raising of a person, not the survival of a soul.2

Beyond this, positions diverge. Questions include:

  • Whether the tomb was found empty. The Gospels, but not Paul explicitly, say it was. Many historians, though not all, think an empty-tomb tradition is early.
  • How to explain the experiences. Explanations range from the Christian belief in an actual resurrection to psychological, visionary or social explanations.

The resurrection as an act of God is a faith claim. Historical method can document the belief and its early date, but cannot confirm or exclude its truth.

In depth

Crucifixion in history and the earliest Easter faith

Crucifixion was a Roman punishment for slaves, bandits and rebels, designed to be public and humiliating. Thousands were crucified in Judea in the first century. The heel bone of a crucified man named Yehohanan, still pierced by an iron nail, was found in a Jerusalem tomb in 1968. It confirms that victims could be nailed and could receive burial.

Jesus' execution under Pilate is among the best-attested facts of ancient history. The Roman historian Tacitus (Annals 15.44, c. 116) says that 'Christus' was executed by the procurator Pontius Pilate in the reign of Tiberius. Josephus also refers to it, in a passage that was later expanded by Christian copyists.

The earliest written account of resurrection belief is the formula Paul 'received' and passed on in 1 Corinthians 15:3–8, written around 54. Many scholars date the formula itself to within a few years of the crucifixion. It lists appearances to Peter, the Twelve, more than five hundred people, James and Paul. The Gospels add the empty tomb and appearances to women, whose testimony carried little legal weight at the time, a detail later writers would hardly have invented.

Historians differ on what can be said. All agree that Jesus' followers sincerely believed they had seen him alive and that this belief launched the movement. Whether the resurrection happened is a question of faith and philosophy that historical method alone cannot settle; this edition presents the evidence and the range of scholarly views.

Why it matters today

Easter is the centre of the Christian year; the resurrection remains the claim on which, as Paul said, the faith stands or falls (1 Corinthians 15:14).
