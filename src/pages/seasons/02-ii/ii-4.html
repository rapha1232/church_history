Jerusalem, Antioch and the Gentile question

Starring

The Jerusalem community

Our main narrative source is Acts, written by the author of Luke, probably c. 80–90, though some scholars date it into the early second century. Where Acts can be compared with Paul's letters, it is broadly accurate on places and people but sometimes differs on details and emphasis. Acts tells of the Spirit's outpouring at Pentecost (Acts 2), a community in Jerusalem that shared goods and attended the Temple, and leadership by the Twelve, with Peter as spokesman. Paul independently names three "pillars" in Jerusalem: James the brother of the Lord, Cephas (Peter) and John (Gal. 2:9). By the 40s James was the leading figure.1

Acts distinguishes "Hebrews" from "", Greek-speaking Jewish believers. The Seven, among them Stephen and Philip, led the Hellenists. Stephen's criticism of the Temple led to his death (Acts 7), and to a persecution that scattered the Hellenists to Samaria, Damascus and Antioch. Paul, a Pharisee from Tarsus, was among the persecutors before his conversion (c. 33–36).

Antioch

At Antioch, the third city of the empire, believers began preaching to non-Jews, and the disciples were first called "Christians" (Acts 11:26). Antioch became the base for missions by Barnabas and Paul.2

The Jerusalem meeting (c. 48–49)

Should Gentile converts be circumcised and keep the whole Torah? Two accounts survive. They agree in substance and differ in details.

Galatians 2:1–10 (Paul, c. 50s)

A private meeting with the pillars. Titus, a Gentile, was not compelled to be circumcised. The pillars recognised Paul's mission to the Gentiles and asked only that he "remember the poor".

Acts 15 (c. 80–90)

A public assembly. Peter and James speak. A decree requires Gentile believers to abstain from food offered to idols, from blood, from strangled animals and from sexual immorality (porneia).

Scholars debate whether these describe the same meeting and whether Paul knew the "apostolic decree". Later Christian tradition saw Acts 15 as the model of a church council (Vol. VI).

The "Antioch incident" (Gal. 2:11–14) shows that the issue was not settled. Peter withdrew from table fellowship with Gentiles under pressure from "men from James", and Paul publicly opposed him. How the dispute ended is unknown.3

Why it matters

  • Accepting Gentiles without circumcision made Christianity a separate, mainly Gentile movement within a few generations.
  • The tension between "Jewish Christianity" and "Gentile Christianity" shaped the New Testament itself.

In depth

Table fellowship, the Law and the first great dispute

The first believers were Jews who kept the Law and worshipped in the Temple. The question was what to require of Gentiles who believed in Jesus: circumcision and the full Law, or something less? Acts describes Peter baptizing the Roman centurion Cornelius (Acts 10) and believers at Antioch preaching to Greeks. It was at Antioch, says Acts 11:26, that the disciples were first called 'Christians'.

Two accounts of the resulting meeting in Jerusalem survive. In Acts 15 James proposes that Gentiles need not be circumcised but should abstain from food offered to idols, from blood, from strangled meat and from sexual immorality (the 'apostolic decree'). In Galatians 2 Paul says the leaders of Jerusalem, James, Cephas and John, recognized his mission and asked only that he remember the poor. Scholars debate how the two accounts relate.

Soon afterwards, at Antioch, Peter stopped eating with Gentile believers when men came 'from James', and Paul opposed him 'to his face' (Galatians 2:11–14). The issue was table fellowship: whether Jewish and Gentile believers could share meals, including the Lord's Supper, without one group compromising its practice.

Paul's answer, that Gentiles are justified by faith in Christ and need not become Jews, shaped Christianity decisively. His collection of money from Gentile churches for the poor in Jerusalem was meant as a sign of unity between the two wings of the movement.

Why it matters today

The first great church argument, over Gentiles and the Law, still informs how Christians relate culture, identity and the gospel, and how they relate to Judaism.
