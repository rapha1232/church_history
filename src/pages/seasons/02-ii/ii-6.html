The deaths of James, Peter and Paul

c. 44–703 minPreviously: Paul: mission and letters

…[he] brought before them the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ, whose name was James, and some others; and when he had formed an accusation against them as breakers of the law, he delivered them to be stoned…

Starring

  • James son of Zebedee was executed by Herod Agrippa I c. 44 (Acts 12:2).
  • James the brother of Jesus was killed in 62, during an interregnum between Roman governors, on the initiative of the high priest Ananus:

    …[he] brought before them the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ, whose name was James, and some others; and when he had formed an accusation against them as breakers of the law, he delivered them to be stoned…

    Josephus, Jewish Antiquities 20.9.1 (20.200). Trans. Whiston. The central phrase was checked; the remainder follows the same translation. Q44 · partly checked
    Hegesippus (2nd century, in Eusebius HE 2.23) gives a later, more legendary account.1
  • Peter and Paul in Rome. The evidence and its assessment are given in Vol. VII ch. 1. The earliest text, 1 Clement 5 (c. 96), commemorates them as martyrs:

    Peter, through unrighteous envy, endured not one or two, but numerous labours and when he had at length suffered martyrdom, departed to the place of glory due to him.

    1 Clement 5.4. ANF 1 (1885). Checked. Q48 · checked
    The tradition that Peter was crucified (upside down, in the later Acts of Peter) and Paul beheaded is later. The crucifixion is perhaps alluded to in John 21:18–19.2
  • Nero's persecution (64). After the great fire of Rome, Nero blamed Christians, whom Tacitus calls "a class hated for their abominations". Many were put to death cruelly in the imperial gardens (Tacitus, Annals 15.44). This is the first documented persecution by the Roman state. It was confined to Rome.3
  • John. Tradition, attested by Irenaeus, places John "the disciple of the Lord" at Ephesus until the reign of Trajan. Whether this John is the son of Zebedee, the author of the Gospel, the author of Revelation, or the "presbyter John" mentioned by Papias was already debated in antiquity (Eusebius, HE 3.39).
  • Other apostles. Traditions associate Thomas with India, Andrew with Scythia and Greece, Mark with Alexandria, and others with Ethiopia, Armenia and Persia. These traditions are mostly late and cannot be verified. They matter as foundation narratives of particular Churches (Vol. V ch. 1; VII.3).

In depth

James, Peter and Paul: what the sources say about their deaths

James the son of Zebedee, one of the Twelve, was beheaded by Herod Agrippa I around 44 (Acts 12:2). He is the first apostle whose death is recorded in the New Testament.

James 'the brother of the Lord', leader of the Jerusalem church, was stoned in 62. The Jewish historian Josephus (Antiquities 20.200) reports that the high priest Ananus took advantage of the gap between two Roman governors to have 'James, the brother of Jesus who was called Christ' condemned. Josephus says fair-minded citizens protested and Ananus was removed. Later Christian tradition (Hegesippus) adds a more legendary account.

Neither Acts nor any New Testament book narrates the deaths of Peter and Paul. The earliest evidence is 1 Clement 5 (c. 96), which speaks of their endurance and martyrdom, and Ignatius of Antioch (c. 110), who mentions both in connection with Rome. Around 200 the Roman presbyter Gaius pointed to their 'trophies' (memorials) on the Vatican hill and the road to Ostia. The tradition that both died under Nero, Peter by crucifixion and Paul by beheading, is widely accepted, though the details are later.

Excavations under St Peter's Basilica in the 1940s found a modest shrine (the aedicula) built around 160 over a poor burial ground. It shows that Roman Christians venerated this spot as Peter's grave by the mid-second century. Whether his bones lie there remains disputed.

Why it matters today

Rome built its claims on the tombs of Peter and Paul; pilgrims still visit them, and the memory of apostolic martyrdom anchors ideas of succession.
