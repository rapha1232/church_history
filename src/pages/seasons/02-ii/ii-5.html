Which letters did Paul write?

  • Undisputed (seven): Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, Philemon.
  • Disputed: 2 Thessalonians, Colossians and Ephesians. Scholars are divided, with Ephesians most often judged pseudonymous.2
  • The Pastorals (1–2 Timothy, Titus) are judged by a majority of critical scholars to be by a later follower. They are defended as Pauline by others, and taught as such in much traditional Christian teaching.
  • Hebrews is anonymous. Its Pauline attribution was doubted in antiquity (Origen, quoted in Eusebius, HE 6.25).

Paul's theology centred on the crucified and risen Christ; justification by faith, not by "works of the law"; the Church as the "body of Christ"; the Spirit; and the expectation of Christ's coming. It became central for every later tradition. It was read in very different ways: by Augustine, by Luther, and by the "New Perspective on Paul" (from E. P. Sanders, 1977), which re-examined what Paul meant by "works of the law".3

In depth

Paul's chronology and the debate over his theology

The firmest anchor for Paul's life is an inscription found at Delphi naming Gallio as proconsul of Achaia, which dates his term to about 51–52. Acts 18 places Paul before Gallio in Corinth, so his first stay there falls around 50–51. Working back with Galatians 1–2 (three years, then fourteen years) puts his conversion in the early to mid 30s; working forward, his arrest in Jerusalem falls in the late 50s and his death in Rome in the 60s.

Seven letters are accepted by almost all scholars as Paul's own: Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians and Philemon. They are the earliest surviving Christian writings, written roughly 50–57. 2 Thessalonians, Colossians and Ephesians are disputed; the Pastoral Epistles (1–2 Timothy, Titus) are widely regarded as later.

Paul's letters were occasional, written to deal with problems, not to set out a system. The central issue in Galatians and Romans is whether Gentile believers must take on circumcision and the Jewish law. The Reformation read his 'justification by faith, not works of the law' as an answer to late medieval anxieties about merit. Since E. P. Sanders (1977) and the 'New Perspective' (J. D. G. Dunn, N. T. Wright), many scholars argue that Judaism was not a religion of earning salvation and that 'works of the law' meant above all the boundary markers separating Jews from Gentiles. Defenders of the older reading reply that Paul's contrast between grace and human achievement is still basic. The debate remains open and has ecumenical consequences, because justification divided Catholics and Protestants (see XI.6).

Why it matters today

Paul’s letters are read in almost every Christian service; the Reformation and today’s debates on grace, the Law and the Church all argue from them.
