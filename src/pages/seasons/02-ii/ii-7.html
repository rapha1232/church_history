War, the Temple and the parting of the ways

Starring

The Jewish revolt against Rome (66–73/74) ended with the destruction of Jerusalem and the Temple by Titus in AD 70. Josephus, who changed sides during the war, is the principal source.1 Later tradition (Eusebius, HE 3.5) says the Jerusalem Christians fled to Pella beyond the Jordan. Some scholars doubt this account.2

Without the Temple, Judaism was reorganised around Torah study and the synagogue, under sages traditionally associated with Yavneh (Jamnia). This became rabbinic Judaism. A second revolt under Simon bar Kosiba ("Bar Kokhba", 132–135) led Hadrian to refound Jerusalem as the pagan colony Aelia Capitolina, which Jews were forbidden to enter.3 The Jerusalem Church thereafter had Gentile bishops (Eusebius, HE 4.6).

How and when did Christianity and Judaism separate?

Older accounts dated a decisive break to c. 85–90, citing a supposed "Council of Jamnia" that fixed the Hebrew canon, and the "Benediction against heretics" (Birkat ha-Minim) in the synagogue prayer, said to target Christians. Both points have been substantially revised.

  • There was no "Council of Jamnia" in the sense of a canonising council. The idea is a nineteenth-century construction.
  • The early form, date and targets of the Birkat ha-Minim are uncertain.
  • Recent scholarship (e.g. Daniel Boyarin, and the volume The Ways that Never Parted, 2003) stresses a long, regionally variable process. Boundaries were drawn by Christian and rabbinic authorities over the second to fourth centuries, and groups of "Jewish Christians" (Ebionites, Nazoraeans) persisted for centuries.4

Christian polemic against Jews developed early: the "Adversus Iudaeos" tradition of the Epistle of Barnabas, Justin's Dialogue with Trypho, and Melito's Peri Pascha, with its charge that Israel killed God. This legacy is treated in Part IV, study H.

In depth

Why the 'parting of the ways' is hard to date

Older accounts gave a neat date for the separation of Church and Synagogue: the supposed 'Council of Jamnia' around 90, which was said to have fixed the Jewish canon and expelled Christians with a curse in the daily prayer (the Birkat ha-minim). Both claims have been heavily revised. There was no council in the formal sense, and the curse against 'heretics' (minim) was not aimed only, or perhaps at first at all, at Christians.

The separation happened at different speeds in different places. The destruction of the Temple in 70 removed a shared centre. Rome's special tax on Jews (the fiscus Judaicus) forced the question of who counted as a Jew; under Nerva (96–98) it was applied more narrowly, which may have helped define Christians as a separate group in Roman eyes. The Bar Kokhba revolt (132–135), whose leader was hailed as , made it impossible for Jesus-believing Jews to join.

On the Christian side, writers such as Ignatius (c. 110) already insisted that 'Christianity' and 'Judaism' were different things, and Melito of Sardis (c. 170) accused 'Israel' of killing God. Yet in the fourth century John Chrysostom was still preaching against Christians who attended synagogue and kept Jewish feasts. That suggests that ordinary believers kept crossing a line their leaders had drawn. Many historians now speak of the 'ways that never parted' cleanly, at least until Christianity gained the backing of the empire.

Why it matters today

The parting of Jews and Christians lies behind centuries of tension and persecution, and behind today’s efforts at reconciliation such as Nostra aetate (1965).
