Worship

Early Christians met in households (Rom. 16:5; 1 Cor. 16:19).1 Their worship included:

  • Baptism "in the name of Jesus" or in the threefold name (Matt. 28:19). The Didache prescribes it:

    …baptize into the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit, in living water. But if thou have not living water, baptize into other water; and if thou canst not in cold, in warm.

    Didache 7.1–2 (late 1st or early 2nd century; date debated). ANF 7 (1886). Checked. Q51 · checked
  • The Lord's Supper / Eucharist, linked to a community meal (1 Cor. 11:17–34). The Didache gives the oldest surviving Eucharistic prayers (Vol. III ch. 8).
  • Meeting on "the first day of the week", the "Lord's day" (1 Cor. 16:2; Rev. 1:10). Jewish believers probably continued to keep the Sabbath.
  • Prayer, Scripture, hymns and prophecy. Possible hymn fragments in Phil. 2:6–11 and Col. 1:15–20 show Christ honoured in worship within two decades of his death. Scholars such as Larry Hurtado and Richard Bauckham argue this "Christ-devotion" was an early development within Jewish monotheism.2

Ministry

The New Testament shows several kinds of leadership, without a single fixed structure.3

  • Paul lists "apostles, prophets, teachers" and gifts of healing and administration (1 Cor. 12:28).
  • Philippians greets "bishops (episkopoi) and deacons (diakonoi)" (Phil. 1:1).
  • Acts and the Pastorals speak of "elders" (presbyteroi), apparently overlapping with episkopoi (Acts 20:17, 28; Titus 1:5–7).
  • Women exercised leadership roles. Phoebe is a diakonos of the church at Cenchreae (Rom. 16:1). Prisca is Paul's co-worker. Junia is "of note among the apostles" (Rom. 16:7). Most scholars now read Junia as a woman's name, though the meaning of the phrase is debated.4

How this plural pattern became a single bishop presiding over presbyters and deacons, the "threefold ministry", is traced in Vol. III ch. 8. The Catholic and Orthodox Churches teach that the episcopate is of apostolic institution, received through succession (). Many Protestant traditions regard church order as more flexible.

In depth

From household gatherings to recognised ministries

The first Christians met in private houses. Paul greets 'the church in their house' of Prisca and Aquila (Romans 16:5), and archaeology has found a house at Dura-Europos in Syria adapted for worship by about 240. Gatherings combined a shared meal, the Lord's Supper (1 Corinthians 11), with prayer, psalms, teaching and, in Corinth, ecstatic gifts such as speaking in tongues and prophecy (1 Corinthians 12–14).

The Didache, a church manual probably from around 100, gives the earliest instructions outside the New Testament. It prescribes baptism in running water if possible, fasting on Wednesdays and Fridays, the Lord's Prayer three times a day, and short eucharistic thanksgivings over the cup and the bread. It also warns about wandering prophets who ask for money.

Leadership developed unevenly. Paul writes to 'the overseers (episkopoi) and deacons' at Philippi (Philippians 1:1). Other communities were led by elders (presbyteroi). Women held recognised roles: Paul commends Phoebe, 'deacon of the church at Cenchreae' (Romans 16:1), and greets Junia as 'prominent among the apostles' (16:7). By the time of Ignatius (c. 110) a single bishop, with presbyters and deacons, led the churches he wrote to.

A Roman governor gives an outside view. Around 112 Pliny the Younger reported to Trajan that Christians met before dawn on a fixed day to sing 'a hymn to Christ as to a god', and later reassembled for an ordinary, harmless meal. He tortured two slave women 'who were called deaconesses' (ministrae) to learn more.

Why it matters today

Baptism, the Eucharist, Sunday worship and the shape of ministry today are each argued for from the practice of the earliest communities.
