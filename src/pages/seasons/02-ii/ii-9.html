The New Testament: writing, transmission and canon

Writing

The 27 books of the New Testament were written between about 50 (1 Thessalonians) and the early second century. 2 Peter is often dated c. 100–130 and is the most widely judged pseudonymous. Other early Christian writings, such as 1 Clement, the Didache, Ignatius and the Shepherd of Hermas, were contemporary with the later New Testament books. Some of them were read in churches.

Formation of the canon

No single council created the Bible. Recognition of a fixed list of authoritative books was a process lasting several centuries.

  1. Early second century. Paul's letters circulated as a collection. 2 Peter 3:16 already refers to them alongside "the other scriptures".
  2. c. 140s. Marcion of Sinope rejected the Jewish scriptures and accepted only an edited Luke and ten Pauline letters. His challenge is often credited with prompting others to define the canon, a claim some scholars now consider overstated.
  3. c. 180. Irenaeus argued that there must be exactly four Gospels:

    It is not possible that the Gospels can be either more or fewer in number than they are. For, since there are four zones of the world in which we live, and four principal winds…

    Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.11.8. ANF 1 (1885). Checked. Q57 · checked
  4. The Muratorian Fragment. A Latin list of New Testament books. It is traditionally dated to late second-century Rome, but some scholars (A. C. Sundberg, G. M. Hahneman) argue for the fourth-century East.1
  5. Early fourth century. Eusebius sorted books into "acknowledged", "disputed" (James, Jude, 2 Peter, 2–3 John) and "spurious", with Revelation placed uncertainly (HE 3.25). On 2 Peter, he reports that it was not considered canonical but "has appeared profitable to many" (HE 3.3.1).
  6. 367. Athanasius' Thirty-Ninth Festal Letter is the first known list of exactly the 27 books now accepted:

    These are fountains of salvation, that they who thirst may be satisfied with the living words they contain. In these alone is proclaimed the doctrine of godliness. Let no man add to these, neither let him take ought from these.

    Athanasius, Festal Letter 39.6 (367). NPNF II/4 (1892). Checked. Q58 · checked
  7. Late fourth and early fifth centuries. Western regional councils (Hippo 393; Carthage 397 and 419) and Roman lists confirmed the 27 books. In the East, Revelation remained disputed for centuries. The Syriac Peshitta has 22 books, omitting 2 Peter, 2–3 John, Jude and Revelation. The Ethiopian Church has a broader canon.

The Council of Nicaea (325) did not discuss the canon (Misconceptions).

The Old Testament canons

Early Christians used the , which included books not in the later rabbinic Hebrew canon: Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, 1–2 Maccabees, and additions to Esther and Daniel. Jerome preferred the Hebrew canon and called the rest "". Augustine and the African councils accepted them. The divergence was formalised in the sixteenth century. The Council of Trent (1546) defined the Catholic list, including the "" books. Protestant Churches adopted the Hebrew canon, with Luther placing the other books in a separate section. Orthodox Churches accept a broader list with some variation between them. The Ethiopian canon is the broadest. Volume XI covers the Reformation debate in full.

Manuscripts and textual variants

The New Testament survives in more than 5,000 Greek manuscripts, along with early translations (Latin, Syriac, Coptic) and quotations by Church Fathers. The earliest are papyrus fragments:

  • P52 (a fragment of John) was traditionally dated c. 125–150. Recent palaeographers allow a later second- or even third-century date.2
  • Substantial papyri such as P46 (Pauline letters) and P66 and P75 (John, Luke) are usually dated c. 200–250.
  • The first complete New Testaments are the fourth-century codices Sinaiticus and Vaticanus.

Most textual variants are minor. A few affect whole passages:

  • The "longer ending" of Mark (16:9–20) is absent from Sinaiticus and Vaticanus.
  • The story of the woman taken in adultery (John 7:53–8:11) is absent from the earliest manuscripts.
  • The Trinitarian "Johannine Comma" (1 John 5:7–8) is a late Latin addition.

Modern critical editions (Nestle-Aland, UBS) reconstruct the earliest attainable text.

Non-canonical writings

Many other gospels, acts and apocalypses circulated. They include the Protevangelium of James (2nd century, the source of the names of Mary's parents), the Gospel of Peter, the Acts of Paul and Thecla, and the texts found at Nag Hammadi in 1945, such as the Gospel of Thomas and the Gospel of Truth. The Gospel of Judas (Codex Tchacos) was published in 2006. Some apocryphal texts shaped later piety and art. Others were associated with groups the emerging mainstream rejected (Vol. III ch. 4).

Why it matters today

Which books count, which manuscripts are reliable and how translations are made still determine the Bible people actually read.
