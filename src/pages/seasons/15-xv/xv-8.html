Where Christians are (2020 data)

According to the Pew Research Center's June 2025 report, based on more than 2,700 censuses and surveys1 :

  • the number of Christians grew from 2.1 billion (2010) to 2.3 billion (2020);
  • their share of the world population fell from about 30.6% to 28.8%, largely because many people "switched" out of religion;
  • sub-Saharan Africa held 31% of the world's Christians, and Europe 22%;
  • sub-Saharan Africa was the only region where the Christian share rose (to 62%);
  • the United States had the largest Christian population of any country.

Source: Pew Research Center, “How the Global Religious Landscape Changed From 2010 to 2020” (June 2025). Only the two figures the source states directly are charted, sub-Saharan Africa (31%) and Europe (22%); the remaining 47% is shown as a single “other regions” bar, since its breakdown was not verified during preparation.

Other projects (e.g. the World Christian Database, Gordon-Conwell's Center for the Study of Global Christianity) use different definitions, such as counting baptised members rather than self-identification. They produce higher totals and should not be mixed with Pew's figures.

Contemporary controversies

  • Clerical sexual abuse. Revelations in many countries include Ireland (Ryan and Murphy reports, 2009), the US (the Boston Globe, 2002; the Pennsylvania grand jury, 2018), Australia (Royal Commission, 2017), France (the CIASE report, 2021), and many Protestant and other bodies, including the Southern Baptist Convention's 2022 report and Church of England reviews. In November 2024 Archbishop Justin Welby resigned over failures in the Smyth case. These revelations have produced reforms (e.g. Vos estis lux mundi, 2019) and a profound loss of trust.2
  • Women's ordination. Most mainline Protestant Churches ordain women. Many Anglican provinces have women bishops (Church of England since 2014/15). The Catholic and Orthodox Churches do not ordain women as priests or bishops; the Catholic Church holds this to be definitive (Ordinatio sacerdotalis, 1994). The question of women deacons is under study in both.3
  • Sexuality and marriage. Divisions have reshaped denominations:
    • Anglican realignment, with GAFCON (2008) and, in 2023, the Global South Fellowship's rejection of the Archbishop of Canterbury's leadership after the Church of England's Prayers of Love and Faith;
    • the United Methodist disaffiliations, in which thousands of US congregations left (2019–2023), the Global Methodist Church was formed (2022), and the 2024 General Conference removed prohibitions on LGBTQ clergy;
    • Catholic debate after the declaration Fiducia supplicans (December 2023), on non-liturgical blessings of couples in irregular situations, which many African bishops declined to implement.
    4
  • Other debates. Bioethics and the beginning and end of life; Christian nationalism; creation and evolution; religious freedom and persecution, with watch lists compiled by groups such as Open Doors and Aid to the Church in Need, whose methods vary.

In depth

Counting Christians and the questions that divide them

Estimates put the world's Christians at about 2.4 to 2.6 billion, roughly a third of humanity. The balance has shifted dramatically: in 1900 most Christians lived in Europe and North America; today most live in Africa, Latin America and Asia. Sub-Saharan Africa now has more Christians than any other region.

Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity, which began with revivals such as Azusa Street in Los Angeles (1906), now counts several hundred million adherents across many denominations, with especially rapid growth in Latin America and Africa. Catholics remain the largest single body, at about 1.3 billion.

In much of Europe, and increasingly in North America, church attendance and affiliation have declined sharply since the 1960s, while immigrant congregations have grown. Questions of sexuality and marriage, the ordination of women, and responses to clerical sexual abuse have divided churches internally, notably the Anglican Communion and the United Methodist Church.

Christians also face persecution in several countries. Watchdog groups and governments differ on figures, but there is broad agreement that pressure on Christian minorities is severe in parts of the Middle East, Africa and Asia.
