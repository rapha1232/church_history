Orthodoxy in the modern world

Starring

  • Diaspora theology. Russian émigrés in Paris (St Sergius Institute, 1925) and America (St Vladimir's Seminary) renewed Orthodox theology. Georges Florovsky's "neo-patristic synthesis", Vladimir Lossky, Alexander Schmemann (liturgy), John Meyendorff, and in Greece John Zizioulas (Being as Communion, 1985) shaped Orthodox thought and ecumenical dialogue. Sergei Bulgakov's sophiology was controversial.1
  • The calendar. Constantinople and Greece adopted the Revised Julian calendar for fixed feasts (1923–24), provoking "Old Calendarist" schisms. Russia, Serbia, Georgia, Jerusalem and Athos keep the Julian calendar. Easter remains on the Julian computation for almost all Orthodox. A common Easter date with Western Christians is under discussion (IX.5).
  • The Holy and Great Council (Crete, June 2016) was decades in preparation. Moscow, Antioch, Bulgaria and Georgia withdrew shortly before it met, so it was attended by ten of the fourteen generally recognised autocephalous Churches.2
  • Ukraine. Constantinople granted a tomos of to the Orthodox Church of Ukraine in January 2019. Moscow had broken communion with Constantinople in October 2018 in anticipation, and several Churches have not recognised the new Church. The dispute over within Orthodoxy (the "Constantinople versus Moscow" question) has deepened since Russia's 2022 invasion.

In depth

Orthodoxy's modern questions

Twentieth-century emigration created Orthodox communities in Western Europe, North America and Australia that do not fit the traditional rule of one bishop per city. Several jurisdictions overlap in the same places, and the question of who has authority over this '' remains disputed between Constantinople and other churches.

Russian émigré theologians in Paris, including Sergei Bulgakov, Vladimir Lossky, Georges Florovsky and Alexander Schmemann (who later moved to New York), introduced Orthodox theology to the West. Florovsky's 'neo-patristic synthesis' and Schmemann's liturgical theology have influenced Catholics and Protestants as well. The Orthodox Church in America received autocephaly from Moscow in 1970, which Constantinople did not recognise.

A Holy and Great Council, planned for decades, met in Crete in June 2016, but the churches of Antioch, Bulgaria, Georgia and Russia did not attend, so its authority is disputed.

In January 2019 Constantinople granted autocephaly to the Orthodox Church of Ukraine. Moscow, which regards Ukraine as its canonical territory, broke communion with Constantinople in 2018. A few churches have recognised the new church; most have not. Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 and Kirill's support for it have deepened the division. The Ukrainian Orthodox Church that had been linked to Moscow declared its independence in May 2022, though the extent of that independence is contested.
