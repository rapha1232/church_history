Vatican II, Catholic social teaching and liberation theology

Starring

John XXIII announced a council in January 1959. The Second Vatican Council met in four sessions from 11 October 1962 to 8 December 1965, with about 2,400 bishops and Protestant and Orthodox observers. Its sixteen documents included four constitutions1 :

  • Sacrosanctum concilium (1963), liturgical reform and the vernacular;
  • Lumen gentium (1964), the Church as the people of God and episcopal collegiality. It says the Church of Christ "subsists in" the Catholic Church, a phrase whose interpretation is debated;
  • Dei verbum (1965), on revelation, Scripture and tradition;
  • Gaudium et spes (1965), on the Church in the modern world.

Its decrees and declarations included:

  • Unitatis redintegratio (1964), on ;
  • Orientalium Ecclesiarum (1964), on the Eastern Catholic Churches;
  • Dignitatis humanae (1965), on religious freedom;
  • Nostra aetate (1965), on non-Christian religions and the Jews.

Implementation brought the Mass of Paul VI (1969/70) and the vernacular. Interpretation has been contested: "hermeneutic of reform in continuity" (Benedict XVI, 2005) versus "rupture" readings, and the traditionalist rejection by Archbishop Lefebvre's Society of St Pius X, whose bishops were excommunicated in 1988 and the excommunications lifted in 2009. Rules2 on the pre-conciliar liturgy were liberalised (2007) and then restricted (Traditionis custodes, 2021).3

Catholic social teaching and liberation theology

From Rerum novarum (1891) through Quadragesimo anno (1931), Pacem in terris (1963), Populorum progressio (1967) and Centesimus annus (1991) to Laudato si' (2015), on the environment, popes developed teaching on the dignity of work, the common good, subsidiarity and solidarity.4 In Latin America, the bishops' conference at Medellín (1968) spoke of structural sin and a "preferential option for the poor". Gustavo Gutiérrez's Theology of Liberation (1971) used social analysis, including Marxist categories. The Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith criticised aspects of it (1984) and affirmed others (1986). Archbishop Óscar Romero was murdered in 1980 and canonised in 2018.5 Pope Francis (2013–2025) emphasised mercy, synodality and the poor. He died on 21 April 2025, and Leo XIV (Robert Prevost) was elected on 8 May 2025.6

In depth

What Vatican II changed

John XXIII announced a council in January 1959. The Second Vatican Council met in four autumn sessions from 1962 to 1965 with about 2,500 bishops, many from Africa, Asia and Latin America, and Protestant and Orthodox observers. Paul VI concluded it.

It produced sixteen documents, of which four constitutions are central. Sacrosanctum concilium (1963) on the liturgy called for 'full, conscious and active participation' and allowed the vernacular; the reformed Roman Missal followed in 1969–1970. Lumen gentium (1964) on the Church described it as the people of God, taught the collegiality of bishops with and under the pope, and said that the Church of Christ 'subsists in' the Catholic Church while elements of sanctification exist outside it. Dei Verbum (1965) on revelation treated Scripture and Tradition as one source flowing from God and encouraged biblical study. Gaudium et spes (1965) addressed the Church in the modern world.

Other decrees transformed relations outside the Church: Unitatis redintegratio on ecumenism, Nostra aetate on non-Christian religions, which repudiated the charge that the Jews were collectively guilty of Christ's death, and Dignitatis humanae on religious freedom, which reversed the nineteenth-century rejection of it.

Interpretation of the council has been contested ever since: a 'hermeneutic of continuity' (Benedict XVI) versus readings that stress a new beginning, and a traditionalist minority, notably Archbishop Marcel Lefebvre's Society of St Pius X, which rejected parts of it.
