Christians in the Middle East

c. 1914–20262 minPreviously: Orthodoxy in the modern world

The twentieth century began with catastrophe.

The twentieth century began with catastrophe. The Armenian genocide, and the Sayfo massacres of Assyrians and Syriac Christians (1914–1924), killed hundreds of thousands, with estimates for Armenians alone commonly 600,000 to 1.5 million.1 The population exchange between Greece and Turkey (1923) ended the Christian presence in much of Anatolia. Since then the Christian share of the population across the region has fallen sharply, through emigration, lower birth rates, conflict and pressure.2

  • Iraq. Christians were often estimated at over a million before 2003. They were targeted in sectarian violence after 2003 and expelled from Mosul and the Nineveh Plain by the Islamic State in 2014. Current estimates are commonly a few hundred thousand; precise figures are uncertain.
  • Syria. The civil war since 2011 brought large-scale displacement. The fall of the Assad government in December 2024 left the future of Syria's Christian communities uncertain, and churches were attacked in 2025.
  • Lebanon. The only state in the region with a Christian head of state. The constitutional balance is under strain, and Christian emigration has increased since the 2019 financial collapse and the 2020 Beirut port explosion.
  • Egypt. Copts are the largest Christian community in the Middle East, perhaps around 10% of Egypt's population. Estimates differ between Church and state sources.3
  • Israel/Palestine. Palestinian Christian communities have declined as a share of the population. Christian holy sites remain governed by the Ottoman-era status quo arrangements.

The Gulf states meanwhile host large migrant Christian populations from Asia and Africa, a reminder that the region's Christianity is changing in composition as well as size.

In depth

Why Middle Eastern Christianity has shrunk

In 1900 Christians made up perhaps a tenth or more of the population of the region from Egypt to Iran. Today the share is far smaller. The decline came from several causes over a century.

Violence: the massacres of Armenians, Assyrians and Greeks in the Ottoman Empire in 1915–1923 (the Armenian Genocide and the Assyrian Sayfo) destroyed communities across eastern Anatolia. In Iraq the Simele massacre of Assyrians (1933) followed independence. After the 2003 invasion of Iraq, Christians were targeted; the Islamic State's capture of Mosul and the Nineveh Plain in 2014 emptied ancient Christian towns. The Syrian civil war from 2011 displaced large numbers.

Emigration: for over a century Christians, often better educated and with links abroad, have emigrated to the Americas, Europe and Australia. Lower birth rates than their Muslim neighbours also reduced their share. Lebanon, where Christians were a majority in 1932, the date of its only census, now probably has a Christian minority.

Survival: Egypt's Copts remain the largest community, probably around ten million, although they face periodic violence and discrimination. Smaller communities persist in Jordan, Israel and Palestine, Syria and Iraq. Migrant workers have created large new Christian populations in the Gulf states. Churches such as the Syriac Orthodox and the Assyrian Church of the East now have more members in the than in their homelands.

Why it matters today

Christians in Iraq, Syria and Egypt face emigration and persecution that threaten communities nearly 2,000 years old.
