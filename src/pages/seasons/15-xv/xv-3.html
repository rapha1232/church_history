Pentecostal, charismatic, evangelical and independent Christianity

Starring

Pentecostalism traces its origins to several centres. At Charles Parham's Bible school in Topeka, Kansas, students spoke in tongues in January 1901. The Azusa Street Revival in Los Angeles (1906–c. 1909) was led by the African American preacher William J. Seymour and was notable for its interracial worship. Parallel revivals took place in Wales (1904–05), India (Mukti, 1905) and Korea (1907).1 Pentecostals teach a "baptism in the Holy Spirit" evidenced, in classical , by speaking in tongues, along with healing and prophecy. They formed denominations such as the Church of God in Christ (Pentecostal from 1907) and the Assemblies of God (1914). A dispute over the Trinity produced "Oneness" Pentecostalism (from 1913–16), which baptises in Jesus' name only and rejects the Trinitarian formulation.2

The charismatic movement brought Pentecostal experience into historic Churches: among Episcopalians (Dennis Bennett, 1960), Catholics (the Catholic Charismatic Renewal, from Duquesne University in 1967) and others. "Neo-charismatic" and independent churches, including many African Initiated Churches such as the Kimbanguist Church (Congo, 1921) and the Aladura churches (Nigeria), and Latin American and Asian megachurches, have made Pentecostal-charismatic Christianity the fastest-growing form of Christianity in the twentieth century.3 Estimates of its size, often cited at over 600 million, depend heavily on how it is defined.4

Evangelicalism was reorganised after the fundamentalist–modernist controversies (1920s). Landmarks include the "new evangelicals", Billy Graham's crusades, the Lausanne Congress (1974) and its Covenant, and global evangelical networks. In the United States, evangelical identity became closely linked with politics from the 1970s. This shift is analysed in an extensive literature. Non-denominational churches have grown rapidly in the US and elsewhere.

In depth

How Pentecostalism became global

Pentecostalism traces itself to revivals around 1900 that taught a 'baptism in the Holy Spirit' after conversion, evidenced by speaking in tongues. Charles Parham's Bible school in Topeka (1901) formulated this teaching. The Azusa Street revival in Los Angeles (1906–1909), led by the African American preacher William J. Seymour, drew Black and white worshippers together and sent missionaries worldwide within months. Parallel revivals occurred in Wales (1904–1905), India (Mukti mission, 1905) and Korea (1907).

Denominations soon formed, among them the Church of God in Christ, the Assemblies of God (1914) and the Pentecostal Assemblies of the World. A dispute over the Trinity produced the 'Oneness' Pentecostals (from 1913).

From the 1960s charismatic renewal brought Pentecostal-style worship into older churches: an Episcopal parish in California in 1960, and Catholic students at Duquesne University in 1967. In the Global South many independent churches developed their own forms, such as the African Initiated Churches (the Kimbanguists in Congo, the Zion Christian Church in South Africa, the Aladura churches in Nigeria) and large neo-Pentecostal churches in Brazil, Nigeria and Korea. The 'prosperity gospel' associated with some of them is criticised by many Pentecostals as well as others.

Estimates of the number of Pentecostals and charismatics vary widely depending on definitions, from around 300 million to over 600 million. It is the fastest-growing form of Christianity of the past century. Evangelicals more broadly organised internationally through the Lausanne Movement (1974).
