The Churches and Nazism

  • Protestants. The pro-Nazi "German Christians" won church elections in 1933. In opposition, the Confessing Church adopted the Barmen Declaration (May 1934), drafted largely by Barth. It rejected the idea that the Church could recognise other sources of revelation besides Christ, but said little explicitly about the Jews. Dietrich Bonhoeffer joined the resistance and was executed on 9 April 1945. Most Protestants, however, accommodated the regime.2
  • Catholics. The Reichskonkordat (July 1933) sought to protect Catholic institutions. Pius XI's encyclical Mit brennender Sorge (1937) condemned Nazi violations and racial idolatry. Clergy and laity resisted in various ways, among them Bishop von Galen's 1941 sermons against euthanasia killings. Thousands of priests were imprisoned (Dachau).3
  • Pius XII (1939–1958). His public reticence about the Holocaust has been fiercely debated since Rolf Hochhuth's play The Deputy (1963). Some see diplomatic prudence and covert rescue efforts; others see moral failure. The Vatican archives of his pontificate opened in March 2020, and research based on them, for example by David Kertzer (2022), continues.4

The Holocaust and Christian antisemitism

Historians broadly agree that centuries of Christian anti-Judaism prepared the ground on which Nazi racial antisemitism grew, though Nazi ideology was itself anti-Christian in important respects. After 1945 Churches undertook major revisions:

  • the Seelisberg Theses (1947);
  • Vatican II's Nostra aetate 4 (1965), which rejected collective Jewish guilt for Christ's death and deplored antisemitism;
  • the Vatican's We Remember: A Reflection on the Shoah (1998);
  • Protestant declarations rejecting the idea that the Church has replaced Israel, such as the Rhineland (1980).

In depth

Churches under Nazism

Most German Protestants welcomed Hitler's appointment in 1933. The 'German Christians' movement sought a Reich Church that would combine Christianity with Nazi racial ideology and exclude Christians of Jewish descent from the ministry. In response, the Pastors' Emergency League (Martin Niemöller) and then the Confessing Church formed. Its Barmen Declaration (May 1934), drafted largely by Karl Barth, rejected the idea that the Church should acknowledge any revelation besides Jesus Christ or submit to any other lord. It said little about the persecution of Jews.

The Catholic Church signed a concordat with the Reich in July 1933, which protected Catholic institutions on paper in exchange for clerical withdrawal from politics. As violations multiplied, Pius XI issued the encyclical Mit brennender Sorge (1937), smuggled into Germany and read from pulpits. Bishop Clemens von Galen of Münster publicly denounced the euthanasia programme in 1941.

Individual Christians resisted. Dietrich Bonhoeffer, a Lutheran theologian involved in the conspiracy against Hitler, was executed at Flossenbürg in April 1945. Others, including the members of the White Rose and many priests in Dachau, were killed. Many Christians across Europe sheltered Jews; others took part in persecution.

The role of Pius XII remains debated: critics argue he should have spoken out clearly against the Holocaust; defenders point to quiet efforts to save Jews, especially in Rome. The Vatican archives for his pontificate were opened in 2020, and research is ongoing. After the war, Christian reckoning with the theological roots of antisemitism led to the Seelisberg conference (1947), Vatican II's Nostra aetate (1965), and many Protestant statements rejecting the idea that the Jews were rejected by God.

Why it matters today

The churches’ responses to fascism and the Holocaust, from complicity to resistance (Bonhoeffer, Kolbe), still shape Christian ethics and Jewish–Christian relations.
