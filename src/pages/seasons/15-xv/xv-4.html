The ecumenical movement

c. 1910–20263 minPreviously: Pentecostal, charismatic, evangelical and independent Christianity

  • 1910. The World Missionary Conference at Edinburgh.
  • 1920. The Ecumenical Patriarchate's encyclical called for a "fellowship of Churches" on the model of the League of Nations.1
  • 1925 and 1927. Life and Work (Stockholm, 1925) and Faith and Order (Lausanne, 1927).
  • 1948. These merged to form the World Council of Churches (Amsterdam). Its Toronto Statement (1950) declared that membership does not imply recognising other members as Churches in the full sense. Orthodox Churches joined, mostly in 1961. The Catholic Church is not a member, but participates in Faith and Order.2
  • Church unions:
    • the Church of South India (1947), uniting Anglican, Methodist and Reformed churches with episcopacy;
    • the United Church of Canada (1925);
    • the Uniting Church in Australia (1977);
    • the Leuenberg Agreement (1973), establishing church fellowship between Lutheran and Reformed churches in Europe;
    • the Porvoo Communion (1996), Anglican and Nordic and Baltic Lutheran churches.3
  • Bilateral dialogues. The Faith and Order convergence text Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry (Lima, 1982). The Joint Declaration on Justification (1999) (XI.6). Catholic–Orthodox and Oriental dialogues (V.5, IX.5, VII.6). ARCIC (Anglican–Roman Catholic) since 1970.4
  • 2025. Commemorations of Nicaea's 1700th anniversary, including at İznik, brought together leaders of many traditions (IX.5).

Assessments differ. Some speak of an "ecumenical winter" since the 1990s, caused by divergence over ethics and ordination. Others point to practical and "receptive" and growing cooperation among Pentecostal and evangelical bodies (the Global Christian Forum, from 1998).

In depth

From Edinburgh 1910 to the Joint Declaration

The modern ecumenical movement is usually dated from the World Missionary Conference at Edinburgh in 1910, where Protestant missionaries concluded that division hindered mission. Two streams followed: Life and Work (Stockholm 1925), on social questions, and Faith and Order (Lausanne 1927), on doctrine. They merged in the World Council of Churches, founded at Amsterdam in 1948. The Orthodox churches joined, many in 1961; the Catholic Church is not a member but takes part in Faith and Order.

Faith and Order's most influential text is Baptism, and Ministry (Lima, 1982), which recorded significant convergence. United churches were formed in several countries: the United Church of Canada (1925), the Church of South India (1947), which united Anglicans, Methodists and Reformed with bishops, and the Church of North India (1970).

Vatican II's decree Unitatis redintegratio (1964) committed the Catholic Church to ecumenism and recognised other Christians as brothers and sisters in Christ through baptism. Bilateral dialogues followed with Anglicans (ARCIC, from 1970), Lutherans, Methodists, Reformed, Orthodox and the Oriental churches.

The best-known result is the Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification signed by the Lutheran World Federation and the Catholic Church in Augsburg on 31 October 1999. It states a 'consensus in basic truths' and that the sixteenth-century condemnations do not apply to the teaching presented in it. The World Methodist Council (2006), the Anglican Communion (2016) and the World Communion of Reformed Churches (2017) later associated themselves with it.
