Christianity under communism

c. 1917–20263 minPreviously: World wars, fascism, Nazism and the Holocaust

Soviet policy toward religion aimed at its eventual elimination.

Starring

  • Soviet Union. The Russian Church restored its patriarchate in 1917 and elected Tikhon. The Bolsheviks then confiscated church property and carried out mass arrests and executions of clergy, especially in 1918–22 and 1937–38. By 1939 only a few hundred parishes remained open. Stalin permitted a revival in 1943 for wartime reasons, under strict state control. Khrushchev's campaign (1959–64) closed thousands of churches again.1 The Russian Church has canonised over a thousand "New Martyrs and Confessors". Their exact number is not settled.2
  • Eastern Europe. Churches faced varied pressures. Albania declared itself an atheist state in 1967. The Greek Catholic Churches of Ukraine and Romania were suppressed (XIII.6). In Poland, the Catholic Church became a pillar of national resistance. Karol Wojtyła was elected pope (John Paul II) in 1978, and Solidarity emerged in 1980.
  • After 1989–91. Massive revival of church life in Russia, Ukraine, Romania and elsewhere. The Cathedral of Christ the Saviour in Moscow, demolished in 1931, was rebuilt and consecrated in 2000. Close relations between the Moscow Patriarchate and the Russian state have been criticised, especially Kirill's support for the war against Ukraine from 2022, which led parts of the Ukrainian Orthodox Church to distance themselves from Moscow.3
  • China. After 1949 missionaries were expelled. The state organised the Protestant Three-Self Patriotic Movement (1954) and the Catholic Patriotic Association (1957). Churches were closed during the Cultural Revolution (1966–76). Christianity then grew rapidly, especially through unregistered "house churches". Estimates of the number of Christians vary greatly with method: Pew's 2025 report estimated roughly 25 million self-identifying Christians in 2020, while other estimates are far higher.4 A Holy See–China provisional agreement on the appointment of bishops was signed in 2018 and renewed in 2020, 2022 and, for four years, 2024.5

In depth

Persecution, survival and revival under communism

Soviet policy toward religion aimed at its eventual elimination. The 1918 decree separated church and state and confiscated church property; the campaign to seize church valuables in 1922 was used to attack the clergy. Patriarch Tikhon was imprisoned. In the late 1930s the Great Terror killed tens of thousands of clergy; by 1939 only a few hundred Orthodox churches were open in the whole country.

In 1943 Stalin, needing support in the war, allowed the election of a patriarch and the reopening of churches and seminaries under strict control. Khrushchev's campaign (1959–1964) closed about half of them again. In Eastern Europe after 1945 regimes imprisoned bishops such as Cardinal Mindszenty in Hungary and Cardinal Stepinac in Yugoslavia, suppressed Greek Catholic churches, and infiltrated church structures with informers. Albania declared itself the world's first atheist state in 1967.

Poland was the exception: the Catholic Church remained a mass institution independent of the state. The election of Karol Wojtyła as John Paul II in 1978 and his visit to Poland in 1979 helped inspire the Solidarity movement. Churches played a part in the 1989 revolutions, notably the peace prayers at St Nicholas Church in Leipzig.

In China the government created state-supervised bodies: the Three-Self Patriotic Movement for Protestants (1954) and the Catholic Patriotic Association (1957). During the Cultural Revolution all public worship stopped. Since 1979 Christianity has grown rapidly, much of it in unregistered 'house churches'. A provisional agreement between the Vatican and China on the appointment of bishops was signed in 2018 and renewed since.

Why it matters today

Churches that survived communism are now growing or struggling in new ways across Eastern Europe, China and Cuba.
