The Council of Trent (1545–1563)

c. 1545–15703 min

The Council of Trent met in three periods (1545–47, 1551–52, 1562–63) under Paul III, Julius III and Pius IV.

The Council of Trent met in three periods (1545–47, 1551–52, 1562–63) under Paul III, Julius III and Pius IV. It met at Trent, an imperial city south of the Alps, as a compromise between the emperor's wish for a German venue and the pope's for an Italian one. Attendance was small at first: about 30 bishops opened it; at the close in December 1563, 255 fathers signed the decrees, about 200 of them bishops.1 Most participants were Italian, with significant Spanish contingents. Protestant envoys attended briefly in 1551–52 but did not participate as equals.2

The council worked in tandem on doctrine and reform.3

The council's work was implemented through:

  • the Profession of Faith (1564);
  • the Roman Catechism (1566);
  • the Breviary (1568) and Missal (1570) of Pius V, which standardised the Roman Rite for four centuries;
  • the Sixto-Clementine Vulgate (1592).

Model reforming bishops such as Charles Borromeo in Milan (1564–84) showed what the decrees could achieve. Implementation was slow and uneven, and depended on rulers. France never formally received the reform decrees.4

In depth

What Trent decided, session by session

The Council of Trent met in three periods: 1545–1547 (moved briefly to Bologna), 1551–1552 and 1562–1563. Attendance was small at first, mostly Italian and Spanish bishops; Protestants who came in 1551–1552 left without any real exchange. The council dealt with doctrine and reform in parallel, at the insistence of Emperor Charles V.

On doctrine it decided: that revelation is contained 'in written books and unwritten traditions', and that the Vulgate is the authentic Latin text for public use (session IV, 1546); on original sin (V); on justification (VI, 1547), its most important decree (see the full text); on the seven sacraments, baptism and confirmation (VII); the and (XIII, 1551); penance and anointing (XIV); communion under one kind (XXI); the sacrifice of the Mass (XXII, 1562); orders (XXIII); marriage (XXIV, 1563); and purgatory, saints, images and indulgences (XXV).

On reform it required bishops to reside in their dioceses, banned holding several bishoprics, ordered regular diocesan synods and visitations, required preaching, and directed every diocese to set up a seminary for training priests (session XXIII, canon 18). The decree Tametsi (XXIV) made marriage valid only before a priest and witnesses, ending clandestine marriages.

The council left some tasks to the pope, which produced the Roman Catechism (1566), the reformed Breviary (1568) and Missal (1570), and the Sixto-Clementine Vulgate (1592). The 'Tridentine' Mass of 1570 remained the standard Roman liturgy until 1969–1970.

Why it matters today

Trent shaped Catholic worship and teaching until Vatican II, and many of its decrees remain in force.
