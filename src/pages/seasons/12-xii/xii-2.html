The Society of Jesus

Ignatius of Loyola (1491–1556), a Basque soldier converted while convalescing (1521), wrote the Spiritual Exercises and gathered companions in Paris. Paul III approved the Society in 1540. The Jesuits took a special vow of obedience to the pope concerning missions. They became famous for education: hundreds of colleges by 1600, under a common plan, the Ratio studiorum (1599). They were also active as missionaries and confessors to rulers, and as polemicists (Robert Bellarmine).2 Their success brought enemies. The order was expelled from Portugal (1759), France (1764) and Spain (1767), suppressed by Clement XIV (1773), and restored in 1814.3

Mystics and saints

  • Teresa of Ávila (1515–1582) reformed the Carmelites (the Discalced, from 1562) and wrote The Interior Castle. She was proclaimed a Doctor of the Church in 1970.
  • John of the Cross (1542–1591), her collaborator, wrote The Dark Night and The Spiritual Canticle. He was imprisoned by his own order in 1577–78.
  • Francis de Sales (1567–1622) wrote the Introduction to the Devout Life, addressed to lay people. Vincent de Paul (d. 1660) and Louise de Marillac founded the Daughters of Charity, an unenclosed women's community serving the poor. Mary Ward's attempts to found an active women's order modelled on the Jesuits were suppressed in 1631.

Baroque art and architecture expressed the period's spirituality and its affirmation of the senses, of images and of sacraments: Bernini at St Peter's, Caravaggio, Rubens, and the churches of Spanish America. The church of the Gesù in Rome (consecrated 1584) became a model.4

In depth

The new orders and the mystics

Ignatius of Loyola (1491–1556), a Basque soldier converted while recovering from a wound in 1521, wrote the Spiritual Exercises, a four-week programme of meditation aimed at discerning God's will. With companions from the University of Paris, including Francis Xavier and Peter Faber, he founded the Society of Jesus, approved by Paul III in 1540. Jesuits took a fourth vow of obedience to the pope regarding missions, ran hundreds of colleges by 1600, and became prominent as confessors, theologians and missionaries.

Other new orders reflected the same mix of personal holiness and pastoral activity: the Theatines (1524), the Capuchins (1528, a reform of the Franciscans), the Barnabites, the Ursulines (1535, founded by Angela Merici for the education of girls) and Philip Neri's Oratory in Rome (recognised 1575).

In Spain, Teresa of Ávila (1515–1582) reformed the Carmelite order and described the stages of prayer in The Interior Castle (1577). John of the Cross (1542–1591), her collaborator, wrote poems and commentaries on the 'dark night' of the soul. Both were investigated by the at times; both were later declared Doctors of the Church.

In France, Francis de Sales (Introduction to the Devout Life, 1609) and Vincent de Paul, who founded the Vincentians and with Louise de Marillac the Daughters of Charity (1633), carried this spirituality to lay people and the poor.
