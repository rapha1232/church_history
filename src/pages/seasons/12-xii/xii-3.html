Inquisition, Index, wars and Westphalia

c. 1542–16483 minPreviously: Jesuits, mystics and the new orders

Paul III reorganised the Roman Inquisition in 1542 as a congregation of cardinals; Paul IV issued the first Roman Index of Prohibited Books in 1559.

Starring

  • The Roman Inquisition. Paul III's bull Licet ab initio (1542) set up the Congregation of the Holy Office (Roman ), aimed mainly at Protestantism in Italy. Its records survive in the archives of the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith, opened to scholars in 1998. Notable cases include Giordano Bruno, burned in 1600 for theological heresies including denial of the Trinity, and Galileo (1633; see XIV.1).1
  • The Index of Prohibited Books. First issued in 1559 (Paul IV), revised after Trent (1564), and abolished in 1966. It shaped Catholic intellectual life and book production, though historians debate how effective it was.2
  • Wars of religion:
    • the French Wars of Religion (1562–1598);
    • the Dutch Revolt against Spain (from 1568);
    • the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648), which began with the Bohemian revolt and the Defenestration of Prague, and drew in Sweden and Catholic France on the Protestant side;
    • Ireland and the British civil wars.
    Historians debate how "religious" these wars were. Confessional loyalty was intertwined with dynastic and state interests.3 The Thirty Years' War (1618–48) killed perhaps a fifth of the Empire's population, around five million people, with far heavier losses in the worst-affected regions.4
  • The Peace of Westphalia (1648). Recognised Calvinism alongside Lutheranism and Catholicism in the Empire, fixed confessional boundaries at their 1624 state, and gave some protection to private worship by minorities. Pope Innocent X protested against its religious clauses (Zelo domus Dei), to no effect. Westphalia is often taken as the start of an order of sovereign states in which religion was largely a matter of state policy. That reading is itself debated.5

In depth

Religious war and the road to Westphalia

Paul III reorganised the Roman Inquisition in 1542 as a congregation of cardinals; Paul IV issued the first Roman Index of Prohibited Books in 1559. In Spain the royal Inquisition, founded in 1478 chiefly against converted Jews suspected of secret Judaism, turned also against Protestants and later against Moriscos.

France fought eight wars of religion between 1562 and 1598. The St Bartholomew's Day massacre (August 1572) began in Paris and spread to provincial cities, killing several thousand Huguenots. Henry IV, a Protestant who converted to Catholicism, issued the Edict of Nantes (1598) granting limited toleration. Louis XIV revoked it in 1685, and around 200,000 Huguenots fled abroad.

In the Empire, the Peace of Augsburg (1555) allowed each prince to choose between Catholicism and Lutheranism for his territory, a principle later summed up as cuius regio, eius religio. It excluded the Reformed. The Thirty Years War (1618–1648) began with a Bohemian Protestant revolt and became a European conflict, in which Catholic France supported Protestant Sweden against the Catholic Habsburgs. Parts of Germany lost a third or more of their population.

The Peace of Westphalia (1648) extended recognition to the Reformed, fixed the confessional map by a 'normal year' (1624), and gave some protection to religious minorities. Innocent X condemned it. Historians often treat 1648 as the moment when religion stopped being the main driver of European wars and the sovereign state became the basic unit of politics.
