In depth

Trent and the Reformation confessions, point by point

Authority. Trent's fourth session (1546) received Scripture and the unwritten apostolic traditions 'with equal affection of piety', and listed a canon including the books Protestants call . The Reformation confessions made Scripture the sole infallible rule () and generally limited the Old Testament canon to the Hebrew books, while often keeping the Apocrypha for edification.

Justification. Trent's decree (1547) taught that justification is the renewal of the inner person by grace, received in baptism, with which the believer freely cooperates; faith is its beginning, and good works merit an increase of grace. The Augsburg Confession (1530, art. 4) and later confessions taught that sinners are justified freely for Christ's sake through faith alone, righteousness being imputed, with good works following as fruit.

Sacraments and the Mass. Trent affirmed seven sacraments, (1551) and the Mass as a true propitiatory sacrifice (1562). Lutherans kept two or three sacraments and Christ's real presence without transubstantiation; Reformed confessions spoke of a spiritual presence; all rejected the Mass as a sacrifice for the living and the dead, along with purgatory and the invocation of saints.

Later dialogue changed the picture on some points. In 1999 the Lutheran World Federation and the Catholic Church signed the Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification, stating that the mutual condemnations of the sixteenth century do not apply to the teaching each now presents. Methodists, Reformed and Anglicans later associated themselves with it.
