Global Catholic missions

Starring

Iberian expansion carried Catholicism worldwide under the padroado (Portuguese) and patronato (Spanish) systems of royal patronage over the Church.1 The missions spanned several regions:

  • The Americas. Conquest and evangelisation were bound together, with devastating consequences for Indigenous peoples. Most deaths came from epidemic disease, but violence, enslavement and forced labour (the encomienda) also took their toll. Dominicans such as Antonio de Montesinos (1511) and Bartolomé de las Casas denounced abuses. Paul III's Sublimis Deus (1537) affirmed the Indians' rationality and right to liberty and property. At the Valladolid debate (1550–51), Las Casas argued against Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda's justification of conquest. Devotions such as Our Lady of Guadalupe (the apparition tradition is dated to 1531; documentation of it before the mid-17th century is debated) became central to Mexican Catholicism.2
  • Asia. Francis Xavier worked in India, Southeast Asia and Japan (d. 1552). In Japan there were perhaps 300,000 Christians by about 1600. Persecution and martyrdom followed under the Tokugawa (from 1614), leaving the "hidden Christians" (kakure kirishitan). Matteo Ricci reached Beijing (1601) and adapted Christianity to Confucian culture. The Chinese Rites controversy, over whether converts could honour ancestors and Confucius, ended with papal prohibitions (1715; 1742) that crippled the mission. They were relaxed in 1939. In India, Roberto de Nobili adopted Brahmin customs in Madurai. Portuguese latinisation of the Saint Thomas Christians culminated in the of Diamper (1599) (XIII.7).
  • Africa. The Kingdom of Kongo, whose king Afonso I reigned c. 1509–1542, adopted Catholicism through its own elite and corresponded with Rome. Its Church was entangled with the Portuguese slave trade.3

In 1622 the papacy created the Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith (Propaganda Fide) to direct missions independently of the Iberian crowns. Its 1659 instructions warned missionaries not to impose European customs.4 Christianity and slavery are treated in XIV.4.

In depth

Catholic missions: methods and controversies

Spanish and Portuguese missions worked under royal patronage (patronato and padroado), by which the crown paid for missions and nominated bishops in return for control. Missionaries arrived with conquest, and the question of how the native peoples should be treated was argued inside the Church. Bartolomé de las Casas, a former colonist turned Dominican, denounced the violence; the Valladolid debate (1550–1551) between him and Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda over whether war against the Indians was just ended without a formal verdict. Paul III's bull Sublimis Deus (1537) declared that the Indians were rational beings who must not be enslaved.

In Asia, the Jesuits developed 'accommodation'. Matteo Ricci (in China from 1583, Beijing 1601) adopted the dress and learning of a Confucian scholar and allowed converts to continue honouring Confucius and their ancestors, which he judged civil rather than religious acts. Roberto de Nobili lived as a Brahmin ascetic in Madurai. Opponents, especially Dominicans and Franciscans, argued that the rites were idolatry. Rome condemned them (1704, 1715, and definitively in 1742); the Kangxi emperor responded by restricting missions. Pius XII relaxed the ban in 1939.

Japan saw rapid growth after Francis Xavier's arrival in 1549 and then fierce persecution: the 26 martyrs of Nagasaki in 1597, a total ban in 1614, and 'hidden Christians' who survived without priests until the 1860s.

In 1622 Gregory XV founded the Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith (Propaganda Fide) to bring missions under papal rather than royal direction and to train local clergy. The Jesuit 'reductions' in Paraguay organised Guaraní communities until the Jesuits' expulsion in 1767.

Why it matters today

Most Catholics today live in Latin America, Africa and Asia, fruit of missions begun in this era, with their mixed legacy.
