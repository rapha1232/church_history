The Churches of Antioch and the Maronite tradition

Starring

The apostolic see of Antioch (VII.3) today has five patriarchs who each claim its succession:

  • the Syriac Orthodox (Damascus; Oriental Orthodox);
  • the Greek Orthodox Patriarch of Antioch (Damascus; Eastern Orthodox; largely Arabic-speaking, historically the "Melkites");
  • the Maronite Patriarch (Bkerke, Lebanon; Catholic);
  • the Melkite Greek Catholic Patriarch (Damascus; Catholic, Byzantine rite);
  • the Syriac Catholic Patriarch (Beirut; Catholic, West Syriac rite).

This multiplicity is the product of the divisions after Chalcedon (V.2), the Arab conquests, crusader-era contacts, and the Catholic unions of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

Maronite origins

The Church takes its name from Maron, a Syrian hermit who died before c. 423. Theodoret describes him (Religious History 16). A monastery bearing his name, Beth Maron, existed in the province of Syria Secunda near Apamea in the 5th–6th centuries. Its monks were prominent defenders of Chalcedon. A letter of Syrian archimandrites to Pope Hormisdas (517), complaining that anti-Chalcedonians had killed 350 monks, is preserved in the acts of a council of 536 and is cited in Maronite tradition.1 Between the seventh and tenth centuries the community, under pressure from war and from other Christian groups, became concentrated in the mountains of Lebanon. It emerged under its own patriarch, separate from the Byzantine-rite patriarchate.

John Maron (d. c. 707) is venerated as the first Maronite patriarch. Little reliable contemporary evidence about him survives, and his historicity and dates are debated by historians.2

The question of Monothelitism and "perpetual communion"

Two narratives meet here, and this edition sets them side by side.

Maronite tradition

The Maronites have always been orthodox and in communion with Rome. This position was developed systematically by Patriarch Stephen Douaihy (1670–1704) and earlier Maronite scholars in Rome. Charges of heresy are said to come from hostile sources, or from confusion with other groups. The Maronite Church officially teaches this "perpetual communion".

Other historical sources

William of Tyre (d. 1186), History 22.8, reports that the Maronites had followed the "error of Maron" (the doctrine of one will) for some 500 years, until about 1182, when they abjured it before the Latin patriarch of Antioch. Some Byzantine, Melkite and Syriac sources also associate them with . Medieval texts such as the 13th-century letter of Innocent III to the Maronites (1215) mention correcting their practices.

Modern scholarship offers several explanations. One is that the Maronites accepted the Ekthesis-era compromise, the formula of "one will" (VI.6), which had imperial and papal support in the 630s (including from Honorius), and simply did not receive the 681 condemnation, cut off as they were by the Arab conquest. If so, they were "Monothelite" in the sense of preserving a formerly official position. Others, including Maronite scholars, argue that the medieval accusations rest on polemical sources and on confusion between different Syrian communities. Most historians accept that a formal of Roman doctrine and Latin contacts took place in the crusader period, whatever the earlier situation.3

Medieval and modern history

  • 1215. Patriarch Jeremias al-Amshiti attended the Fourth Lateran Council.4
  • 1584. The Maronite College in Rome was founded. It produced scholars such as the Assemani family, who catalogued Syriac manuscripts for the Vatican.
  • 1736. The of Mount Lebanon organised the Church on Tridentine lines. It introduced some latinisation, such as the use of unleavened bread, while preserving the Syriac-Antiochene liturgy.5
  • Monasticism and places. The Qadisha Valley, the Holy Valley (a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1998), was the refuge of patriarchs and hermits. The patriarchal seat moved to Bkerke, used from the 18th century and the principal see since the 19th.
  • Politics. The Maronites were central to the emergence of Mount Lebanon and, after the 1860 conflict with the Druze and the French Mandate, of the Lebanese state. Under the unwritten National Pact (1943), the president of Lebanon is a Maronite. Large diasporas exist in the Americas, Australia and Africa.
  • Saints. Charbel Makhlouf (d. 1898, canonised 1977), Rafqa (canonised 2001) and Nimatullah Kassab al-Hardini (canonised 2004).

The other Antiochian Churches

  • Greek Orthodox (Rum) Patriarchate of Antioch. , Byzantine-rite since about the 12th–13th centuries (earlier using the Syriac-Antiochene rite). Arab patriarchs since 1899. Seat in Damascus since the 14th century.
  • Melkite Greek Catholic Church. Formed after the contested election of 1724, when the pro-Catholic Cyril VI Tanas was elected and a rival, Sylvester, was appointed from Constantinople. Notable at Vatican II (Patriarch Maximos IV) for defending Eastern traditions.
  • Syriac Catholic Church. A Catholic patriarch first in 1662. Continuous since 1782 (Michael Jarweh).
  • Syriac Orthodox Church. See V.2. Its heartland in Tur Abdin, south-east Turkey, suffered the Sayfo ("sword") massacres of 1914–1924, alongside Assyrians and Armenians.
