Eastern Catholic Churches and their relations

Starring

The Catholic Church today includes 23 Eastern Catholic Churches sui iuris. They are governed by the Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches (1990), and several are led by patriarchs or major archbishops.1 They arose in different ways:

  • Some never had a separate non-Catholic counterpart: the Maronites, on their own account (ch. 5), and the Italo-Albanians.
  • Most arose from unions with parts of Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox or Church of the East communities:
    • the Union of Brest (1595–96)2, the Ruthenians, today the Ukrainian and Belarusian Greek Catholics;
    • Uzhhorod (1646);
    • Alba Iulia (1698–1700), Romanians;
    • the Melkites (1724);
    • Armenian Catholics (1742);
    • Chaldeans (1553/1830);
    • Syriac Catholics;
    • Coptic Catholics (1824/1895);
    • the Ethiopian and Eritrean Catholic Churches;
    • the Syro-Malabar and Syro-Malankara Churches (1930).

Their history includes heavy latinisation, resisted by figures such as Andrey Sheptytsky in Ukraine. Vatican II's decree Orientalium Ecclesiarum (1964) affirmed their right to their own traditions. Under communism, the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church was forcibly absorbed into the Russian Orthodox Church at the Lviv pseudo- (1946) and survived underground until legalised (1989). The Romanian Greek Catholic Church was suppressed in 1948. Orthodox Churches have long regarded such unions ("") as an obstacle to dialogue. The 1993 Balamand document rejected uniatism as a method of seeking unity while recognising the existing Eastern Catholic Churches' right to exist and to pastoral care of their faithful (IX.4).3

In depth

How the Eastern Catholic Churches came about

Eastern Catholic Churches are communities that keep an Eastern liturgy, canon law and spiritual tradition while in full communion with Rome. There are now 23 of them (sui iuris). Most arose from unions with groups of Orthodox or Oriental Christians from the sixteenth century onward; the Maronites (XIII.5) and the Italo-Albanians claim never to have broken communion.

Key unions include: the Chaldean Catholic Church, from a group of the Church of the East that elected a in union with Rome in 1552 (the line became stable in 1830); the Union of Brest (1595–1596), in which most Orthodox bishops of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth accepted Rome, creating the Ruthenian (now Ukrainian Greek Catholic) Church; the Union of Uzhhorod (1646); the Romanian Greek Catholics (1698–1700); the Greek Catholics after a disputed patriarchal election in Antioch in 1724; the Armenian Catholics (1742); the Syriac Catholics (1783); and the Syro-Malankara Catholics in India (1930).

For the Orthodox these unions, called 'uniatism', were experienced as division of their churches and often as coercion, especially where state power was involved. In the other direction, Eastern Catholics in the Soviet Union and Romania were forcibly merged into Orthodox churches after 1945, the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church in 1946, and were legalised again only in 1989–1990, which led to bitter disputes over church buildings.

Vatican II's decree Orientalium Ecclesiarum (1964) affirmed that Eastern Catholics should preserve their own traditions and called latinisation a distortion. The Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches (1990) gave them their own law. The Balamand statement (1993) accepted their existence but rejected uniatism as a model for future reunion.
