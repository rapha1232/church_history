Rus', Moscow and the Russian Church

Starring

  • The baptism of Rus'. Princess Olga of Kiev was baptised in the 950s. Her grandson Vladimir adopted Byzantine Christianity c. 988, the date given by the Primary Chronicle, and married the emperor's sister Anna. The Chronicle's story of envoys comparing religions is literary. The political alliance is well attested.1 The of Kiev was appointed from Constantinople, usually a Greek.
  • Mongol rule (from 1237–40) and the move of the metropolitan's residence to Vladimir (1299) and then Moscow (1325). The Church gained privileges under the khans. Sergius of Radonezh (d. 1392) renewed monasticism.
  • Autocephaly. Moscow rejected the Union of Florence and its metropolitan, Isidore (IX.4). In 1448 a Russian elected Jonah metropolitan without Constantinople's approval, a de facto . The Moscow Patriarchate was created in 1589 with the consent of Jeremias II, and confirmed by Eastern synods in 1590 and 1593.2
  • "Moscow, the Third Rome." The monk Philotheus of Pskov, in letters usually dated to the 1520s, wrote that two Romes had fallen, the third stood, "and a fourth there shall not be". Historians debate how much this eschatological idea shaped state ideology. Some see it as later nationalist retrojection.3
  • The Old Believers. Patriarch Nikon (1652–58, deposed 1666) aligned Russian rites with contemporary Greek practice. The changes included three fingers for the sign of the cross instead of two, and revised service books. Opponents led by Archpriest Avvakum refused. The councils of 1666–67 anathematised the old rites, and Avvakum was burned (1682). , divided into priested and priestless groups, endured centuries of persecution. The Russian Orthodox Church lifted the anathemas in 1971.4
  • The Synodal period. Peter the Great abolished the patriarchate and created the Holy Synod (1721), supervised by a lay official. This lasted until the patriarchate was restored in 1917. The period also saw missions across Siberia to Alaska and Japan, the spiritual revival around the startsy (elders) of Optina and Seraphim of Sarov, and Russian religious philosophy. The twentieth century is in XV.2.

In depth

From Kiev to Moscow to the Synodal period

The Rus' Primary Chronicle tells how Prince Vladimir of Kiev sent envoys to examine Islam, Judaism, Latin Christianity and Byzantine worship, and how the envoys reported of Hagia Sophia that 'we knew not whether we were in heaven or on earth'. The story is literary, but the baptism of Vladimir and of the people of Kiev around 988, connected with his marriage to the emperor's sister Anna, is historical. The Metropolitan of Kiev was appointed from Constantinople and was usually Greek until the fifteenth century.

The Mongol conquest (1237–1240) devastated Kiev. The Mongols generally exempted the Church from taxes. The metropolitan moved to Vladimir and then, in 1325, to Moscow, whose princes rose to dominate the region. Monastic revival led by Sergius of Radonezh (d. 1392) spread monasteries into the northern forests.

Moscow rejected the Union of Florence, deposed the metropolitan Isidore who had signed it, and in 1448 elected its own metropolitan without Constantinople's approval: in effect, autocephaly. After 1453 some Russian writers spoke of Moscow as the 'Third Rome'. Constantinople recognised a Patriarchate of Moscow in 1589.

Patriarch Nikon's reforms in the 1650s brought Russian liturgical books and practices into line with contemporary Greek usage (for example, making the sign of the cross with three fingers instead of two). Councils in 1666–1667 condemned those who refused, the Old Believers, who were persecuted and divided into many groups. Peter the Great abolished the patriarchate and in 1721 placed the Church under a Holy Synod supervised by a lay official, the Ober-Procurator. The patriarchate was restored only in 1917, weeks before the Bolshevik seizure of power.
