The Christians of Kerala trace their origin to the apostle Thomas, traditionally said to have arrived in AD 52. This tradition is ancient, but it cannot be confirmed from contemporary sources.1 They are attested from the sixth century at the latest (Cosmas Indicopleustes), were linked with the Church of the East, which supplied their bishops, and held privileges recorded in copper plates (c. 849). They used the East Syriac liturgy, while living as a high-status community with local customs.

Their history after the arrival of the Portuguese fragmented the community:

  1. Latinisation. After the Portuguese arrived (1498), Archbishop Aleixo de Menezes of Goa held the Synod of Diamper (Udayamperur, 1599). It imposed Latin practices, condemned "" books (many were burned) and placed the community under Jesuit bishops.2
  2. The Coonan Cross Oath (1653). Many swore at Mattancherry never to submit to the Jesuits. Rome sent Carmelites, and a large part of the community returned to Catholic communion; it became the Syro-Malabar Church. Others accepted the West Syriac (Syriac Orthodox) bishop Gregorios (1665), forming the Malankara tradition (V.2).3
  3. Later divisions:
    • the Mar Thoma Syrian Church, reformed in the 19th century under the influence of Anglican missionaries;
    • the Malabar Independent Syrian Church (Thozhiyur, 1772);
    • the Malankara Orthodox and Jacobite division (1912 onwards);
    • the Syro-Malankara Catholic Church (1930, Mar Ivanios);
    • the Chaldean Syrian Church (Assyrian Church of the East, in Thrissur).

The Syro-Malabar Church is today one of the largest Eastern Catholic Churches. A long dispute over the direction of celebrating the (the "liturgy dispute") has troubled the archdiocese of Ernakulam-Angamaly since the 2020s.

In depth

How the Thomas Christians divided

The Christians of Kerala trace their origin to the apostle Thomas, said to have arrived in 52. The tradition cannot be verified, but Christian communities on the Malabar coast are attested by the sixth century, when the traveller Cosmas Indicopleustes mentions them. A tradition of migration under Thomas of Cana (variously dated to the fourth or eighth century) explains the origin of the Knanaya community. Copper plates granted by local rulers in the ninth century show the Christians as a privileged merchant group. For centuries they received bishops from the Church of the East and used East Syriac liturgy.

The Portuguese arrived in 1498. Archbishop Aleixo de Menezes of Goa held the of Diamper (Udayamperur) in 1599, which brought the community under Latin jurisdiction, condemned 'Nestorian' books and latinised its practices. Resentment led to the Coonan Cross Oath of 1653, in which many swore never to submit to the Jesuits.

Those who returned to Rome became the Syro-Malabar Church, now one of the largest Eastern Catholic Churches, with its own major archbishop. The rest received a bishop from the Syriac Orthodox of Antioch in 1665 and adopted the West Syriac liturgy. They later divided into the Malankara Orthodox Syrian Church (autocephalous ) and the Jacobite Syrian Christian Church (under the patriarch of Antioch), whose property dispute has been in Indian courts for a century.

Further groups include the Mar Thoma Church, reformed under Anglican influence in the nineteenth century; the Syro-Malankara Catholic Church (1930); the Chaldean Syrian Church of the East in Thrissur; and the Malabar Independent Syrian Church. In all, the Thomas tradition is now shared by at least seven churches.
