Under Islamic and Ottoman rule

After the Arab conquests (630s–710s), Christians and Jews became dhimmī, protected but subordinate communities. They paid a poll tax (jizya) and were subject to restrictions associated with the "Pact of ʿUmar", a text whose attribution to the seventh century is doubted. Enforcement varied greatly by period and ruler.1 Christians remained a majority in many regions for centuries, and conversion to Islam was gradual. They also contributed to Arabic culture. Theodore Abu Qurrah wrote Christian theology in Arabic, and the translators Hunayn ibn Ishaq (Church of the East) and Yahya ibn ʿAdi (Syriac Orthodox) worked at Baghdad.2 See also Part IV, study G.

The Ottoman millet

After 1453 Mehmed II installed Gennadios Scholarios as (1454). The Ecumenical Patriarch became the civil as well as religious head of the Orthodox subjects of the Sultan (the Rum millet). This increased his authority over the other Orthodox patriarchates and over Slavs and Romanians, but exposed the office to purchase and deposition. There were very frequent changes of patriarch in the 17th–18th centuries. The " system" as a formal, uniform institution has been questioned as a later idealisation.3 Neomartyrs, Christians executed for apostasy from Islam or for refusing conversion, were venerated. The Greek Church's leadership was dominated by Phanariot families (named after the Phanar district of Istanbul).

Patriarch Cyril Loukaris (d. 1638) published a Calvinist-leaning Confession (1629) and was strangled amid court intrigue. The of Jerusalem (1672, under Dositheos) answered with a confession using Latin-influenced terms such as metousiōsis. This shows the complex relation of post-Byzantine Orthodoxy to Western confessional debates.4

National Churches

As the Ottoman Empire contracted in the nineteenth century, national Orthodox Churches claimed :

  • Greece declared it in 1833, and Constantinople recognised it in 1850;
  • Serbia (1879);
  • Romania (1885);
  • the Bulgarian Exarchate (1870), which Constantinople declared schismatic in 1872 for "ethnophyletism" (organising the Church by ethnicity), a schism healed in 1945.

The tension between universal Orthodoxy and national Churches, and between Constantinople and Moscow, continues today (XV.6).

In depth

The Church under the Ottoman sultans

After taking Constantinople in 1453, Mehmed II installed the anti-unionist monk Gennadios Scholarios as patriarch in 1454. Over time the patriarch became head of the Orthodox community (Rum millet) in civil as well as religious matters: he answered to the sultan for his flock, collected taxes and judged family law. The Armenian patriarch of Constantinople had a similar role for non- Christians.

Christians were dhimmis: protected but subordinate, paying the poll-tax (jizya), forbidden to build new churches without permission, and barred from carrying arms. Through the devshirme, Christian boys from the Balkans were taken for the Janissary corps and administration, mainly between the fifteenth and seventeenth centuries. Conversion to Islam was common in some regions, such as Bosnia and Albania. Christians who converted to Islam and returned to Christianity could be executed; the Church honours many of them as 'neomartyrs'.

The patriarchate became dependent on the Ottoman court and on wealthy Greek families of the Phanar district, and patriarchs were frequently deposed. The eighteenth century saw the Kollyvades movement and the publication of the Philokalia (Venice, 1782), an anthology of hesychast texts that later shaped Russian and modern Orthodox spirituality.

Nineteenth-century nationalism broke the millet into national churches as states won independence: Greece declared autocephaly in 1833 (recognised 1850), Serbia in 1879, Romania in 1885. The Bulgarian Exarchate, created by an Ottoman decree in 1870, was declared schismatic by Constantinople in 1872 on the ground of 'ethnophyletism', the organisation of the Church by nationality. The schism was healed only in 1945.
