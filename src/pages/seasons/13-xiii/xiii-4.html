Georgia, Armenia, Egypt and Ethiopia

Starring

Georgia

Royal conversion in Kartli in the 330s (St Nino). The Church followed the Armenians in hesitating over Chalcedon, then definitively accepted it c. 600–608, separating from the Armenian Church. Georgia is thus Eastern Orthodox. Its is traditionally dated to the 5th century; the details and dates are debated. It was abolished by Russia (1811), restored in 1917, and recognised by Constantinople in 1990.

Armenia

The Armenian Church developed its own alphabet (Mesrop Mashtots, c. 405) and literature. Medieval centres included the Cilician kingdom (1198–1375), which had close contacts with the crusaders and Rome. The Catholicosate returned to Etchmiadzin in 1441, while a Cilician line continued at Sis. The genocide of 1915–1923 destroyed most of the Armenian population of Anatolia. Historians widely recognise it as genocide, a characterisation the Turkish state disputes.1

Egypt

Copts remained the majority in Egypt perhaps until the 10th–14th centuries; estimates of the timing differ.2 Arabic replaced Coptic in daily use and increasingly in church. The 13th-century "Coptic renaissance" produced the Awlad al-ʿAssal theologians. Since the 20th century the Church has seen a monastic and Sunday School revival (Matta al-Miskin; Pope Shenouda III, 1971–2012), alongside periodic sectarian violence, such as the 2015 killing of 21 Coptic Christians in Libya. Pope Francis enrolled them in the Roman Martyrology in 2023.

Ethiopia

Distinctive practices include circumcision, Sabbath observance alongside Sunday, and the tabot (altar tablet). The Zagwe dynasty (Lalibela rock churches, c. 12th–13th c.) and the "Solomonic" restoration (1270) were followed by a literary flowering (the Kebra Nagast, 14th c.). Survival through the Adal invasion (1529–43) and Portuguese Jesuit attempts at union (the conversion of Emperor Susenyos in 1622, reversed in 1632) shaped a fiercely independent Church. Patriarchate from 1959 (V.2).3

In depth

Four ancient churches on the edges of empires

Georgia. Tradition credits Saint Nino, a woman from Cappadocia, with converting the royal court of Kartli (Iberia) in the 330s. The Georgian Church followed the Armenians in rejecting Chalcedon for a time but reaccepted it around 607. Its gained autocephaly in the early medieval period; it was absorbed into the Russian Church in 1811 and restored its autocephaly in 1917.

Armenia. The monk Mesrop Mashtots created the Armenian alphabet around 405 to translate the Bible. At the battle of Avarayr (451) Armenians fought the Persians to defend their faith. Armenia did not attend Chalcedon and later rejected it, partly in reaction to Byzantine pressure. The catholicos at Etchmiadzin heads the Armenian Apostolic Church, with a second catholicosate at Antelias (Cilicia) in Lebanon.

Egypt. After 451 the majority of Egyptian Christians rejected Chalcedon and followed their own patriarchs. The Arab conquest (639–642) ended Byzantine interference. Over centuries Arabic replaced Coptic as the spoken language, and Christians became a minority, probably by the tenth to fourteenth centuries. The Coptic Orthodox Church today is the largest Christian community in the Middle East.

Ethiopia. Frumentius, a Syrian captive at the court of Aksum, converted King Ezana in the mid-fourth century and was consecrated bishop by Athanasius. Ezana's coins replaced pagan symbols with the cross. Monks known as the Nine Saints spread monasticism around 500. The rock-hewn churches of Lalibela date from around 1200. For centuries the Ethiopian Church's (abuna) was an Egyptian monk sent from Alexandria; the church became autocephalous with its own in 1959. Its canon of Scripture is the largest of any church and includes 1 Enoch and Jubilees.

Why it matters today

Georgia, Armenia, Egypt and Ethiopia hold some of the world’s oldest Christian cultures, still vibrant.
