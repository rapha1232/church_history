Starring

The mission of Cyril and Methodius to Moravia (863) and its Slavonic liturgy are described in VIII.6. Expelled from Moravia, their disciples Clement and Naum took the tradition to Bulgaria. There the Preslav and Ohrid schools developed Old Church Slavonic literature and the Cyrillic script, named in Cyril's honour but probably created in Bulgaria c. 890s–900s. Bulgaria's Church gained a patriarchate, recognised by Constantinople in 927. Serbia received an autocephalous archbishopric under St Sava (1219). The Serbian patriarchate was established in 1346, abolished by the Ottomans in 1766, and restored in 1920. Croats, Slovenes, Czechs and Poles entered the Latin Church. The line between Latin and Slavonic-Byzantine Christianity ran through central Europe and remains a cultural fault line.1

In depth

Cyril, Methodius and the Slavonic liturgy

In 862 Prince Rastislav of Great Moravia asked Constantinople for teachers who could instruct his people in their own language, partly to reduce the influence of Frankish clergy. Emperor Michael III and Photius sent two brothers from Thessalonica, Constantine (who took the monastic name Cyril shortly before his death) and Methodius, who knew the Slavonic dialect of the Thessalonica region.

Constantine created the Glagolitic alphabet and translated the Gospels and liturgical texts. Frankish bishops objected that only Hebrew, Greek and Latin were fit for worship. The brothers went to Rome, where Pope Hadrian II approved the Slavonic liturgy (c. 868). Cyril died in Rome in 869; Methodius was consecrated archbishop of Sirmium, imprisoned for over two years by Bavarian bishops, and after his death in 885 his disciples were expelled from Moravia.

The work survived in Bulgaria. Boris I had been baptized around 864–865 and, after flirting with Rome, chose Constantinople. Disciples such as Clement and Naum founded schools at Ohrid and Preslav, and the Cyrillic alphabet, based on Greek letters, was developed there in the late ninth or early tenth century. Old Church Slavonic became the liturgical and literary language of Bulgaria, Serbia and Rus'.

Serbia's church became autocephalous under Saint Sava in 1219. The Slavic churches inherited Byzantine theology and liturgy but in their own language, which made Orthodoxy a central part of national identity in the Balkans and Eastern Europe. John Paul II named Cyril and Methodius co-patrons of Europe in 1980.
