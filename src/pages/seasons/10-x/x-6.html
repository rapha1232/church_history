Starring

Innocent III (1198–1216) took papal power to its height. He intervened in imperial elections, made England a papal fief (1213) and convened Lateran IV. Boniface VIII came into conflict with Philip IV of France over the taxation and trial of clergy, and issued the bull Unam sanctam (18 November 1302):

Porro subesse Romano Pontifici omni humanae creaturae declaramus, dicimus, diffinimus et pronunciamus omnino esse de necessitate salutis.

“…it is absolutely necessary for salvation that every human creature be subject to the Roman Pontiff.”

Boniface VIII, Unam sanctam (1302), final sentence. English phrase checked against the translation at papalencyclicals.net; Latin from the standard text (not re-checked). Q65 · partly checked

Philip's agents seized Boniface at Anagni (1303), and he died weeks later. How far Unam sanctam's claims are doctrinally binding has long been debated among Catholic theologians. Its final sentence is usually read in light of the Church's later teaching on salvation outside its visible bounds.1

Avignon and the Great Western Schism

From 1309 to 1377 the popes resided at Avignon. The papacy there was efficient and heavily taxing. Critics such as Petrarch called it the "Babylonian captivity". Catherine of Siena and Bridget of Sweden urged a return to Rome. Gregory XI returned in 1377. After his death, the disputed election of Urban VI (1378) led a group of cardinals to elect a rival, Clement VII, who went back to Avignon. From 1409, after the Council of Pisa, there were three claimants. Western Christendom divided its allegiance largely along political lines.

Constance and conciliarism

The Council of Constance (1414–1418) ended the schism. It deposed John XXIII (the Pisan line) and Benedict XIII (Avignon), accepted the resignation of Gregory XII (Rome), and elected Martin V (1417).2 Its decree Haec sancta (1415) declared that a general council holds its power immediately from Christ and that everyone, including the pope, must obey it in matters of faith, the ending of schism and reform. Frequens (1417) required regular councils.3 Whether Haec sancta was a dogmatic definition or an emergency measure is a classic dispute. Catholic doctrine since Vatican I rejects the superiority of council over pope. Conciliarist historians (Brian Tierney, Francis Oakley) stress its roots in canon law and its long afterlife.4 The Council of Basel (1431–1449) clashed with Eugenius IV, who moved the council to Ferrara-Florence (IX.4). Pius II's bull Execrabilis (1460) forbade appeals from pope to council.

In depth

Conciliarism: a council above the pope?

The Great Western Schism began in 1378 when the cardinals, having elected Urban VI in Rome under pressure from the crowd, declared the election invalid and chose Clement VII, who returned to Avignon. Europe divided along political lines. A council at Pisa (1409) deposed both popes and elected a third, which only made matters worse.

The Council of Constance (1414–1418), backed by Emperor Sigismund, ended the schism: John XXIII (of the Pisan line) was deposed, Gregory XII (Roman line) resigned after formally convoking the council, Benedict XIII (Avignon) was deposed, and Martin V was elected in 1417. Constance also condemned Wycliffe and burned Jan Hus.

In the decree Haec sancta (1415) Constance declared that a general council holds its power directly from Christ and that everyone, including the pope, must obey it in matters of faith, the ending of the schism and reform. Frequens (1417) required councils to meet regularly. These were the high points of , a theory with roots in canon law and in thinkers such as Jean Gerson and Pierre d'Ailly.

The Council of Basel (1431–1449) pushed the theory into open conflict with Eugenius IV, deposed him and elected an antipope, and lost support. Pius II's bull Execrabilis (1460) forbade appeals from the pope to a future council. The status of Haec sancta is still debated by Catholic historians: was it a valid dogmatic decree, or an emergency measure from a council whose legitimacy at that session was itself uncertain? Vatican I (1870) settled the question in favour of papal , but conciliar ideas remained alive in Gallicanism and in some Reformation-era Catholic thought.
