Canon law, heresy and the inquisitions

c. 1140–15004 minPreviously: Crusades, military orders, Muslims and Jews

Canon law. Gratian's Decretum (c. 1140) organised a millennium of conciliar and papal texts.

Starring

Canon law. Gratian's Decretum (c. 1140) organised a millennium of conciliar and papal texts. Papal decretals were added (the Liber Extra, 1234), forming the Corpus iuris canonici, which remained in force until the Code of 1917. Canon lawyers developed ideas of corporations, representation and natural rights that influenced Western legal thought.

Lateran IV (1215). The largest medieval council. Its decisions included:

  • the first conciliar use of the language of : in its creed (canon 1), the bread and wine are transsubstantiatis, “changed in substance”;
  • annual confession and Easter communion for all the faithful (canon 21);
  • a ban on new religious orders;
  • regulation of Jews (canons 67–70);
  • a call for crusade.

Dissent

Popular religious movements multiplied from the twelfth century. The Waldensians (from c. 1170) were lay preachers of poverty, excommunicated in 1184. They survived and later joined the Reformed movement. The Cathars of southern France and Italy were described by their opponents as dualists with their own "perfect" clergy. Some historians (e.g. R. I. Moore, Mark Pegg) have argued that the image of an organised Cathar church owes much to inquisitors' constructions. Others defend its substantial reality.2

Death tolls have been exaggerated in popular accounts and minimised in apologetic ones. Archival research on the Spanish Inquisition (e.g. Gustav Henningsen and Jaime Contreras) suggests that a few thousand people were executed in total across its history, most of them before 1530, out of tens of thousands tried.3 The procedures still involved secret accusers, torture and the death penalty for beliefs. The Catholic Church has since expressed repentance for such methods, notably in John Paul II's "Day of Pardon" in 2000. Protestant authorities also executed people for heresy, for example Servetus at Geneva (1553) and in several territories (XI.4).4

In depth

How the inquisitions actually operated

'The Inquisition' was not a single institution in the Middle Ages. There were inquisitorial procedures used by bishops and, from the 1230s, papally commissioned inquisitors, mostly Dominicans and Franciscans, operating in particular regions such as Languedoc and northern Italy. The Spanish Inquisition (1478) and the Roman Inquisition (1542) were later, more centralised bodies.

The legal background was the revival of Roman law and canon law. Gratian's Decretum (c. 1140) and the Decretals of Gregory IX (1234) made canon law a university discipline. 'Inquisitorial' procedure meant that a judge investigated on his own initiative instead of waiting for a private accuser. Lucius III's Ad abolendam (1184) required bishops to seek out heretics, and Innocent IV's Ad extirpanda (1252) permitted torture under limits.

Inquisitors' manuals, such as that of Bernard Gui (c. 1324), describe the procedure: a period of grace for voluntary confession, interrogation, and penalties ranging from pilgrimage and wearing crosses to prison. The Church handed over obstinate or relapsed heretics to the secular authorities for execution, usually by burning.

Modern research based on surviving registers shows that most cases ended in penances rather than death; in Bernard Gui's own sentences, for example, a minority were handed over for execution. That qualification does not alter the basic fact that the medieval Church accepted the killing of heretics as legitimate, a position the Catholic Church has since repudiated.

Why it matters today

The inquisitions are a warning about coercion in religion, and the source of modern principles of due process in church law.
