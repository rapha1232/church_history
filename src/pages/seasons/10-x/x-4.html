Crusades, military orders, Muslims and Jews

Starring

Urban II's appeal at Clermont (1095) launched the First Crusade. Crusaders took Jerusalem in July 1099 with a massacre of its Muslim and Jewish inhabitants that the Latin sources themselves describe.1 The crusader states lasted until the fall of Acre in 1291. Crusading ideas were extended against Muslims in Spain (the Reconquista, completed with the fall of Granada in 1492), against pagans in the Baltic, against heretics (the Albigensian Crusade, 1209–1229), and against the popes' political enemies. Historians disagree about crusaders' motives. Religious devotion, the penitential , land and family strategy all played a part, with different weight for different people.2 The Fourth Crusade's sack of Constantinople is in IX.3.

Military orders. The Templars (c. 1119), the Hospitallers and the Teutonic Knights combined monastic vows with warfare. The Templars were arrested in France in 1307 on charges extracted by torture and suppressed by Clement V in 1312, at the Council of Vienne, under pressure from Philip IV. Most historians consider the charges of heresy unfounded.3

Jews in medieval Christendom

The First Crusade began with massacres of Jewish communities in the Rhineland (1096), which some bishops tried to stop.4 Other measures followed:

  • Lateran IV (1215), canon 68, required Jews and Muslims to wear distinctive dress.
  • Ritual-murder and host-desecration libels spread, often repudiated by popes and bishops but also exploited.
  • Jews were expelled from England (1290), France (1306 and later) and Spain (1492).

Augustine's teaching that the Jews should be preserved as "witnesses" underlay a papal policy of protection (Sicut Iudaeis, from c. 1120) that coexisted with degradation. See also Part IV study H.

Christians and Muslims

Muslim–Christian relations included war, trade, translation and debate. Peter the Venerable of Cluny commissioned the first Latin Qur'an (1143). Francis of Assisi met Sultan al-Kamil at Damietta (1219). Ramon Llull (d. c. 1316) promoted the study of Arabic for mission. In the East, Christians living under Muslim rule were a majority in some regions until roughly the ninth to fourteenth centuries. The timing of their conversion to Islam is debated (Part IV, study G).

In depth

The crusades and their victims

Urban II's appeal at Clermont in November 1095 responded to a Byzantine request for help against the Seljuk Turks, but he framed it as an armed pilgrimage to free Jerusalem, with remission of penance for participants. The First Crusade took Jerusalem in July 1099 with a massacre of Muslims and Jews. Four crusader states were founded. Military orders combined monastic vows and fighting: the Templars (c. 1119) and the Hospitallers, originally a hospice in Jerusalem.

Later crusades failed to repeat the success. Edessa fell in 1144, the Second Crusade (1147–1149) failed, Saladin retook Jerusalem after the battle of Hattin (1187), and the Third Crusade (1189–1192) won back only the coast. The Fourth Crusade was diverted to Constantinople in 1204 (IX.3). Acre, the last major crusader city, fell in 1291.

The crusade idea was also turned on other targets: the Reconquista in Spain, pagan peoples around the Baltic, the Cathars of southern France (the Albigensian Crusade from 1209), and political enemies of the papacy in Italy.

For European Jews the crusades brought massacres, beginning in the Rhineland towns of Speyer, Worms and Mainz in 1096 at the hands of crusading bands, despite efforts by some bishops to protect them. The thirteenth century added the blood libel, accusations of host desecration, the distinctive dress required by Lateran IV (1215), burnings of the Talmud (Paris, 1242) and expulsions from England (1290), France (1306) and Spain (1492).

Why it matters today

The Crusades remain a sore point in Christian–Muslim relations and in Christian memory of violence done in the name of faith.
