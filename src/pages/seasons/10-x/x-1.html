The reform papacy and the investiture conflict

c. 1046–11223 min

From 1046 the German emperor Henry III installed reforming popes.

Starring

From 1046 the German emperor Henry III installed reforming popes. The reform party they brought to Rome (Leo IX, Humbert, Hildebrand) attacked simony (the sale of church offices) and clerical marriage ("Nicolaitism").1 They also pursued a larger aim, the "freedom of the Church" (libertas ecclesiae) from lay control. The Lateran of 1059 reserved papal elections to the cardinals.2

Hildebrand became Pope Gregory VII (1073–1085). A set of propositions entered in his register in 1075, the Dictatus papae, claimed among other things that the Roman Church had never erred, that the pope alone could depose bishops, and that he could depose emperors. Historians debate whether it was a programme, an index of canon-law texts, or a private memorandum.3

Investiture

The conflict came to a head over who should invest bishops with ring and staff, the symbols of their spiritual office. In much of Europe bishops were also great lords and royal officials. When Henry IV insisted on investing them, Gregory excommunicated and deposed him (1076). Henry did penance at Canossa in January 1077 and was absolved, but war followed. Gregory died in exile in Salerno (1085). A compromise was reached in the Concordat of Worms (1122): the emperor renounced with ring and staff, while keeping a role in elections and investiture with temporal rights.4

The "Gregorian reform" made clerical celibacy a general rule in the Latin Church. The First and Second Lateran Councils (1123, 1139) declared marriages of priests invalid. It also built up papal government through legates, appeals and canon law, and sharpened the distinction between clergy and laity. Historians differ on whether it was a "revolution" (Harold Berman, Gerd Tellenbach) or the acceleration of longer trends.5 It also widened the gap with the Greek Church, where married clergy and imperial involvement were accepted (IX.1).

In depth

What the investiture conflict was really about

In the eleventh century kings and great lords routinely chose bishops and abbots and invested them with the ring and staff, symbols of their spiritual office, in return for oaths of loyalty. Bishops were also great landholders and royal servants. Reformers attacked two linked abuses: simony (buying church offices) and clerical marriage (nicolaitism). From the 1050s they increasingly saw lay investiture itself as the root of both.

Gregory VII (1073–1085) pressed the issue furthest. The Dictatus papae (1075), a list of propositions in his register, claimed that the pope alone may use imperial insignia, may depose emperors, and can be judged by no one, and that the Roman Church has never erred. When Henry IV of Germany and his bishops declared Gregory deposed in 1076, Gregory excommunicated Henry and released his subjects from their oaths. Henry's penance at Canossa in January 1077 was a political move to regain support; the conflict resumed, and Henry later installed an antipope.

The Concordat of Worms (1122) between Henry V and Calixtus II was a compromise. Bishops would be elected canonically and invested with ring and staff by the Church, while the emperor would invest them with their lands and temporal rights by the sceptre, in Germany before consecration. Similar settlements were made in England (1107) and France.

The longer consequence was the idea of the Church as a distinct legal body under the pope, separate from secular rule. Some historians, following Harold Berman, see in this the origin of Western legal tradition and of the separation of spiritual and secular spheres.

Why it matters today

The struggle over who appoints bishops still appears in disputes between the Vatican and governments such as China.
