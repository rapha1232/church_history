The Black Death, Wycliffe, Hus and late medieval piety

Starring

The Black Death of 1347–1351 killed perhaps a third to a half of Europe's population. Estimates vary by region.1 Religious responses included penitential flagellant processions, condemned by Clement VI in 1349, and massacres of Jews accused of poisoning wells, which Clement VI also condemned. The plague brought new cults of plague saints and intensified attention to death, purgatory and intercession.

John Wycliffe and the Lollards

The Oxford theologian John Wycliffe (d. 1384) argued for the supreme authority of Scripture and attacked the wealth of the clergy and the doctrine of . His followers produced English translations of the Bible (the "Wycliffite Bible", 1380s–90s). His followers, the "Lollards", were persecuted after 1401. The Council of Constance condemned him posthumously.2

Jan Hus

Jan Hus, a Prague preacher influenced by Wycliffe, criticised indulgences and clerical abuses. He came to Constance under a safe-conduct from Emperor Sigismund, was condemned, and was burned on 6 July 1415.3 The Hussite movement that followed demanded communion in both kinds (the chalice for the laity, hence "Utraquists"). It fought off crusades (1420–1431) and secured the Compacts of Basel (1436). The Unity of the Brethren (Unitas Fratrum, 1457) descends from it and is an ancestor of the later Moravian Church. Luther later declared, "We are all Hussites."4

Late medieval piety

Older historiography (Protestant and Catholic alike) portrayed the late medieval Church as decadent. Recent research (Eamon Duffy, The Stripping of the Altars, 1992) stresses the vitality of lay religion on the eve of the Reformation, at least in England.5 Its features included:

  • parish guilds, pilgrimages and devotion to the saints and the (the feast of Corpus Christi, 1264);
  • chantries for the dead;
  • books of hours;
  • the Devotio moderna of the Brethren of the Common Life (Geert Groote, d. 1384), and Thomas à Kempis' Imitation of Christ (c. 1418–27).

Calls for reform "in head and members" were constant, and were echoed by humanists such as Erasmus (XI.1).

In depth

Wycliffe, Hus and the devotional currents of the late Middle Ages

The Black Death (1347–1351) killed perhaps a third or more of Europe's population. Responses ranged from flagellant processions, condemned by Clement VI, to massacres of Jews accused of poisoning wells; Clement issued bulls defending the Jews. The shortage of clergy and the scale of death affected the Church's pastoral life for generations.

John Wycliffe (d. 1384), an Oxford theologian, argued that dominion depends on grace, that the Church is the body of the predestined rather than the visible hierarchy, that the Bible is the sole supreme authority, and that the doctrine of transubstantiation is philosophically incoherent. His followers produced the first complete English Bible (c. 1380s–1390s). The Lollards were suppressed after the 1401 statute De heretico comburendo and Archbishop Arundel's Constitutions (1409), which banned unlicensed English translations.

In Bohemia Jan Hus (c. 1370–1415) drew on Wycliffe to attack clerical wealth and indulgences. Summoned to Constance under a safe-conduct from Sigismund, he was condemned and burned on 6 July 1415. His followers demanded Communion in both kinds (from which the chalice, calix, gave the name Utraquists or Calixtines) and fought off five crusades. The Compactata of Basel (1436) granted the chalice to the Bohemians. The Unity of the Brethren (Unitas Fratrum), founded in 1457, later became the Moravian Church.

Alongside protest there was intense devotion. The Devotio Moderna, begun by Geert Groote in the Netherlands (d. 1384), created communities of lay Brothers and Sisters of the Common Life and promoted inner piety. Its classic text is the Imitation of Christ, usually attributed to Thomas à Kempis. Confraternities, pilgrimages, relic cults, the rosary and the growth of preaching all show how much late medieval people invested in religion; the Reformation grew out of a vigorous religious culture, not a dead one.
