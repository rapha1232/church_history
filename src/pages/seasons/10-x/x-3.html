In depth

How scholastic theology worked

Scholasticism means the method of the medieval schools: questions were posed, authorities on both sides were set out, and a resolution was reached by reasoned argument. Anselm of Canterbury (d. 1109) described theology as 'faith seeking understanding' and in Why God Became Man argued that the incarnation was necessary to make satisfaction for human sin. Peter Abelard's Sic et non (c. 1120) listed apparently contradictory patristic texts to train students in resolving them.

Peter Lombard's Sentences (c. 1150), a structured collection of patristic opinions in four books, became the standard textbook: every master of theology had to lecture on it until the sixteenth century. Universities grew out of cathedral schools and guilds of masters and students: Bologna (law) and Paris (theology) by about 1200, Oxford soon after.

The recovery of Aristotle's works, through translations from Arabic and Greek in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, along with the commentaries of Avicenna and Averroes, raised the question of how far natural reason could go without revelation. Thomas Aquinas (d. 1274) argued that grace does not destroy nature but perfects it, so reason and faith cannot finally conflict. His Summa theologiae was influential but contested: some of his theses were caught up in the Paris condemnations of 1277.

Later scholastics took different paths. John Duns Scotus (d. 1308) emphasised the freedom of God's will and defended the Immaculate Conception. William of Ockham (d. 1347) rejected real universals, a position called nominalism, and stressed God's absolute power. The 'modern way' (via moderna) influenced by Ockham was the theology in which Luther was trained.
