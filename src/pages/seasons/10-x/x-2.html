They preached in the growing cities, staffed universities, and took missions as far as the Mongol court.2 The Franciscans John of Plano Carpini (1245–47) and William of Rubruck (1253–55) travelled to the Mongols; John of Montecorvino became the first Latin archbishop of Khanbaliq (1307). In depth

Monks, canons and friars: the shapes of medieval religious life

The Rule of Benedict (c. 530) set the pattern for Western monks: stability in one community, obedience to an abbot, and a day divided between the Divine Office, work and reading. Cluny, founded in 910, built a vast network of houses under one abbot, famous for its elaborate liturgy. In reaction, the Cistercians (Cîteaux, 1098), above all under Bernard of Clairvaux, sought simplicity, manual labour and remote sites.

Canons regular were priests living in community under the Rule of Augustine, serving churches and cathedrals. The Premonstratensians, founded by Norbert of Xanten in 1120, are the best-known order of canons.

The friars of the thirteenth century were different again. They lived by begging (mendicancy) and moved through the growing towns to preach. Francis of Assisi's brothers received papal approval in 1209–10 and a final rule in 1223. Dominic's Order of Preachers was confirmed in 1216 to counter heresy through learned preaching. Carmelites and Augustinian friars followed. Friars dominated the new universities, where Thomas Aquinas (Dominican) and Bonaventure (Franciscan) taught.

Women's religious life ranged from enclosed nuns, such as the Poor Clares founded with Clare of Assisi, to the beguines of the Low Countries, who lived in communities without permanent vows. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) forbade founding new orders with new rules, so later groups adapted existing ones.

Why it matters today

Franciscans, Dominicans and other medieval orders are still active worldwide in education, mission and care for the poor.
