Catholic and Orthodox doctrine compared

1009–1484, with modern dialogue6 minPreviously: Toward reconciliation (1964–2026)

This chapter sets the Catholic and Eastern Orthodox positions side by side on ten issues.

Starring

This chapter sets the Catholic and Eastern Orthodox positions side by side on ten issues. Each entry states the position as found in authoritative documents of the tradition, outlines how it developed, and notes internal diversity. Use the filter to show one topic at a time.

A note on sources: for the Catholic Church, the teaching of councils, papal definitions and the Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992/1997) is binding in a way that has no exact Orthodox equivalent. Orthodox positions are drawn from councils, from patristic consensus, and from widely received texts such as the Encyclical of the Eastern Patriarchs (1848). Orthodox theologians themselves debate the authority of some of these, such as the seventeenth-century "confessions".

1. Papal primacy

Catholic

The bishop of Rome, as successor of Peter, has not only of honour but of jurisdiction, by divine institution (Vatican I, Pastor aeternus, 1870, chs. 1–3). This is exercised collegially with the bishops (Vatican II, Lumen gentium 22).1

Orthodox

Rome held the first place (presbeia) in the communion of Churches by the canons. After the schism, that place among the Orthodox belongs to Constantinople. Primacy exists at every level, but as presidency within a , not authority over it.

Development and diversity: see Vol. VII ch. 6. Some Orthodox theologians (e.g. Afanasiev) and the Ravenna document (2007) accept a universal primacy in principle. The disputed questions are its nature and its relation to the bishop of Rome.

2. Universal jurisdiction

Catholic

The pope has "full, supreme and universal power over the whole Church, a power which he can always exercise unhindered" (Lumen gentium 22, paraphrasing Vatican I). This power is ordinary and immediate over all Churches and faithful.2

Orthodox

No bishop holds jurisdiction in another's Church. Each local Church is fully the Church. Autocephalous Churches govern themselves through their own synods.

Development: Catholic theology of universal jurisdiction developed through the Gregorian reform and canon law (11th–13th c.) and was defined in 1870. Eastern Catholic Churches retain their own synods and patriarchs within Catholic communion.

3. Infallibility

Catholic

When the pope defines a doctrine of faith or morals ex cathedra, he possesses the infallibility with which Christ endowed the Church (Pastor aeternus ch. 4). The college of bishops with the pope, and ecumenical councils, also teach infallibly (Lumen gentium 25).

Orthodox

The Church as a whole is preserved in truth by the Holy Spirit. No individual bishop is infallible. Councils are authoritative through their by the whole Church. The 1848 Encyclical states that the guardian of piety is the whole body of the Church, the people itself.3

Development: The term and the debate are medieval. In Brian Tierney's influential (and contested) account, the doctrine was first articulated by the Franciscan Peter Olivi (13th c.). The 1870 definition was contested within Catholicism; the Old Catholic Churches separated over it. The condemnation of Honorius was a central historical argument. Infallibility is now under study in the Joint Commission (Vol. VII ch. 6).

4. Filioque

Catholic

The Spirit proceeds eternally from the Father and the Son as from one principle (Lyon II, 1274; Florence, 1439). The Father is the source of the whole Trinity. The Greek formula "through the Son" is recognised as expressing the same faith (Florence; PCPCU clarification, 1995).

Orthodox

The Spirit proceeds (ekporeuetai) from the Father alone as sole cause. The Son's role may be expressed as an eternal manifestation or shining forth (Blachernae, 1285). The unilateral addition to the creed is uncanonical.

Diversity: Some Orthodox theologians (e.g. Bolotov, 19th c.) treated the Filioque as a permissible theological opinion (theologoumenon). Others (e.g. Lossky) saw it as the root of all Western divergence. See Vol. VIII ch. 5.

5. Original / ancestral sin

Catholic

Through Adam's sin, all humans are born deprived of original holiness and justice. This is "original sin", a state "contracted" and not committed, which baptism removes (Council of Trent, Session 5; Catechism 404–405). The concept is shaped by Augustine.4

Orthodox

Humans inherit the consequences of the ancestral sin (mortality, corruption, a tendency to sin), but not personal guilt for Adam's act. Many Orthodox writers prefer the term "ancestral sin" (propatorikē hamartia).

Diversity: The sharpness of this contrast is partly a twentieth-century construction (e.g. John Romanides, 1957). Earlier Orthodox confessional texts use language close to Latin usage. The Catechism itself states that original sin is called "sin" only in an analogical sense.

6. Purgatory and purification after death

Catholic

Those who die in God's grace but imperfectly purified undergo purification after death. The living help them through prayer and the (Lyon II; Florence; Trent, Session 25; Catechism 1030–1032). The image of fire is traditional but not defined.5

Orthodox

Prayer for the dead is ancient and constant, and the departed are helped by the Church's prayers. The Orthodox reject a distinct place or state of satisfaction-paying, punitive purification, and the associated system of indulgences. Mark of Ephesus argued this at Ferrara (1438).

Development: The term purgatorium appears as a noun in the 12th century (J. Le Goff). The issue was first a point of East–West dispute in the 13th century. Many modern theologians on both sides consider the difference reconcilable.

7. Immaculate Conception

Catholic

Mary was preserved from all stain of original sin from the first moment of her conception, by a singular grace in view of Christ's merits (Pius IX, Ineffabilis Deus, 8 December 1854).6

Orthodox

Mary is Panagia, "all-holy", and the Orthodox celebrate her conception (9 December). The dogma is rejected, as unnecessary given the Orthodox understanding of ancestral sin and as implying that Mary was removed from the common human condition.

Development and diversity: The doctrine was debated in the West for centuries. Bernard and Aquinas opposed it; Duns Scotus defended it. Some late Byzantine homilists used language of Mary's purity from conception, and Orthodox opposition hardened after 1854.

8. Grace and deification

Catholic

Grace is a participation in the divine life (Catechism 1996–1997, citing 2 Pet. 1:4). Western theology also developed a distinction between "created grace" (a habit in the soul) and uncreated grace (God himself). Deification language is ancient in the West too (Augustine; Aquinas, ST III q.1 a.2).

Orthodox

Salvation is theosis: real participation in God through his uncreated , by the Spirit, in the Church's sacramental and ascetic life. The Latin concept of created grace is often criticised.

Diversity: Recent scholarship (e.g. Norman Russell, A. N. Williams) has emphasised a shared patristic heritage of deification and found less contrast than older polemics claimed.7

9. Essence and energies

Catholic

God is simple. Distinctions in God are conceptual, not real (Lateran IV's teaching on the incomprehensibility of God; the Thomist tradition). The saints in heaven see the divine essence (Benedict XII, Benedictus Deus, 1336). There is no dogmatic condemnation of Palamism.8

Orthodox

God's essence is utterly unknowable, even to the blessed. God is truly known and participated in his uncreated energies, which are God himself and not created effects (councils of 1341–1351).

Diversity: Some Catholic theologians accept Palamite theology as compatible with Catholic faith, and Eastern Catholics honour Palamas. Some Orthodox theologians question how sharply the distinction should be opposed to Western simplicity.

10. Liturgy and discipline

Catholic

Latin rite: unleavened bread; celibate clergy as a rule (with exceptions); confirmation normally by a bishop and, in many places, deferred to later childhood or adolescence; Communion under one kind is common; Gregorian calendar. Eastern Catholic Churches follow Eastern practice.

Orthodox

Leavened bread; married priests and deacons (bishops celibate); chrismation and communion given to infants at baptism; Communion in both kinds; many Churches keep the Julian calendar for some or all feasts.

Status: Both Churches now officially recognise the other's liturgical practices as legitimate, though popular polemic persists. The Easter date question is under active discussion (ch. 5).
