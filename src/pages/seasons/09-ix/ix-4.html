The Second Council of Lyon (1274)

Michael VIII was threatened by Charles of Anjou's plans to restore the Latin Empire. He sought papal protection through church union. At the fourth session of the Second Council of Lyon (6 July 1274), his envoys, led by George Akropolites, presented a profession of faith in his name. It accepted the , purgatory, seven sacraments and Roman , and the Greek clergy sang the creed with the Filioque. The council's constitution on the Holy Spirit defined procession "from the Father and the Son … not as from two principles but as from one principle".1

The union was imposed in Constantinople against fierce resistance. The unionist John Bekkos was installed in 1275. Michael VIII was excommunicated by Pope Martin IV in 1281, for political reasons, and died in 1282. His son Andronikos II repudiated the union immediately. A council at Blachernae (1285) condemned Bekkos and adopted the Tomus of Patriarch Gregory II of Cyprus. It acknowledged an eternal "manifestation" of the Spirit through the Son while rejecting the Filioque as cause.

Interlude: the Hesychast controversy (1337–1351)

Barlaam of Calabria, a Greek from southern Italy, attacked the claims of hesychast monks on Athos to see the uncreated light of Christ's Transfiguration. Gregory Palamas defended them by distinguishing God's unknowable essence (ousia) from his uncreated (energeiai), in which God is truly participated. Councils in Constantinople in 1341, 1347 and 1351 endorsed Palamas. Barlaam and Akindynos, who cited Thomas Aquinas and Western , were condemned. Palamism became a defining element of Orthodox theology and a later point of East–West theological difference (ch. 6).

Ferrara-Florence (1438–1439)

In the fifteenth century the Ottomans had reduced Byzantium to Constantinople and a few territories. Emperor John VIII Palaiologos, Patriarch Joseph II and a delegation of some 700, including bishops representing the other patriarchs and the Russian Church (led by Isidore of Kiev), came to Italy. The council opened at Ferrara in April 1438 and moved to Florence in January 1439. It was also contending with the rival Council of Basel. Unlike Lyon, it involved more than a year of genuine theological debate on purgatory, the Filioque (both its doctrine and its addition), and primacy. The leading figures were Bessarion of Nicaea, the unionist Isidore, and Mark Eugenikos of Ephesus, the leader of the opposition. Patriarch Joseph II died in Florence on 10 June 1439.2

The decree of union, Laetentur caeli, was signed and promulgated on 6 July 1439. In summary :

  • Filioque. The Latin doctrine was declared legitimate, explained as equivalent to the Greek "through the Son", with the Father as the source. The addition to the creed was declared licit. The Greeks were not required to use it.
  • Bread. Both leavened and unleavened bread are valid.
  • Purgatory. Souls who die in God's love before completing satisfaction are purified after death and helped by the prayers of the living. The decree avoided the image of fire, to which the Greeks objected.
  • Primacy. The Roman pontiff holds primacy over the whole world as successor of Peter, with full power to govern the universal Church, "as is contained also in the acts of the ecumenical councils and in the sacred canons". The rights and privileges of the Eastern patriarchs were reserved.

Mark of Ephesus alone among the Greek bishops refused to sign. He is venerated as a saint in the Orthodox Church. The union was rejected by most clergy and people in Constantinople and by Moscow, where Isidore was arrested on his return in 1441. It was proclaimed in Hagia Sophia only on 12 December 1452. On 29 May 1453 Constantinople fell to Mehmed II. In 1454 the Sultan installed Gennadios Scholarios, a former unionist turned opponent of the union, as patriarch. A of Constantinople in 1484 formally repudiated Florence.3

Scholarly debate: why did Florence fail?

Explanations include:

  • Popular memory of 1204.
  • The perception that the Greeks had signed under political duress and in hope of military aid that came too late (the Crusade of Varna, 1444, ended in disaster).
  • The monastic and lower-clergy resistance led by Mark's followers.
  • The decree's ambiguity: Latins read it as Eastern acceptance of Latin doctrine; Greek unionists read it as Latin recognition of Eastern tradition.
  • Its failure to reckon with how Eastern Churches receive a council: through acceptance by the whole Church, not just the signatures of delegates.4

Later unions

Florence became the model for later partial unions, in which Eastern communities entered communion with Rome while keeping their liturgy and discipline. Examples are the Union of Brest (1595–96) with Ruthenian bishops in the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, the Union of Uzhhorod (1646), and the division of 1724. These unions produced today's Eastern Catholic Churches. Orthodox critics call such unions "". The Joint Commission's Balamand document (1993) rejected uniatism as a method for the future while recognising the right of existing Eastern Catholic Churches to exist. In 1755 the patriarchs of Constantinople, Alexandria and Jerusalem required Latin converts to be rebaptised. This was a sign of how far estrangement had gone. These developments are treated in Volume XIII.
