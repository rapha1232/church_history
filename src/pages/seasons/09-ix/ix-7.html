The most accurate answer is that the schism was a process rather than an event. Communion between Rome and Constantinople broke several times before 1054. After 1054 it did not break completely or everywhere at once: in some places, Latins and Greeks still shared sacraments into the eighteenth century. By the late fifteenth century, both Churches nevertheless regarded themselves as separated.1

In depth

Four ways historians date the schism

1054. The traditional date, used in most textbooks, focuses on the excommunication laid on the altar of Hagia Sophia by Cardinal Humbert and the 's counter-excommunication of the legates. Critics point out that the legates excommunicated the patriarch and his supporters, not the Eastern Church, and that the pope who sent them had already died.

1204. Many historians, including Steven Runciman in The Eastern Schism (1955), argue that the sack of Constantinople by the Fourth Crusade and the installation of Latin patriarchs in Constantinople, Antioch and Jerusalem made the division real for ordinary people. From then on there were rival hierarchies in the same cities.

1484 or 1755. Others look to the formal Orthodox rejection of the Union of Florence by a in Constantinople in 1484, or to the decree of 1755 under Patriarch Cyril V requiring Latin converts to be rebaptized, which treated Western sacraments as invalid. On the Catholic side, the growth of Eastern Catholic Churches from 1596 onward was experienced by the Orthodox as an attack.

A process. Most recent scholarship, for example Tia Kolbaba and Henry Chadwick, treats the schism as a long process with no single date, in which relations varied from region to region. In southern Italy, the Crusader states and the Venetian islands, Greeks and Latins lived together under various arrangements for centuries.

Why it matters today

Whether the schism happened in 1054 or later affects how both churches see the possibility of reunion.
