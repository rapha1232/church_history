Byzantine sources report that the name of the pope was not commemorated in Constantinople from about 1009, under Pope Sergius IV. One explanation offered is that his letter of enthronement contained the . The evidence is late and thin, and whether this was a deliberate break or neglect is unclear.1 In the 1020s, Eustathius and Pope John XIX discussed a formula recognising Constantinople's autonomy within its sphere. The negotiations collapsed in the face of reformist protest in the West.

The reform papacy

From 1046 a series of German-sponsored popes undertook a programme of reform. Its targets were simony (the buying of church offices) and clerical marriage. Its aim was the "freedom of the Church" from lay control. The movement is named after its greatest representative, Gregory VII (1073–1085). Leo IX (1049–1054) was its first great figure. He travelled widely and held synods. His closest adviser was Humbert of Silva Candida, a learned and combative cardinal from Lorraine. The reformers held an exalted view of Roman authority, informed in part by forgeries such as the Donation of Constantine and the Pseudo-Isidorian decretals. They tended to see Eastern differences, such as married priests, as abuses requiring correction.2

Southern Italy

Byzantium still ruled Apulia and Calabria, where Greek and Latin rite communities lived side by side. Norman adventurers were carving out lordships at the expense of both Byzantium and local Lombard princes. Leo IX allied with the Byzantine governor Argyros against them. On 18 June 1053, at Civitate, the Normans destroyed the papal army and captured the pope. Leo was held at Benevento until March 1054 and died in Rome on 19 April 1054. Meanwhile, the reform papacy was introducing Latin practices, including and celibacy, to the Greek communities of southern Italy.

Michael Cerularius and Leo of Ohrid

Michael Cerularius, patriarch 1043–1058, was a forceful aristocrat. He is reported to have closed the Latin-rite churches in Constantinople, probably in 1052–53, in reaction to latinisation in southern Italy. In 1053 Leo, archbishop of Ohrid, wrote a letter, probably at Cerularius' prompting, to John, bishop of Trani in Apulia, addressed to "all the bishops of the Franks and the most reverend pope". It attacked the Latin use of unleavened bread and Saturday fasting as Jewish practices. Humbert translated it into Latin and composed sharp replies. Cerularius and Emperor Constantine IX, who needed papal support against the Normans, then sent conciliatory letters to Rome. Leo IX responded by sending a legation.3

In depth

Normans, azymes and reforming popes

In the 1040s Norman adventurers conquered much of southern Italy, where Greek-speaking churches had long been under Constantinople. Pope Leo IX, a reformer from Alsace, led an army against them and was defeated and captured at Civitate in June 1053. Both pope and Byzantine emperor now had reason to cooperate against the Normans.

Church issues pulled the other way. As the Normans and reforming popes imposed Latin customs in the south, Patriarch Michael Cerularius closed the Latin churches in Constantinople. In 1053 Archbishop Leo of Ohrid wrote a letter attacking Latin practices, above all the use of unleavened bread (azymes) in the Eucharist, which Greeks regarded as Jewish, and fasting on Saturdays.

The reform papacy, from Leo IX onward, insisted on Rome's authority over the whole Church with new energy. Cardinal Humbert of Silva Candida, Leo's adviser, wrote sharp replies defending Latin customs and Roman . Each side treated its own liturgical custom as the norm and the other's as error.

Leo IX sent Humbert to Constantinople in 1054 to negotiate. The mission's failure produced the famous excommunications, but the conflict over bread, the Filioque and authority had been building for decades.
