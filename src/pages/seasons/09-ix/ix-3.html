After 1054: Crusades and the sack of Constantinople

Starring

Continuing contact

Relations continued after 1054. Gregory VII contemplated sending aid to Byzantium after Manzikert (1071). In 1089 Emperor Alexios I convened a at Pope Urban II's request. According to the account reconstructed by Walther Holtzmann, it found no written record of a canonical break with Rome and expressed willingness to restore the pope's name to the once he sent a profession of faith.1 Urban preached the First Crusade at Clermont in November 1095, partly in response to Alexios' request for military help.

The Crusades and local schism

The Crusades brought large numbers of Latins and Greeks into contact, and often into conflict. The decisive ecclesiastical change came in the conquered territories:

  • At Antioch (1098) and Jerusalem (1099) the crusaders installed Latin patriarchs, and the Greek patriarchs went into exile in Constantinople. For the first time there were rival patriarchs of the same see, one Latin and one Greek. Steven Runciman argued that this, rather than 1054, is when the schism became real for ordinary Christians in the East.
  • At the Council of Bari (1098), Anselm of Canterbury defended the before Greek bishops of southern Italy.
  • In the 1100s, exchanges of theological debate (Anselm of Havelberg's debates at Constantinople in 1136) coexisted with growing popular hostility.
  • In 1182 the Latin (largely Italian) population of Constantinople was massacred during a political coup.

1204

The Fourth Crusade set out to attack Egypt. Unable to pay Venice for its fleet, it was diverted first to the Christian city of Zara (1202). It then went to Constantinople to restore a deposed Byzantine prince, Alexios IV, who promised money and church union. When Alexios was overthrown and his promises unpaid, the crusaders stormed the city on 12–13 April 1204. Three days of plunder followed, including the desecration of Hagia Sophia. The Venetian Thomas Morosini was installed as Latin . A Latin Empire ruled Constantinople until 1261, when Michael VIII Palaiologos recovered it. Byzantine Greek government continued in exile at Nicaea, Epirus and Trebizond.2

Innocent III had forbidden attacks on Christians and condemned the sack in strong terms when he learned of it. He nonetheless accepted the new Latin hierarchy as a means of bringing the Greek Church under Rome. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215), canon 4, rebuked Greeks who rebaptised Latins or washed altars after Latin masses. Canon 5 ranked the patriarchates with Constantinople second, meaning in practice its Latin patriarch.

Most historians regard 1204 as the event that made the schism irreversible in Greek popular feeling. Union with Rome came to mean submission to those who had sacked the city.

In depth

Crusades, Latin patriarchs and the wound of 1204

In 1095 Pope Urban II answered an appeal for help from Emperor Alexios I with a call to arms at Clermont. The First Crusade took Antioch (1098) and Jerusalem (1099). Instead of restoring Greek bishops, the crusaders installed Latin patriarchs in both cities, creating rival hierarchies on the ground.

Distrust grew over the twelfth century. In 1182 a mob in Constantinople massacred many of the city's Latin residents. Westerners suspected Byzantium of hindering the crusades; Byzantines saw Latins as arrogant and dangerous.

The Fourth Crusade, deep in debt to Venice, was diverted first to the Christian city of Zara (1202) and then to Constantinople to install the young Alexios IV. When he could not pay, the crusaders stormed the city in April 1204 and sacked it for three days, looting churches, relics and treasures. A Latin empire and a Latin patriarch ruled the city until 1261. For Orthodox Christians, 1204 made the schism real.

Reunion attempts followed, at Lyon in 1274 and Florence in 1439, but neither was accepted by the Orthodox faithful. In 2001 Pope John Paul II expressed sorrow for the sack, and in 2004 Patriarch Bartholomew accepted the apology.
