The events of July 1054

Starring

The legates were Cardinal Humbert, Frederick of Lorraine (chancellor of the Roman Church, later Pope Stephen IX), and Archbishop Peter of Amalfi. They reached Constantinople in April 1054. Their letters, drafted by Humbert, demanded that the submit to Rome and denied him the title "ecumenical". The emperor received them honourably. Cerularius refused to deal with them, questioning their credentials. He suspected collusion between them and Argyros, his enemy. Humbert debated publicly with the monk Niketas Stethatos, who retracted his treatise against the Latins under imperial pressure.

16 July 1054

On Saturday 16 July 1054, as the afternoon liturgy was about to begin, the legates entered Hagia Sophia and laid a bull of excommunication on the altar. They then left, shaking the dust from their feet. The bull, written by Humbert, excommunicated Michael Cerularius, Leo of Ohrid, the patriarchal official Constantine, and their followers. It praised the emperor and the citizens of Constantinople as orthodox. Among its charges, it compared the Greeks to a series of ancient heretics. It also accused them of having removed the from the creed, inverting the actual history.1

Leo IX had died three months earlier. Canonists have argued that the legates' mandate lapsed on his death, so that the excommunication was of doubtful validity. In 1054 no successor had yet been elected (Victor II took office in 1055).

The patriarch's response

After an attempt to recall the legates for a meeting failed, rioting broke out. The emperor allowed Cerularius to act. A in Constantinople (with a document dated 20/24 July) anathematised the authors of the bull and those who had helped them, and ordered the bull burned. It did not anathematise the pope or the Latin Church.2

What was, and was not, excommunicated

  • Both sentences were directed at persons, not at Churches.
  • Neither side declared the other Church heretical as a whole. Neither required other patriarchs to break communion.
  • Patriarch Peter III of Antioch, consulted by Cerularius, urged restraint. He wrote that the Latins were brothers, that most of their customs were tolerable, and that only the Filioque was serious.
  • Contemporaries in the West paid little attention to the episode. Many Byzantine chroniclers do not mention it.

The 1965 joint declaration (ch. 5) acknowledged this. It described the sentences as directed against persons and not intended to break ecclesiastical communion between the sees.

Key takeaways

  • 1054 was an exchange of personal excommunications between a papal legate and a patriarch. The pope who sent the legate was already dead, and the patriarch did not anathematise the Latin Church.
  • It became symbolically decisive only later, when it was remembered as the start of a schism that subsequent events made real.

In depth

Sixteenth of July 1054: what happened and what it meant

The papal delegation was led by Cardinal Humbert, with Frederick of Lorraine (later Pope Stephen IX) and Archbishop Peter of Amalfi. Emperor Constantine IX received them warmly, but Patriarch Michael Cerularius refused to deal with them. Pope Leo IX died in April 1054, which arguably ended the legates' authority, but they stayed on.

On Saturday 16 July 1054 the legates laid a bull of excommunication against Cerularius and his supporters on the altar of Hagia Sophia during the liturgy. The text accused them of many errors, including, ironically, removing the Filioque from the creed.

On 24 July a patriarchal synod condemned the bull and anathematized the legates who had written it, not the pope or the Latin Church as a whole. Neither side excommunicated the other church. Contemporaries did not see 1054 as a final break; contacts, negotiations and even communion continued in many places.

On 7 December 1965 Pope Paul VI and Patriarch Athenagoras I declared that they 'regret' and 'commit to oblivion' the excommunications of 1054. The gesture removed a symbol, but the underlying questions of authority and doctrine remain.
