How the Church was organised before 313

c. 150–3133 minPreviously: Beyond the Greco-Roman centres

By Constantine's time the Church already had a recognisable institutional structure, developed without state support.

Starring

By Constantine's time the Church already had a recognisable institutional structure, developed without state support.1

  • Synods. Bishops met in councils to handle common problems: the New Prophecy (Asia, 170s), the date of Easter (c. 190, across several regions, Eusebius HE 5.23), the lapsed (Carthage and Rome, 251), rebaptism (Carthage, 255–256), and Paul of Samosata (Antioch, 268). These meetings are the direct ancestors of the councils of Volume VI.
  • Regional hierarchy. Bishops of major cities (Rome, Alexandria, Antioch, Carthage) exercised informal leadership over wider regions. Nicaea canon 6 later called this "ancient custom" (VII.2).
  • Communication. Letters of communion and peace allowed Christians to travel and be received. Bishops informed each other of their election.
  • Property. Churches owned cemeteries and buildings. Gallienus' restitution (260) and Aurelian's ruling in the Paul of Samosata case (272) show that the state recognised Christian corporate property. Aurelian ordered the church building "to be given to those to whom the bishops of Italy and of the city of Rome should adjudge it" (Eusebius, HE 7.30.19, NPNF II/1).
  • Charity. Organised care for widows, orphans, the sick, prisoners and the dead, as in Rome in 251 (ch. 8). Pagan observers, including the emperor Julian a century later, remarked on it.
  • Size. Christians were perhaps a tenth of the empire's population by 300, unevenly distributed. This is an estimate, not a count (IV.1).2

This is the Church Constantine encountered in 312: organised, divided by its own disputes, and practised in settling them through bishops in council. Volume IV continues the story.

In depth

Numbers, money and synods before Constantine

How many Christians were there? No census exists, so all figures are estimates. A widely discussed model (Rodney Stark, 1996) suggests growth of about 40 per cent a decade, from perhaps a thousand or so in 40 to over six million by 300, around a tenth of the empire. Other historians think the figure for 300 was lower. Christians were concentrated in cities and in the East: Asia Minor, Syria, Egypt and North Africa had far more than Gaul or Spain.

Churches held property, though under Roman law their legal status was uncertain. The letter of Licinius in 313 (see the full text) orders the return of buildings 'which belonged not to individuals but to their society', which shows that churches owned assembly places. Bishop Cornelius of Rome wrote around 251 that his church supported 46 presbyters, 7 deacons, 7 subdeacons, 42 acolytes, 52 exorcists, readers and doorkeepers, and more than 1,500 widows and people in need.

Bishops met in regional synods to settle disputes, a practice visible in the Easter controversy of the 190s, in Carthage under Cyprian in the 250s, and at Antioch against Paul of Samosata in 268. The bishop of the provincial capital (metropolis) gradually acquired a presiding role; Nicaea would later confirm this (VII.2). Letters between churches, carried by travellers, held this network together.

Why it matters today

How dioceses, metropolitans and synods relate is still a live question in Catholic–Orthodox dialogue and in church disputes over authority.
