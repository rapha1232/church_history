The Apologists

Starring

From the reign of Hadrian, educated Christians addressed "apologies" (defences) to emperors and the public.1 Major writers include:

  • Quadratus and Aristides (c. 125–140), in Athens;
  • Justin Martyr (First and Second Apology, c. 150–155; Dialogue with Trypho), a convert from Platonism teaching in Rome;
  • Tatian (Oration to the Greeks; and the Diatessaron, a harmony of the four Gospels, influential in Syriac Christianity);
  • Athenagoras of Athens (c. 177);
  • Theophilus of Antioch (c. 180), who first used the Greek trias of God, his Word and his Wisdom;
  • Minucius Felix and Tertullian in Latin.

The answered the charges of atheism, immorality and disloyalty. They argued that Christianity was the true philosophy and the fulfilment of Hebrew prophecy, older than the Greek thinkers. Justin developed the idea of the Logos spermatikos, the "seed-bearing Word": whatever truth the philosophers had spoken came from the who became incarnate in Christ (2 Apology 13).

Justin also gives the earliest detailed description of Christian Sunday worship:

And on the day called Sunday, all who live in cities or in the country gather together to one place, and the memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read, as long as time permits; then, when the reader has ceased, the president verbally instructs, and exhorts to the imitation of these good things.

Justin Martyr, First Apology 67.3–4 (c. 155). ANF 1 (1885). Checked. Q52 · checked

Note "the president" (proestōs), the person who presides, as opposed to the reader. Scholars debate whether Justin means a bishop in the later sense.2

In depth

What the Apologists argued, and why

The Apologists were second-century writers who addressed defences (apologiai) of Christianity to emperors, officials or educated pagans. The main names are Aristides and Quadratus (under Hadrian), Justin Martyr (two Apologies, c. 150–155, and the Dialogue with Trypho), his pupil Tatian, Athenagoras (Plea for the Christians, c. 177), Theophilus of Antioch, and in Latin Minucius Felix and Tertullian.

They answered three popular charges: atheism (Christians refused to honour the gods), cannibalism (a misunderstanding of the ) and incest (a misunderstanding of 'brothers and sisters' and the kiss of peace). They argued that Christians were loyal subjects who prayed for the emperor, that their morals were higher than those of their accusers, and that punishing people for the name alone was unjust.

Their more lasting contribution was intellectual. Justin used the Stoic and Platonic idea of the Logos, the divine reason, to explain Christ: the same Logos had sown 'seeds of truth' in philosophers like Socrates and was fully revealed in Jesus. This gave Christians a way to claim the best of Greek thought while asserting that it pointed to Christ. It also raised the question, pressed in the third and fourth centuries, of how the Logos relates to God the Father: is he a second, lesser god, or God in the full sense? The Arian controversy (IV.5) grew partly out of that question.

Not all Apologists were friendly to philosophy. Tatian attacked Greek culture as a whole, and Tertullian later asked 'What has Athens to do with Jerusalem?' The tension between those two attitudes runs through Christian history.

Why it matters today

The Apologists started Christianity’s long conversation with philosophy and public reason, a conversation every attempt to explain faith in public continues.
