In depth

The Apostolic Fathers: what each writing tells us

'Apostolic Fathers' is a modern label (from the seventeenth century) for early Christian writings outside the New Testament that were thought to come from people who had known the apostles. The collection usually includes: 1 Clement, a letter from the church of Rome to Corinth (c. 96); the seven letters of Ignatius of Antioch, written on his way to martyrdom in Rome (c. 110, though some scholars argue for later); Polycarp's Letter to the Philippians and the Martyrdom of Polycarp; the Didache, a church manual; the Epistle of Barnabas; the Shepherd of Hermas, a long book of visions from Rome; the Epistle to Diognetus; and fragments of Papias.

Together they show the second generation dealing with practical questions. 1 Clement argues that the Corinthians should not depose their presbyters, appealing to order and succession from the apostles. Ignatius argues for a single bishop in each church, with presbyters and deacons, as the sign of unity against teachers who denied that Christ truly suffered in the flesh (docetism). The Didache describes baptism in running water, fasting on Wednesdays and Fridays, a eucharistic prayer and travelling prophets who must be tested.

These texts also show variety. Rome in 1 Clement and Hermas seems still to be led by a group of presbyters, while Ignatius already assumes a single bishop in Asia Minor. Barnabas reads the Old Testament allegorically against Jewish interpretation. Hermas allows one repentance after baptism, an early sign of the later penitential system.

Why it matters today

The Didache, Clement and Ignatius are quoted in debates over bishops, the Eucharist and church order, as the earliest witnesses after the apostles.
