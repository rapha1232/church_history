Irenaeus: rule of faith, Scripture and succession

c. 170–2002 minPreviously: Marcion, “Gnosticism” and Montanism

Irenaeus came from Asia Minor and in his youth heard Polycarp. He became bishop of Lyons after the persecution of 177.

Starring

Irenaeus came from Asia Minor and in his youth heard Polycarp. He became bishop of Lyons after the persecution of 177. His Against Heresies (c. 180) is the first comprehensive refutation of Valentinian and other teachers. It set out three criteria that became fundamental to later Christianity.

  1. The rule of faith (regula fidei, Greek kanōn tēs alētheias): a summary of belief in one God the Creator, in Christ incarnate for our salvation, and in the Holy Spirit, held by the churches everywhere (AH 1.10.1). It is a forerunner of the creeds.1
  2. The Scriptures of both Testaments, including exactly four Gospels (Q57).
  3. Apostolic succession. The public succession of bishops in churches founded by apostles guarantees that their teaching is the apostles' teaching, in contrast to teachers who claimed secret traditions. Rome serves as his model example (Q28; see VII.1).

Irenaeus' theology of "recapitulation" held that Christ sums up and restores the whole of human history in himself. This became a lasting resource for Eastern and Western theology. In the Quartodeciman dispute he also mediated against Victor of Rome (VII.1).2

In depth

Irenaeus' answers to the Gnostics

Irenaeus grew up in Smyrna, where he heard Polycarp, and became bishop of Lyon after the persecution there in 177. His five-book Against Heresies (c. 180) targeted especially the Valentinian teachers, whose systems described a series of divine emanations, a flawed creator god and a saving secret knowledge.

He used three linked arguments. First, the : a short summary of belief in one God, creator of all, in Christ who became flesh, and in the Spirit who spoke through the prophets, taught to every church at baptism. Second, Scripture: Irenaeus is the first writer to insist on exactly four Gospels and to read the Old and New Testaments as one story. Third, succession: the apostles handed their teaching to bishops in the churches they founded, who taught openly; there was no secret tradition. He illustrated this with the list of Roman bishops (see VII.1).

Irenaeus' own theology centres on 'recapitulation'. Christ sums up and repeats the human story, succeeding where Adam failed, so that humanity can grow into the likeness of God. He stresses the goodness of matter and the body against the Gnostic contempt for the material world, and the resurrection of the flesh. His famous line 'the glory of God is a living human being' (Against Heresies 4.20.7) comes from this vision.

Why it matters today

Irenaeus’ appeal to Scripture, the rule of faith and succession is still the backbone of how churches decide what counts as authentic teaching.
