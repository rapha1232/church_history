Starring

Syria and Edessa

Edessa (Urhay) became the centre of Syriac-speaking Christianity. The legend of King Abgar's correspondence with Jesus (Eusebius, HE 1.13; the Doctrine of Addai) is legendary. A Christian church at Edessa is recorded in a flood of 201, and the philosopher Bardaisan (d. 222) wrote at the royal court. Tatian's Diatessaron was the Syriac churches' gospel until the fifth century. Early Syriac poetry (Odes of Solomon) shows a distinctive, Semitic Christianity.

Mesopotamia and Persia

Christian communities in Adiabene and Mesopotamia by the late second or third century; Roman captives deported by Shapur I. Mani (216–274/277), founder of Manichaeism, grew up in a Jewish-Christian baptist sect (the Elchasaites). See V.1.

Egypt

Early evidence is scanty until Bishop Demetrius (189–231/2), though Christian papyri from Oxyrhynchus and elsewhere attest to Christians from the 2nd century. Coptic translations of Scripture had appeared by the 3rd century.1

North Africa

Latin Christianity's first centre: the Scillitan martyrs (180), Tertullian and Cyprian. Councils at Carthage gathered dozens of bishops; in 256, 87 bishops were present.2 The density of African sees, several hundred by 300, was unique.

Armenia and the Caucasus

Royal conversion under Tiridates III through Gregory the Illuminator, traditionally 301, often dated c. 314 by historians. Georgia followed in the 330s (V.2).

Gaul, Spain and Britain

Lyons and Vienne (177). Spanish bishops at the Council of Elvira (date debated, c. 300–314). Three British bishops at Arles (314).3

In depth

Christianity beyond the Roman Empire before 313

By the third century Christianity had spread well beyond the Greek and Latin cities. In Syriac-speaking Mesopotamia, Edessa (Urfa) was an early centre. The legend of King Abgar's letter to Jesus is late, but Christians were there by about 200: a flood chronicle records the destruction of a church building in 201, and the philosopher Bardaisan wrote in Syriac at the Edessan court. The Diatessaron, Tatian's harmony of the four Gospels, was the standard Gospel text in Syriac churches for two centuries.

At Dura-Europos on the Euphrates, excavations uncovered a house converted into a church around 240, with a baptistery decorated with paintings of the Good Shepherd and the healing of the paralytic. The synagogue in the same town had even more extensive paintings, a reminder that Jews and Christians lived side by side.

Armenia's tradition holds that King Tiridates III was converted by Gregory the Illuminator and made Christianity the religion of the kingdom, traditionally in 301; many historians prefer a date around 314, which would place it after Constantine. Georgia and Aksum (Ethiopia) followed in the fourth century (XIII.4).

Within the Persian Empire, Christians included deportees from Roman Syria taken captive in the wars of the 250s. This community became the Church of the East (V.1). Once Rome became Christian, Persian Christians were open to the charge of being a fifth column, and the great persecution under Shapur II (from 340) followed.
