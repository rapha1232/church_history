Persecution and martyrdom (c. 100–311)

c. 100–3113 minPreviously: The Apostolic Fathers

Popular imagination pictures the early Christians in hiding, under constant persecution.

Starring

Popular imagination pictures the early Christians in hiding, under constant persecution. The evidence shows something different: long periods of local tolerance, broken by local outbreaks of violence and, after 250, by a few empire-wide campaigns.

The legal position

Around 112, Pliny the Younger, governor of Bithynia-Pontus, asked the emperor Trajan how to treat people accused of being Christians. Trajan's reply set the pattern for over a century. Christians were not to be hunted out, and anonymous accusations were to be ignored. Those formally accused who refused to sacrifice to the gods and the emperor's image were to be punished, but those who recanted were to be pardoned (Pliny, Ep. 10.96–97). Being a Christian was thus punishable "for the name" itself, yet not actively pursued.1 Why Christians were hated is a separate question. They refused to honour the gods on whom civic welfare depended, which made them "atheists". Rumours spread of incest and cannibalism, distortions of the "love-feast" and the . They were also a new, unregistered association.

Principal episodes

  • c. 155–167: Polycarp at Smyrna.

    Eighty and six years have I served Him, and He never did me any injury: how then can I blaspheme my King and my Saviour?

    Martyrdom of Polycarp 9.3. ANF 1 (1885). Checked. Q54 · checked
  • c. 165: Justin Martyr and companions at Rome. Their trial record (Acta Iustini) survives in several recensions.
  • 177: Lyons and Vienne, in a letter preserved in Eusebius, HE 5.1.
  • 180: The Scillitan martyrs of North Africa. Their trial record is the earliest dated Christian document in Latin.
  • 203: Perpetua and Felicity at Carthage, whose Passion contains Perpetua's own prison diary. This is one of the earliest surviving texts by a Christian woman.
  • 250–251, Decius. The first empire-wide requirement for all inhabitants to sacrifice. Certificates (libelli) on papyrus survive from Egypt. Many Christians complied or bought certificates. Bishop Fabian of Rome was executed.2
  • 257–260, Valerian. Measures targeting clergy and Christian property. Cyprian of Carthage (258) and Sixtus II of Rome were executed. Gallienus ended the persecution in 260.
  • 303–311, the Great Persecution. See IV.1.

The oftener we are mown down by you, the more in number we grow; the blood of Christians is seed.

Tertullian, Apology 50.13 (197). Trans. S. Thelwall, ANF 3 (1885). Checked. The popular form “the blood of the martyrs is the seed of the Church” is a later paraphrase. Q55 · checked
Scholarly debate: how many martyrs?

No reliable total exists. Later martyrologies multiplied numbers and invented stories. Modern estimates for the whole pre-Constantinian period range from the low thousands to a few tens of thousands. Candida Moss (2013) argued that many martyr narratives were later inventions and that systematic persecution was rarer than often supposed. Her critics accept the source-critical point but stress the reality and trauma of the persecutions under Decius, Valerian and Diocletian.3

Persecution shaped Christianity beyond the deaths it caused. It produced the cult of martyrs, their tombs and their anniversaries. It made "confessors" who had survived torture into authorities. And it raised the divisive question of how to treat the "lapsed", which produced the Novatianist (251) and Donatist schisms (ch. 8; IV.4).

Why it matters today

Christians are still persecuted in many countries, and the martyrs’ example shapes how churches remember suffering and witness.
