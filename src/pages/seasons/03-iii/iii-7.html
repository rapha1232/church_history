Monarchianism and the Logos

Starring

How can there be one God, the "monarchy" (single rule), if the Son is also God? Two "monarchian" answers, later rejected, prepared the ground for the fourth-century debates.1

  • Modalism ("", "Patripassianism"): Father, Son and Spirit are names or modes of one divine person, so that "the Father suffered". Noetus of Smyrna, Praxeas and Sabellius taught versions of this in Rome c. 190–220.

    He alleged that Christ was the Father Himself, and that the Father Himself was born, and suffered, and died.

    Hippolytus, Against Noetus 1 (early 3rd century; attribution debated). ANF 5 (1886). Opening of the sentence checked; its continuation is quoted from the same text. Q59 · checked
  • Adoptionism ("dynamic "): Jesus was a man empowered by God. It was taught by Theodotus "the cobbler" in Rome (c. 190). Paul of Samosata, bishop of Antioch, was deposed in 268 by a for teaching a related view. The synod also reportedly rejected the term homoousios in his sense, which later complicated its use at Nicaea (IV.6).

Against both, theologians developed a " theology". The Word, distinct from the Father, was generated for creation and revelation. This affirmed distinction, but often ranked the Son below the Father. In Rome, Hippolytus accused bishops Zephyrinus and Callistus of modalism and appears to have led a rival community. The authorship of the works under his name, including the Apostolic Tradition, is now much debated.2 In the 260s the "affair of the two Dionysii", bishops of Rome and Alexandria, showed that East and West already had different worries: Rome feared tritheism, Alexandria feared modalism.3

In depth

Two kinds of monarchianism

'Monarchianism' covers attempts in the late second and third centuries to protect the oneness (monarchia) of God. There were two very different kinds.

Dynamic or adoptionist monarchianism held that Jesus was a man empowered by God's Spirit or adopted as Son. Theodotus the cobbler taught this in Rome around 190 and was excommunicated by Bishop Victor. Its most famous representative was Paul of Samosata, bishop of Antioch, deposed by a synod in 268. That synod is said to have rejected the word in Paul's sense, which later made the word suspect to some Easterners at Nicaea.

Modalist monarchianism held that Father, Son and Spirit are three modes or names of the one God, so that the Father himself was born and suffered (hence the label 'patripassianism'). Noetus of Smyrna, Praxeas (Tertullian's target) and Sabellius taught versions of it. Hippolytus accused Bishop Callistus of Rome of sympathy with it.

The responses shaped later doctrine. Tertullian's Against Praxeas (c. 213) introduced Latin terms that stuck: God is one substance (substantia) in three persons (personae), and he used the word trinitas. In the East, Origen spoke of three hypostases. 'Sabellianism' became the standard accusation against anyone who seemed to merge the three, and Nicaea's supporters were regularly accused of it.
