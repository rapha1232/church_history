The threefold ministry

By the late second century, almost every church known to us had a single bishop, with presbyters and deacons. How this happened is debated. Three explanations have been offered:

  • Bishops emerged as presidents of presbyteral colleges, at different speeds in different places. Ignatius' Asia was early, Rome and perhaps Alexandria later.
  • The apostles directly appointed single bishops.
  • The pattern was a response to the need for unity against division.

Catholic and Orthodox teaching holds that the episcopate is of apostolic and divine institution (). Historians agree that by Irenaeus (c. 180) the churches understood their bishops as successors of the apostles.

Other ministries developed alongside. Readers and subdeacons are listed at Rome c. 251, when Bishop Cornelius counted 46 presbyters, 7 deacons, 7 subdeacons, 42 acolytes and 52 exorcists, readers and doorkeepers, with over 1,500 widows and poor supported (Eusebius, HE 6.43.11). There was also an order of widows, and in the East deaconesses (the Didascalia, 3rd century).

Cyprian and the unity of the episcopate

Cyprian, bishop of Carthage (248/9–258), faced two crises: how to readmit those who lapsed under Decius, and the rival Roman bishop Novatian, who refused readmission and founded a rigorist church. Cyprian's On the Unity of the Church (251) taught that "the episcopate is one, each part of which is held by each one for the whole" (De unit. 5)1. There is no salvation outside the Church: "He can no longer have God for his Father, who has not the Church for his mother" (De unit. 6, ANF 5; register Q62). His conflict with Stephen of Rome over heretical baptism is discussed in VII.1.

Worship

The Didache preserves early Eucharistic prayers:

Now concerning the Thanksgiving (), thus give thanks. First, concerning the cup: We thank thee, our Father, for the holy vine of David Thy servant, which Thou madest known to us through Jesus Thy Servant; to Thee be the glory for ever.

Didache 9.1–2. ANF 7 (1886). Checked. Q50 · checked

Justin (c. 155) describes the Eucharist as something received "not as common bread and common drink", but as "the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh" (1 Apol. 66; Q53). The text is quoted in Catholic, Orthodox and Lutheran arguments for Christ's real presence, and read differently by some Reformed scholars.

For not as common bread and common drink do we receive these; but in like manner as Jesus Christ our Saviour, having been made flesh by the Word of God, had both flesh and blood for our salvation, so likewise have we been taught that the food which is blessed by the prayer of His word … is the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh.

Justin Martyr, First Apology 66.2 (c. 155). ANF 1 (1885). Checked; the ellipsis omits a clause. Q53 · checked

Other developments in this period :

  • Infant baptism is attested by c. 200. Tertullian argued against it (On Baptism 18); Origen called it apostolic tradition.
  • Initiation came to involve a long catechumenate, renunciation of Satan, threefold immersion with a creedal questioning, anointing and laying on of hands. Its fullest description is in the Apostolic Tradition, whose date, origin and attribution to Hippolytus are debated.2
  • The earliest securely identified church building is the house-church at Dura-Europos on the Euphrates. It was adapted c. 232/233, with a baptistery decorated with paintings, and buried in 256.
  • Easter was the great feast. Its date was disputed (the Quartodeciman controversy), and it became the main time for baptism.
