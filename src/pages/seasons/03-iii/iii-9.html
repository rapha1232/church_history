The beginnings of asceticism

Starring

Celibacy "for the kingdom" and voluntary poverty were present from the beginning (Matt. 19:12; 1 Cor. 7). By the second and third centuries the following forms are attested :

  • Consecrated virgins and widows living in their families or communities, addressed by Tertullian and Cyprian.1
  • In Syria, the "Encratites" made celibacy a condition of baptism, a view associated with Tatian. The bnay and bnāt qyāmā (sons and daughters of the covenant), described in the fourth century by Aphrahat, had probably earlier roots.2
  • Origen's demanding asceticism, and the Acts of Thomas and Acts of Paul and Thecla, which celebrate renunciation of marriage.
  • By the 270s, individuals were withdrawing to the edges of Egyptian villages and then to the desert. Antony's withdrawal (c. 270–285) is the best-known example (IV.10).3

Peter Brown's The Body and Society (1988) remains the classic account4 of how sexual renunciation became a defining Christian ideal and a source of authority.

In depth

Ascetic Christianity before the monks

Organised monasticism is usually dated to Antony and Pachomius in fourth-century Egypt (IV.10), but ascetic practice is much older. Paul already praised celibacy for those who could bear it (1 Corinthians 7). In the second century 'encratite' groups, associated with Tatian, rejected marriage, meat and wine for all believers; mainstream writers rejected that as a rule but honoured voluntary celibacy.

Consecrated virgins and widows formed recognised groups in many churches. Cyprian of Carthage wrote a treatise on the dress of virgins (c. 249). The popular Acts of Paul and Thecla presented a young woman who rejects marriage to follow Paul. In Syria the 'sons and daughters of the covenant' (bnay qyama) lived celibate lives in service of the local church.

Theological reasons included imitation of Christ, readiness for the coming kingdom, freedom for prayer, and an ideal of the body disciplined by the spirit. Origen is said to have castrated himself (Eusebius reports it, though some doubt it), and his teaching on spiritual ascent influenced later monks deeply. When persecution ended and the Church became respectable, many saw the ascetic life as a new form of martyrdom.
