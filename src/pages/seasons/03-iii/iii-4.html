Marcion, “Gnosticism” and Montanism

Starring

Second-century Christianity was diverse. Several movements offered interpretations that the majority of churches eventually rejected. Their rejection helped define what became "orthodoxy". Walter Bauer's influential thesis (1934) held that "heresy" often preceded "orthodoxy" in particular regions. It has been heavily criticised in its details, but it changed the way historians approach the question.1

Marcion

Marcion, a shipowner from Sinope, broke with the Roman church c. 144. He taught that the God of the Old Testament, a just but harsh creator, was a different being from the good Father revealed by Jesus. He rejected the Jewish scriptures and accepted a single Gospel, an edited Luke, and ten Pauline letters. His church spread widely and lasted for centuries in the East. Our knowledge comes mainly from opponents, above all Tertullian's five books Against Marcion.

“Gnosticism”

Church writers such as Irenaeus and Hippolytus described many teachers: Basilides, Valentinus (in Rome c. 140–160) and his followers Ptolemy and Heracleon, the "Sethians", and others. These teachers offered secret knowledge (gnōsis) of the soul's divine origin and its escape from a material world made by an ignorant or hostile lesser god (the demiurge). The discovery in 1945 of the Nag Hammadi codices, thirteen Coptic books of the fourth century, allowed these traditions to be read in their own words for the first time.

Recent scholarship questions whether "" names a coherent religion. Michael A. Williams argued it should be "dismantled" as a category (1996). Karen King argued that the term is a construction of modern and ancient polemic (2003). Others, such as David Brakke, prefer to speak of a specific "Gnostic school".2 This edition therefore uses the term cautiously.

Montanism (“the New Prophecy”)

Beginning in Phrygia c. 160s–170s, the prophet Montanus and the prophetesses Prisca (Priscilla) and Maximilla proclaimed new revelations of the Paraclete (the Holy Spirit). They called for rigorous fasting and discipline and for readiness for martyrdom. Asian synods condemned the movement. It was probably among the first occasions for synods of bishops (ch. 11). Tertullian joined a rigorist party sympathetic to the New Prophecy later in life, though whether he formally left the Catholic church is debated. The movement raised a question that would recur: is the Spirit's authority found in prophecy, or in Scripture and the episcopal office?

In depth

Why the second-century alternatives mattered

Marcion, a wealthy shipowner from Sinope on the Black Sea, came to Rome and was expelled by its church around 144. He taught that the God of the Old Testament, a just but harsh creator, was a different being from the good Father revealed by Jesus. His scripture consisted of an edited Gospel of Luke and ten letters of Paul. Marcion's church spread widely and lasted for centuries.

Marcion forced the other churches to say which books they accepted and why. Many historians see his list as one stimulus for defining the New Testament canon, and for insisting that the Old Testament belonged to Christian scripture.

The term 'Gnosticism' covers several second-century movements that taught salvation through secret knowledge (gnosis) of a spiritual world from which souls had fallen into matter. Most of what was known about them came from their opponents until 1945, when a jar of fourth-century Coptic books was found at Nag Hammadi in Egypt. It contained the Gospel of Thomas, the Gospel of Truth and many other texts. Some scholars, such as Michael Williams and Karen King, argue that 'Gnosticism' is a modern label lumping together quite different groups.

, or the New Prophecy, began in Phrygia in the 170s when Montanus and two women, Priscilla and Maximilla, prophesied in the name of the Holy Spirit and called for strict discipline. Its most famous convert was Tertullian. The movement's challenge helped the wider Church to stress the authority of bishops and the closure of revelation in the apostolic age.

Why it matters today

Questions raised by Marcion and the Gnostics, about the Old Testament, the body and secret knowledge, resurface in modern spirituality and popular books.
