Tertullian and Latin theology

Tertullian of Carthage (c. 160–c. 225) was the first major Christian author in Latin. He coined or popularised much of the West's theological vocabulary: trinitas, persona, substantia, sacramentum. In Against Praxeas (c. 213) he answered a teacher who identified the Father and Son:

…which distributes the Unity into a Trinity, placing in their order the three Persons — the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost: three, however, not in condition, but in degree; not in substance, but in form; not in power, but in aspect; yet of one substance, and of one condition, and of one power, inasmuch as He is one God…

Tertullian, Against Praxeas 2. Trans. Peter Holmes, ANF 3 (1885). Checked. Q56 · checked

The formula "one substance, three persons" anticipates later orthodoxy. However, Tertullian's thought also contains subordinationist elements, which judged by fourth-century standards would be inadequate. This shows that the doctrine of the Trinity developed over time, without being "invented" at any single moment (Misconceptions).

Alexandria: Clement and Origen

Alexandria had a tradition of Christian teaching that came under episcopal supervision by about 200. Its relation to the bishop is debated.1 Clement of Alexandria (c. 150–c. 215) presented Christianity as the true gnōsis and philosophy as a preparation for the Gospel.

Origen (c. 185–c. 254) was the greatest scholar of the early Church. His works include:

  • the Hexapla, a six-column comparison of the Hebrew Old Testament and its Greek translations;
  • commentaries and homilies on most of Scripture;
  • On First Principles, the first systematic theology;
  • Against Celsus, a reply to a pagan critic.

His preface to On First Principles distinguishes the "apostolic teaching" held by the Churches from questions left open to inquiry. He then explores speculative answers: the pre-existence of souls, and the possible final restoration of all things (apokatastasis).

Origen fell out with Bishop Demetrius of Alexandria, who objected to his ordination as presbyter in Palestine. He moved to Caesarea (c. 231–234) and died after torture under Decius. His legacy was immense: both Arius and Athanasius, and the Cappadocians, drew on him. But some of his ideas, or those of later "Origenists", were condemned in 400, 543 and 553 (VI.5). Modern scholarship stresses the gap between Origen's exploratory theology and the later Origenism that was condemned.2

In depth

Philosophy, Scripture and the first systematic theology

Tertullian of Carthage (c. 155–c. 220) was the first major Christian author in Latin. He coined or popularised much of the West's theological vocabulary: 'trinity' (trinitas), 'person' (persona) and 'substance' (substantia), especially in Against Praxeas (c. 213). He asked 'What has Athens to do with Jerusalem?' (Prescription Against Heretics 7), yet used Stoic ideas and Roman legal argument throughout his work.

Clement of Alexandria (c. 150–c. 215) took a warmer view of Greek learning. In his Stromata ('Miscellanies') philosophy is a schoolmaster preparing the Greeks for Christ, as the Law prepared the Jews. His ideal is the 'true gnostic', the mature Christian whose knowledge is grounded in faith and love.

Origen (c. 185–c. 253) was the greatest scholar of the early Church. His Hexapla set the Hebrew text of the Old Testament beside Greek versions in six columns. On First Principles was the first attempt at a systematic Christian theology. He read Scripture on several levels, literal, moral and spiritual, and his commentaries and homilies shaped exegesis for centuries. Driven from Alexandria, he taught at Caesarea in Palestine and died after torture in the Decian persecution.

Some of Origen's speculations, such as the pre-existence of souls and the hope that all might finally be restored, were attacked in the controversies of around 400 and condemned in the sixth century. Yet Athanasius, the Cappadocians and Jerome all drew on his work.

Why it matters today

Origen’s methods of reading Scripture and Tertullian’s Latin vocabulary (“Trinity”, “person”, “substance”) are still in use.
