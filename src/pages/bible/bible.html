Why the Bibles of the churches differ

All Christians share the 27 books of the New Testament. The difference lies in the Old Testament. Early Christians mostly read it in Greek, in the Septuagint, which included books and passages not found in the later Hebrew canon: Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, 1–2 Maccabees and additions to Esther and Daniel. Jerome, translating the Vulgate around 400, preferred the Hebrew and called the extra books “apocrypha”, but translated several of them; councils at Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) listed them as canonical.

At the Reformation, Luther placed these books in a separate section, “useful and good to read” but not equal to Scripture, and later Protestant Bibles dropped them altogether. The Council of Trent (1546) affirmed them as fully canonical: Catholics call them deuterocanonical. The Orthodox churches, following the Septuagint, read a slightly wider set (including 1 Esdras, 3 Maccabees, the Prayer of Manasseh and Psalm 151), confirmed at the Synod of Jerusalem (1672), while giving them a secondary place as anagignoskomena, books “to be read”.
