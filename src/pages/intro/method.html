The problem of sources for this period

The history of the councils is unusually well documented for antiquity. For Ephesus (431), Chalcedon (451), Constantinople II (553), Constantinople III (680–81) and Nicaea II (787), large parts of the minutes (acta) survive. They record speeches, acclamations, the reading of documents and lists of signatories. The standard critical edition is Eduard Schwartz's Acta Conciliorum Oecumenicorum (from 1914), and Richard Price and his collaborators have produced annotated English translations in the Translated Texts for Historians series.1 For Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381), no minutes survive. Historians rely on the creed, the canons, a synodal letter, and the accounts of participants such as Eusebius of Caesarea and Athanasius and of fifth-century church historians: Socrates, Sozomen and Theodoret.

These sources share three limitations that the reader should keep in mind:

  • The winners kept the records. Most surviving texts were copied by communities that accepted a council's outcome. The writings of Arius, Nestorius, Eutyches, and the opponents of icons survive mainly as hostile quotations, though important exceptions exist, such as Nestorius' own Book of Heracleides, preserved in Syriac.
  • The acta are edited documents. They were compiled, sometimes revised, and transmitted in Greek, Latin, Syriac and other versions that do not always agree.
  • Later tradition shaped earlier memory. Round numbers (318 fathers at Nicaea, 150 at Constantinople, 630 at Chalcedon) and tidy narratives grew up after the events. They tell us how a council was remembered, which is itself historically important, but they are not always reliable records.

Approach

Each chapter starts from the primary evidence, gives the range of current scholarly interpretation, and reports separately how Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox and Church of the East traditions understand the events, along with Protestant traditions where relevant. Terms that later became party labels ("Arian", "Nestorian", "Monophysite") are used with care: they are often names opponents used, and modern scholarship has shown they can misdescribe the people they label. The glossary explains each one.

Dates are AD/CE. Where a date is disputed, the range is given and the dispute is noted.

Source standards (Edition 4)

Every footnote and reference in this edition points to one of five kinds of source. Each bibliography entry is labelled with its kind:

  • Primary sources and official texts: Scripture; ancient and medieval authors cited by book and chapter; council acts and canons (Tanner's Decrees of the Ecumenical Councils); papal and curial documents (cited by Denzinger–Hünermann number, DH, where possible); confessions of faith (the Book of Concord, Schaff's Creeds of Christendom); and joint declarations and statistics published by the Churches themselves.
  • University-press monographs (Oxford, Cambridge, Harvard, Yale, Princeton, Chicago, California and others).
  • Peer-reviewed articles and reference works, including the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy.
  • Academic publishers (Brill, de Gruyter, Eerdmans, Fortress, Blackwell and similar).
  • Trade books by recognised specialists. A few standard works by leading scholars appeared with general publishers. They are labelled as such and are never the only support for a contested claim.

News reports are not used as authorities. They were used only to find official texts. For a few events of 2024–2026 in Volume XV, news reporting is still the only available record. Those statements are kept brief and general. Secondary works are cited as whole works or by volume, never by invented page numbers. The editorial report lists what was verified and what was corrected.
