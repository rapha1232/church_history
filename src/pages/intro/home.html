What this edition is

Edition 5 adds a redesigned reading layout, “In depth” sections in 90 chapters, and a library of 25 complete creeds, conciliar definitions and confessions with a built-in reader. Each chapter that discusses one of these texts now links to it directly.

This edition contains all fifteen planned volumes:

  • Volumes I–III: the background to Christianity, Jesus and the apostolic age, and the Church before Constantine.
  • Volumes IV–IX: the imperial Church, the councils and the East–West schism. These are the most detailed volumes, with each council analysed under eleven headings.
  • Volumes X–XV: the medieval Church, the Protestant and Catholic Reformations, Eastern Christianity beyond Constantinople (with a dedicated chapter on the Antiochian and Maronite Churches), the modern world, and the twentieth and twenty-first centuries.

Part III profiles fifteen Christian traditions and compares twelve of them on twenty-four topics. Part IV gathers specialised studies. Volumes X–XV cover very long periods and are written at survey depth. The editorial report explains what has been verified and what remains to deepen.

The aim is historical understanding supported by evidence, not religious advocacy. The text tries throughout to separate three things that popular accounts often blur together:

  • What the surviving sources show: documents, council acts, letters, inscriptions.
  • What historians infer, including where they agree and where they disagree.
  • What particular Churches teach about these events. These teachings are reported accurately and in their own terms, but they are not presented as historical findings.

How to read the evidence labels

Important claims carry a small label showing what kind of claim they are. Tap or hover a label for its definition.

Supported by contemporary or near-contemporary sources whose authenticity is not seriously disputed.
An inference accepted by a clear majority of specialists, even though the sources leave room for argument.
A question on which qualified historians actively disagree. Both or all positions are summarised.
An account preserved within a Church's own tradition that cannot be independently confirmed from early sources. This is not a judgement that it is false.

A story first attested long after the events it describes.

A note on quotations and translations

Primary sources are quoted from public-domain translations, chiefly the Ante-Nicene Fathers (1885–96) and the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers (1886–1900). Their English is old-fashioned and occasionally loose, but their texts are freely available, so every quotation here can be checked against them. Each quotation panel names the translator and volume. Where a modern critical translation differs significantly, the note says so. Modern church documents still under copyright are quoted only in short phrases and otherwise summarised. Every quotation is listed in the Verification register with its verification status.

Honest limits of this edition

  • Quotations marked “checked” were compared word for word against an edition during preparation. Other factual claims rest on the standard scholarship listed in the bibliography, but not every claim was individually re-checked. The editorial report gives the exact figures.
  • Secondary works are cited by author, title and chapter or section rather than page number. No page numbers have been supplied that could not be checked.
  • This hosting environment cannot display remotely hosted photographs. The maps and diagrams are original drawings made for this edition, and the Visual sources page directs readers to museum and archive images of the objects discussed.
