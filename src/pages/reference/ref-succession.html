Apostolic succession

The lists of bishops of the great sees of the ancient Church, as the churches themselves keep them, from the apostles to today. Each list shows where the record is secure and where it rests on later tradition, and where a see divided into rival lines.

By about 180 Hegesippus and Irenaeus were drawing up lists of bishops to show that the apostles’ teaching had been handed down in public, from one bishop to the next, against the Gnostics’ claim to a secret tradition (see Irenaeus: rule of faith, Scripture and succession). The early lists are evidence of what churches believed about their past, not always of the past itself.

Bishops of Rome (popes)

Irenaeus of Lyons, writing about 180, gives the first list: from the apostles Peter and Paul to Eleutherius, the bishop of his own day (Against Heresies 3.3.3). Hegesippus had drawn up a list a little earlier. Many historians think Rome was led by a group of presbyters until the middle of the second century, so the names before Anicetus are traditional and their dates conjectural. The dates from Victor I follow the Annuario Pontificio.

Bishops and patriarchs of Constantinople

Byzantium became Constantinople in 330. Later tradition traced its see to the apostle Andrew through Stachys (Romans 16:9), but that list was compiled centuries afterwards. The reliable series begins in the fourth century. The council of 381 gave the bishop “the prerogative of honour after the bishop of Rome” (canon 3), and Chalcedon confirmed it in 451 (canon 28, which Rome did not accept). The title “ecumenical patriarch” is used from the late sixth century.

Bishops and patriarchs of Alexandria

Tradition, recorded by Eusebius, names the evangelist Mark as the founder of the Church of Alexandria and gives a list of his successors. The names before Demetrius are known only from that list. The bishop of Alexandria was called “pope” (papas) by the third century, before the title was used of Rome. The see divided after Chalcedon (451): the majority of Egyptian Christians rejected the council and kept their own line, today the Coptic Orthodox popes; the line that accepted it became the Greek Orthodox patriarchate.

Bishops and patriarchs of Antioch

Antioch is where the disciples were first called Christians (Acts 11:26). Tradition counts Peter as its first bishop and Ignatius, martyred in Rome about 110, as one of his early successors. Eusebius preserves the list; the dates are uncertain before the third century. No see has divided more: today five churches each have a Patriarch of Antioch, and none of them lives in Antioch (Antakya, Turkey).

Bishops and patriarchs of Jerusalem

The first leader of the Jerusalem church was James, the brother of the Lord, killed in 62. Eusebius, following Hegesippus, lists fifteen bishops “of the circumcision” down to the revolt of 132–135; after it, Jerusalem became the Roman colony Aelia Capitolina and its bishops were Gentiles. Jerusalem was under the metropolitan of Caesarea until Chalcedon (451) made it a patriarchate, fifth in rank.

The early list

  1. James, the brother of the Lord d. 62
  2. Symeon son of Clopas d. c. 107
  3. Justus, Zacchaeus, Tobias, Benjamin, John, Matthias, Philip, Seneca, Justus, Levi, Ephres, Joseph, Judas to 135, Jewish-Christian bishops (Eusebius, Church History 4.5)
  4. Mark the first Gentile bishop, from 135
  5. Narcissus c. 190
  6. Alexander d. 251 in the Decian persecution
  7. Macarius I c. 314–333; at Nicaea
  8. Cyril c. 350–386, with exiles
  9. John II 387–417
  10. Juvenal 422–458; first patriarch

Archbishops of Canterbury

Gregory the Great sent the monk Augustine to the Anglo-Saxon kingdom of Kent in 597. His successors at Canterbury led the English Church in communion with Rome until the break under Henry VIII; Reginald Pole was the last archbishop in communion with Rome. The Church of England counts an unbroken succession of archbishops across the Reformation, and the archbishop is the focus of unity of the worldwide Anglican Communion. Leo XIII judged Anglican orders invalid in 1896 (Apostolicae curae); the Church of England’s archbishops replied in 1897.
