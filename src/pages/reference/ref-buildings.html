Church buildings and the liturgy

How the shape of a church follows what happens inside it. Three plans (the early basilica, the Byzantine cross-in-square church and the Gothic cathedral), then the Eucharist as four traditions celebrate it today, step by step.

The early Christian basilica

Before Constantine, Christians met in houses, some of them adapted for worship, like the house-church at Dura-Europos (c. 240). From 312 Constantine paid for large churches in Rome, Jerusalem and Constantinople. Their builders chose the basilica, the Roman hall used for law courts and markets, not the temple: a church had to hold a congregation, while a temple housed a god. Old St Peter’s (begun c. 319) and St Paul Outside the Walls kept this plan; Santa Sabina in Rome (c. 425) still shows it almost unchanged.

The bishop presided from the apse, facing the people across the altar, and in Rome the old basilicas still face west, so the priest stands behind the altar looking east over the congregation. Most later churches were built facing east, toward the rising sun as a sign of the risen Christ (Malachi 4:2; Luke 1:78). See Constantine’s conversion and The history of Christian worship.

The Byzantine cross-in-square church

Justinian’s Hagia Sophia (537) set a huge dome over a basilica. From the ninth century the usual Byzantine church was smaller and centred: a square with a dome carried on four columns, the arms of a cross spreading from it under barrel vaults. The interior was a picture of the cosmos: Christ the Pantocrator in the dome as heaven, the Mother of God in the apse between heaven and earth, saints and feasts on the walls below. In the Russian Church, from the fifteenth century, the low chancel screen grew into a wall of icons several rows high, the iconostasis.

On the iconostasis, Christ is always to the right of the Royal Doors (as you face them) and the Mother of God with the Child to the left; the Annunciation and the four evangelists are usually on the doors themselves. See How to read an icon and Iconoclasm.

The Gothic cathedral

From Abbot Suger’s choir at Saint-Denis (consecrated 1144), builders in northern France combined the pointed arch, the ribbed vault and the flying buttress. The walls no longer had to carry the vault, so they could be opened into windows of coloured glass. The plan kept the Latin cross of the basilica but lengthened the east end, where the canons sang the daily office, and wrapped it in an ambulatory so pilgrims could pass the shrines without disturbing them. See Cathedrals, art and the Byzantine Church under pressure.

The Reformation changed these spaces. Lutheran churches mostly kept their altars and images; Reformed churches removed images and placed the pulpit and communion table at the centre; the Church of England kept its cathedrals and choirs but took down rood lofts. After Trent, Catholic churches removed many choir screens so that the people could see the altar, and the Second Vatican Council led to free-standing altars facing the people.

The Eucharist compared

Every ancient liturgy has the same two parts, already visible in Justin Martyr’s description of Sunday worship in Rome about 155 (First Apology 67): the readings and preaching, then the prayer of thanksgiving over bread and wine and the communion. The table follows the usual Sunday form of each rite today. Rows line up by function, so a cell is empty where a rite has no equivalent at that point.
