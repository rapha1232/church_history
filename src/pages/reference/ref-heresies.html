Heresies and the Church’s answers

The teachings the councils and Fathers rejected: what each claimed, who taught it, which council or synod answered it, who wrote against it, and whether anything like it survives today.

“Heresy” (Greek hairesis, “choice” or “party”) is the word of the side that prevailed. Most of what we know of these teachers comes from their opponents. Several groups listed here reject the label, and some are honoured by churches that exist today; each entry says so where it applies. See also Index of controversies and How doctrines developed.

late 1st–2nd centuryDocetismChrist

Christ only seemed (Greek dokein) to have a body, to suffer and to die.

Taught by
A tendency rather than a party; taught by some Gnostic teachers
Answered by
No council
Written against by
Ignatius of Antioch, the letters of John (1 John 4:2–3; 2 John 7)
What happened
Absorbed into the Gnostic systems of the second century.
Today
No surviving community.

late 1st–4th centuryEbionitesChrist

Jesus was the human Messiah, son of Joseph and Mary, chosen by God for his righteousness. Christians must keep the Law of Moses. They used a single Gospel related to Matthew and rejected Paul.

Taught by
No founder is known. “Ebion” is a later invention from the name, which means “the poor”.
Answered by
No council; described as heretical by the second-century writers
Written against by
Irenaeus, Origen, Epiphanius
What happened
Faded by the fifth century as Jewish Christianity disappeared.
Today
No surviving community.

2nd–3rd centuryThe “Gnostic” teachers Valentinians, Sethians, BasilideansMatter and dualism

The material world was made by a lower, ignorant creator (the demiurge), not by the highest God. Christ came to bring the saving knowledge (gnosis) that awakens the divine spark in some people.

Taught by
Basilides, Valentinus, Ptolemy, Heracleon
Answered by
No council; excluded by local churches
Written against by
Irenaeus, Hippolytus, Tertullian, Clement of Alexandria
What happened
Declined from the third century. Many of their writings were rediscovered at Nag Hammadi, Egypt, in 1945.
Today
No surviving community.

Many scholars now avoid “Gnosticism” as a single category: the groups differed widely, and the label comes mostly from their opponents.

from 144 to the 5th centuryMarcionismScripture and revelation

The God of the Old Testament, a just but harsh creator, is not the good Father revealed by Jesus. The Church should read only an edited Gospel of Luke and ten letters of Paul.

Taught by
Marcion of Sinope
Answered by
Excluded by the Roman church in 144
Written against by
Justin Martyr, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Against Marcion (five books)
What happened
Marcionite churches lasted in the East into the fifth century. Marcion’s challenge pushed other churches to define their own canon.
Today
No surviving community.

Read more: Marcion, “Gnosticism” and Montanism

c. 160 to the 6th centuryMontanism the New ProphecyScripture and revelation

The Holy Spirit (the Paraclete) speaks anew through the prophets Montanus, Priscilla and Maximilla, calling for stricter fasting and discipline and for readiness to die as a martyr.

Taught by
Montanus, Priscilla, Maximilla, Tertullian, from about 207
Answered by
Synods of bishops in Asia Minor, among the first known
Written against by
Apollinarius of Hierapolis, Miltiades, the anonymous writer quoted by Eusebius (Church History 5.16)
What happened
Survived in Phrygia until Justinian’s persecution in the sixth century.
Today
No surviving community.

Read more: Marcion, “Gnosticism” and Montanism

late 2nd–3rd centuryAdoptionism dynamic monarchianismChrist

Jesus was a man on whom the power of God came, at his baptism, making him Son of God by adoption.

Taught by
Theodotus the Tanner, Paul of Samosata
Answered by
Theodotus excommunicated by Victor of Rome (c. 190); Synods of Antioch, 264–268, depose Paul
Written against by
Hippolytus, the synod letter of 268
What happened
Paul’s followers were still a separate group at Nicaea (canon 19). A different “Adoptionism” arose in eighth-century Spain and was condemned at Frankfurt in 794.
Today
No surviving community.

Read more: Monarchianism and the Logos

c. 200–4th centuryModalism Sabellianism, PatripassianismGod and the Trinity

Father, Son and Spirit are one person under three names or modes, so that the Father himself suffered on the cross.

Taught by
Noetus of Smyrna, Praxeas, Sabellius
Answered by
Sabellius excommunicated by Callixtus of Rome (c. 220)
Written against by
Hippolytus, Against Noetus, Tertullian, Against Praxeas, Dionysius of Alexandria
What happened
Survived mainly as an accusation: both sides of the fourth-century debates charged the other with it.
Today
Oneness Pentecostals (from 1913) hold a similar doctrine of God, though they arose independently.

Read more: Monarchianism and the Logos

251 to the 7th centuryNovatianismChurch and sacraments

The Church has no power to readmit those who denied the faith under persecution; it must remain a church of the pure.

Taught by
Novatian
Answered by
Roman synod of 251
Written against by
Cornelius of Rome, Cyprian of Carthage, Dionysius of Alexandria
What happened
Novatianist churches existed, with their own bishops, until the seventh century. Nicaea (canon 8) set the terms for receiving their clergy.
Today
No surviving community.

Read more: Ministry and worship: from Ignatius to Cyprian

311 to the 7th centuryDonatismChurch and sacraments

Sacraments given by clergy who had handed over the Scriptures in the persecution (traditores) are invalid. The true Church is the church of the pure.

Taught by
Donatus of Casae Nigrae, Parmenian
Answered by
Synod of Rome (313) and Council of Arles (314); Conference of Carthage (411), followed by imperial laws
Written against by
Optatus of Milevis, Augustine
What happened
Remained the majority church in parts of North Africa until the Arab conquest. Augustine’s answer (the sacraments are Christ’s, not the minister’s) became the Western teaching.
Today
No surviving community.

Read more: The Donatist controversy

318 to the 7th centuryArianism with the later Homoian and Anomoean partiesGod and the Trinity

The Son is the first and highest of God’s creatures: “there was when he was not”. He is God in a derived sense, not eternal and not of the same being as the Father.

Taught by
Arius, Eusebius of Nicomedia, Eunomius, Ulfilas, missionary to the Goths
Answered by
Nicaea (325); Constantinople (381)
Written against by
Alexander of Alexandria, Athanasius, Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nazianzus, Hilary of Poitiers
What happened
Homoian Christianity remained the faith of the Goths, Vandals and Lombards. The Visigoths of Spain accepted Nicaea at the Third Council of Toledo in 589.
Today
Jehovah’s Witnesses teach a similar view of Christ, without historical continuity.

Read more: Arius and the outbreak of the controversy

360s–5th centuryPneumatomachians “fighters against the Spirit”, MacedoniansGod and the Trinity

The Son is divine, but the Holy Spirit is not God: a creature or a ministering power.

Taught by
Eustathius of Sebaste, the followers of Macedonius
Answered by
Constantinople (381), whose creed calls the Spirit “the Lord, the giver of life … worshipped and glorified” with the Father and the Son
Written against by
Athanasius, Letters to Serapion, Basil, On the Holy Spirit, Gregory of Nazianzus
What happened
Disappeared in the fifth century.
Today
No surviving community.

Read more: Theodosius, 380–381, and the Nicene settlement

360s–5th centuryApollinarianismChrist

In Christ the divine Word took the place of a human mind, so Christ had a human body and soul but not a human intellect.

Taught by
Apollinarius of Laodicea
Answered by
Roman synods (377–382); Constantinople (381), canon 1
Written against by
Gregory of Nazianzus, Gregory of Nyssa
What happened
Faded in the fifth century, though some of Apollinarius’s writings circulated under the names of orthodox Fathers.
Today
No surviving community.

Gregory of Nazianzus’s reply became a rule of Christology: what Christ did not assume, he did not heal.

c. 410–431PelagianismSin and grace

Human beings can keep God’s commands by their own free will. Adam’s sin harmed his descendants by bad example, not by an inherited condition. Grace is God’s law and teaching, not an inner power that moves the will.

Taught by
Pelagius, Caelestius, Julian of Eclanum
Answered by
Council of Carthage (418); Ephesus (431), which condemned Caelestius
Written against by
Augustine, Jerome, Prosper of Aquitaine
What happened
Condemned in West and East.
Today
No surviving community.

Read more: Ephesus (431)

5th–6th century“Semi-Pelagianism” the Massilian positionSin and grace

The first movement of faith comes from the human will; God’s grace then helps it. Augustine’s teaching on predestination is a novelty.

Taught by
John Cassian, Faustus of Riez
Answered by
Council of Orange (529), confirmed by Pope Boniface II
Written against by
Prosper of Aquitaine, Caesarius of Arles
What happened
Orange settled the question in the West for centuries; its canons were rediscovered and cited in the sixteenth century.
Today
No surviving community.

The name is a sixteenth-century label. Cassian and his circle opposed Pelagius and never called themselves anything of the kind.

Read more: The rise of monasticism

428 onward“Nestorianism”Christ

As charged by Cyril: Christ is two persons, the Word and the man Jesus, joined only by a moral union, so Mary should not be called Theotokos (“God-bearer”).

Taught by
Nestorius
Answered by
Ephesus (431); Chalcedon (451)
Written against by
Cyril of Alexandria, Celestine of Rome
What happened
Nestorius died in exile. The Church of the East, beyond the empire, never accepted Ephesus.
Today
The Church of the East honours Nestorius but rejects the name “Nestorian”. The Common Christological Declaration with Rome (1994) recognises a shared faith in Christ.

Whether Nestorius himself taught “two sons” is disputed. His own later defence, the Book of Heraclides, says he did not.

Read more: Nestorius, Cyril, and Ephesus (428–433)

448–451EutychianismChrist

After the union, Christ has one nature, in which his humanity is absorbed and is not of the same being as ours.

Taught by
Eutyches
Answered by
Home synod of Constantinople (448); Chalcedon (451), after the “Robber Council” of Ephesus (449) had cleared him
Written against by
Flavian of Constantinople, Leo the Great, Tome
What happened
Condemned by every party: the Oriental Orthodox Churches anathematise Eutyches too.
Today
No surviving community.

Eutychianism is not the same as the miaphysite (“one incarnate nature”) teaching of the Oriental Orthodox Churches, which rejected Chalcedon on other grounds. See S5 · E3.

Read more: From Eutyches to Chalcedon (448–451)

633–681Monothelitism and MonoenergismChrist

Christ has two natures but one activity (energy) and one will. It was offered by the emperor Heraclius as a compromise with the miaphysites.

Taught by
Sergius I of Constantinople, Cyrus of Alexandria, Honorius I of Rome, who accepted the formula
Answered by
Lateran Synod (649); Constantinople III (680–681), which also condemned Honorius
Written against by
Maximus the Confessor, Sophronius of Jerusalem, Martin I of Rome
What happened
Officially abandoned in 681; briefly revived by the emperor Philippicus (711–713).
Today
Some Byzantine and later Latin writers said the Maronites held it. Maronite historians reject the charge, and the question is still debated (S13 · E5).

Read more: Constantinople III (680–681)

7th–15th centuryPaulicians and BogomilsMatter and dualism

A good God made the spiritual world and an evil power the material one. They rejected the sacraments, the cross, images and the hierarchy.

Taught by
Constantine-Silvanus (Paulicians, Armenia and Asia Minor), the priest Bogomil (Bulgaria, 10th century)
Answered by
Byzantine imperial campaigns and trials
Written against by
Peter of Sicily, Cosmas the Priest, Euthymius Zigabenus
What happened
The Paulicians were deported to the Balkans; the Bogomils spread into Bosnia and influenced the Cathars of the West.
Today
No surviving community.

Read more: Canon law, heresy and the inquisitions

c. 730–787 and 815–843IconoclasmImages

Images of Christ and the saints are idols. Christ cannot be depicted without either separating or confusing his natures; the Eucharist is the only true image of Christ.

Taught by
Emperor Leo III, Emperor Constantine V, the Council of Hieria (754)
Answered by
Nicaea II (787); The restoration of 843, kept as the Sunday of Orthodoxy
Written against by
John of Damascus, Theodore the Studite, Patriarch Germanus I
What happened
Defeated in 843. The Frankish Libri Carolini and the Council of Frankfurt (794) criticised Nicaea II from another angle.
Today
Most Protestant churches did not accept Nicaea II’s decree, and the Reformed tradition removed images from churches.

Read more: Iconoclasm and the Western reaction

c. 1140–1320Cathars AlbigensiansMatter and dualism

The material world is the work of an evil principle. Salvation comes through the consolamentum, a laying on of hands, after which the “perfect” renounced meat, sex and property.

Taught by
The “good men” (perfecti) of Languedoc and northern Italy
Answered by
Third Lateran Council (1179); The Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229); The inquisitions from the 1230s; Montségur fell in 1244
Written against by
Dominic, Bernard of Clairvaux, the Cistercian legates
What happened
Destroyed by the early fourteenth century.
Today
No surviving community.

Some historians argue that the idea of a single organised “Cathar Church” was partly constructed by its persecutors.

from c. 1173WaldensiansChurch and sacraments

Laypeople, men and women, may preach the Gospel in poverty without a bishop’s licence. Later they rejected purgatory, oaths and the death penalty.

Taught by
Valdes (Peter Waldo) of Lyon
Answered by
Condemned at Verona (1184, Ad abolendam)
Written against by
the inquisitors of the thirteenth century
What happened
Survived persecution in the Alpine valleys of Piedmont; joined the Reformed churches at the synod of Chanforan in 1532.
Today
The Waldensian Evangelical Church in Italy survives, united with the Methodists since 1975.

Read more: Canon law, heresy and the inquisitions

1380s–1620sWycliffe and the HussitesChurch and sacraments

Scripture is the supreme authority. The true Church is the body of the predestined, not the visible hierarchy. Hus’s followers gave the cup to the laity at communion.

Taught by
John Wycliffe, Jan Hus, Jerome of Prague
Answered by
Council of Constance (1415), which burned Hus
Written against by
the Council of Constance, Jean Gerson
What happened
The Hussite wars (1419–1434); the Utraquist church was tolerated in Bohemia until 1620.
Today
The Moravian Church (Unity of the Brethren) traces its origin to the Hussite movement.

Protestants see Wycliffe and Hus as forerunners of the Reformation rather than heretics.

Read more: The Black Death, Wycliffe, Hus and late medieval piety

16th–17th centuryAntitrinitarians SociniansGod and the Trinity

The doctrine of the Trinity is not in Scripture. Jesus is a man, raised by God and given divine authority, but not eternal God.

Taught by
Michael Servetus, Faustus Socinus, the Minor Reformed Church of Poland (Racovian Catechism, 1605)
Answered by
Condemned by Catholics and by the Lutheran and Reformed churches alike; Servetus was burned at Geneva in 1553
Written against by
John Calvin, Philip Melanchthon
What happened
Expelled from Poland in 1658; influenced English and American Unitarianism.
Today
Unitarian churches exist today, though most no longer base their teaching on Scripture in the Socinian way.

Read more: The Radical Reformation

1640s–18th centuryJansenismSin and grace

Following Cornelius Jansen’s Augustinus (1640): fallen humanity cannot resist efficacious grace, and Christ did not die for all. In practice it meant rigorist piety and infrequent communion.

Taught by
Cornelius Jansen, Antoine Arnauld, the nuns of Port-Royal, Pasquier Quesnel
Answered by
Innocent X, Cum occasione (1653); Clement XI, Unigenitus (1713)
Written against by
the Jesuits, Blaise Pascal took the Jansenists’ side
What happened
Port-Royal was destroyed in 1710; the movement fed later conflicts in the French Church.
Today
The Old Catholic Church of Utrecht, separated from Rome in 1723, was shaped by Jansenist refugees.

Read more: The theological disputes

The claims are summarised in their own terms where their writings survive (Arius’s letters, the Nag Hammadi texts, the Book of Heraclides, Jansen’s Augustinus) and otherwise from the opponents who report them, chiefly Irenaeus, Hippolytus, Eusebius and Epiphanius.
