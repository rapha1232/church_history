Glossary

108 terms. Many changed meaning over time, and the definitions note where that matters. Throughout the text, dotted-underlined terms open these definitions in place.

Adventism
Movements expecting Christ's imminent return; especially the Seventh-day Adventist Church (1863). →
Anabaptists
“Re-baptisers”: radical reformers practising believers' baptism (Swiss Brethren, Mennonites, Hutterites). →
Anathema
A formal declaration excluding a person or teaching from the Church's communion; from the Greek for “set apart”. Conciliar anathemas condemn propositions and the people who hold them. →
Apocalyptic
Literature and worldview of revealed secrets about the end of the age, cosmic conflict and judgement (e.g. Daniel, 1 Enoch, Revelation). →
Apocrypha
(1) For Protestants, the deuterocanonical books; (2) non-canonical early Christian gospels, acts and apocalypses. →
Apologists
Second- and third-century authors who defended Christianity to outsiders (Justin, Athenagoras, Tertullian, etc.). →
Apostolic fathers
Modern label for the earliest non-canonical Christian writings (1 Clement, Ignatius, Polycarp, the Didache, Hermas, etc.). →
Apostolic see
A church founded by an apostle. In the West “the Apostolic See” came to mean Rome specifically; in the East several sees (Antioch, Alexandria, Jerusalem, and by later tradition Constantinople, via Andrew) claimed apostolic foundation. →
Apostolic succession
The continuity of bishops from the apostles through ordination and occupancy of a see. Irenaeus (c. 180) used succession lists to guarantee authentic teaching; later theology emphasised sacramental transmission. →
Arminianism
Theology of Jacobus Arminius (d. 1609): resistible grace and conditional election; adopted by Methodists. →
Autocephaly
“Self-headed”: the status of an Orthodox Church that elects its own primate and governs itself. →
Azymes
Unleavened bread used for the Eucharist in the Latin (and Armenian) Church; a major issue in 1053–54. →
Canon
A rule of church discipline issued by a council or recognised authority. Also: the list of books accepted as Scripture. The two senses should not be confused. →
Catechumen
A person under instruction preparing for baptism. →
Catholic
Originally “universal, whole” (Ignatius, c. 110). Used by the imperial Church of itself from the 4th century (cf. Cunctos populos, 380); later the self-designation of the Church in communion with Rome. Orthodox Churches also confess the Church as “catholic” in the creed. →
Catholicos
Title of the head of certain Eastern Churches outside the Roman imperial system: the Church of the East, the Armenian and Georgian Churches, and the Malankara Orthodox Church. →
Chalcedonian
Accepting the Definition of Chalcedon (451). Catholic, Eastern Orthodox and most Protestant Churches are Chalcedonian. →
Church of the east
The Church of the Persian Empire and Asia, self-governing from 410–424, theologically shaped by Theodore of Mopsuestia. Today: Assyrian Church of the East, Ancient Church of the East, and (in communion with Rome) the Chaldean and Syro-Malabar Churches. →
Communion
Mutual recognition between churches, expressed above all by sharing the Eucharist and commemorating each other's bishops. →
Conciliarism
The view that a general council holds authority superior to the pope (Constance, Haec sancta, 1415). →
Conciliarity
Government of the Church through councils or synods of bishops (Greek synodality). →
Council
An assembly of bishops (also “synod”). An ecumenical council claims authority for the whole Church; see the introduction to Vol. VI. →
Deuterocanonical
Old Testament books in the Septuagint but not the Hebrew canon (Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, 1–2 Maccabees…); canonical for Catholics and Orthodox, “Apocrypha” for most Protestants. →
Dhimmi
Protected non-Muslim subject under Islamic law, paying jizya and subject to restrictions. →
Diaspora
Jewish communities living outside the land of Israel. →
Diptychs
Lists of living and departed bishops commemorated at the Eucharist; inclusion or removal signified communion or its breach. →
Doctrine
Church teaching. Dogma is doctrine formally defined as binding; theological opinions (theologoumena) are not binding. →
Dogma
A doctrine formally defined as binding on the faithful by an authority the Church recognises (e.g. an ecumenical council). →
Dyophysite
“Two-nature”: affirming two natures in Christ after the union. Used of Chalcedonians and, differently, of the Church of the East. →
Eastern catholic
Churches of Eastern liturgical tradition in full communion with Rome (e.g. Maronite, Melkite, Ukrainian Greek Catholic, Chaldean, Syro-Malabar). →
Ecumenical
From oikoumenē, the inhabited world (i.e. the Roman Empire). In modern usage also: concerning Christian unity. →
Ecumenism
The movement for Christian unity. →
Energies
In Palamite theology, God's uncreated activities, in which he is truly present and participated, distinct from his unknowable essence. →
Episcopos
Greek “overseer”, origin of “bishop”. In the New Testament overlapping with “presbyter” (elder); by the 2nd century the single leader of a local church. →
Essenes
Ascetic Jewish communities described by Josephus, Philo and Pliny; usually linked with the Dead Sea Scrolls community. →
Eucharist
“Thanksgiving”: the Lord's Supper, the central Christian rite of bread and wine. →
Evangelicalism
Transdenominational Protestant movement marked by conversion, the Bible, the cross and activism. →
Exarch
In the 5th century, the bishop over a civil “diocese” (group of provinces) — the sense of Chalcedon canon 9; later, a delegate of a patriarch. →
Filioque
Latin, “and the Son”: the Western addition to the Creed of 381 stating that the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son. →
Gnosticism
Modern umbrella term for 2nd-century movements offering saving knowledge and distinguishing the true God from the world's creator; its usefulness is debated. →
Hellenists
In Acts, Greek-speaking Jewish believers in Jerusalem (Stephen, Philip). →
Henotikon
Emperor Zeno's “instrument of union” (482) attempting to reconcile Chalcedon's opponents by silence about the council. →
Heresy
From Greek hairesis, “choice, school”. A teaching judged contrary to the Church's faith; distinguished from schism (a break in communion over order rather than doctrine). →
Homoian
The 4th-century position that the Son is “like” (homoios) the Father, avoiding substance language; the imperial creed of 359–60. →
Homoiousios
“Of like substance”: a mediating 4th-century formula (Basil of Ancyra). →
Homoousios
“Of the same substance/being”: the Nicene term (325) for the Son's relation to the Father. →
Hypostasis
Concrete individual reality; in Trinitarian theology each of the three “persons”; in Chalcedonian Christology the one subject of Christ. →
Iconoclasm
“Image-breaking”: Byzantine imperial policy against religious images (c. 726/730–787; 815–843). →
Indulgence
In Catholic teaching, remission of temporal punishment for sins already forgiven; its abuse sparked the Reformation. →
Inquisition
Judicial procedures against heresy — episcopal, papal, Spanish, Portuguese and Roman forms differed greatly. →
Investiture
Conferral of office; the 11th–12th-century conflict over lay investiture of bishops. →
Jurisdiction
Governing power over a territory or body of faithful (to ordain, judge, legislate), distinct from honour or precedence. →
Lapsi
Christians who sacrificed or obtained certificates during persecution (especially under Decius, 250). →
Latrocinium
“Robbery”, “robber council”: Leo I's name for the Council of Ephesus of 449. →
Liberation theology
Latin American theology from the late 1960s emphasising God's preferential option for the poor. →
Logos
Greek “word, reason”: in Stoicism the rational order of the cosmos; in Philo God's agent; in John 1 and the apologists the pre-existent Son. →
Magisterium
The teaching authority of the Catholic Church (pope and bishops). →
Marcionism
The church founded by Marcion (c. 144), which rejected the Old Testament God and scriptures. →
Maronite
Catholic Church of Syriac-Antiochene tradition, named after the hermit Maron; centred in Lebanon. →
Melkite
From Syriac malkā, “king”: Chalcedonian (imperial) Christians of the Middle East; today also the name of the Melkite Greek Catholic Church. →
Mendicants
“Begging” orders of friars (Franciscans, Dominicans, Carmelites, Augustinians) founded in the 13th century. →
Messiah
Hebrew mashiach, “anointed one” (Greek Christos): originally a king or priest anointed with oil; in Second Temple texts, various expected agents of God's deliverance. →
Metropolitan
The bishop of a provincial capital, presiding over the bishops of the province. →
Miaphysite
“One-nature” (mia physis): the Christology of Cyril's formula as held by the Oriental Orthodox — one united nature of the incarnate Word, without confusion or loss of humanity. →
Millet
Ottoman term for a recognised religious community administered through its leaders. →
Monarchianism
Third-century theologies safeguarding God's oneness either by making the Son a mode of the Father (modalism) or a man adopted by God (adoptionism). →
Monophysite
Older (often polemical) term for opponents of Chalcedon; in strict use, the Eutychian view that Christ has only a divine nature. Avoided for the Oriental Orthodox, who reject it. →
Monothelitism
The 7th-century doctrine of one will in Christ, condemned in 681. Monenergism: one “activity”. →
Montanism
The “New Prophecy” of Montanus, Prisca and Maximilla (Phrygia, c. 160s–170s), claiming new revelations of the Spirit. →
Nestorian
Polemical term for the teaching that Christ is two persons, attributed to Nestorius; historically misapplied to the Church of the East. →
Old believers
Russian Orthodox who rejected Nikon's 17th-century liturgical reforms. →
Ordinance
Term preferred by Baptists and others for baptism and the Lord's Supper, viewed as commanded acts rather than means of grace. →
Oriental orthodox
The communion of Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopian, Eritrean and Malankara Orthodox Churches, which accept three councils and reject Chalcedon. →
Orthodox
“Right belief / right glory”. Claimed by all parties; now the self-designation of the Eastern Orthodox (Chalcedonian, Byzantine-tradition) Churches and of the Oriental Orthodox. →
Ousia
Being, essence, substance: the one divine nature shared by Father, Son and Spirit. →
Patriarch
Title of the heads of the major sees (from the 5th–6th century); now used by several Churches. →
Pentarchy
The Byzantine theory of church government by the five patriarchates of Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch and Jerusalem. →
Pentecostalism
Movement from 1901–06 stressing baptism in the Holy Spirit and spiritual gifts. →
Pharisees
A Second Temple Jewish movement devoted to precise interpretation of the Torah and to ancestral traditions; believed in resurrection. →
Physis
Nature. Ambiguous in the 5th century: for Cyril often a concrete individual; for Chalcedon the set of properties of divinity or humanity. →
Pietism
Lutheran renewal movement stressing personal faith and practice (Spener, Francke). →
Pneumatomachians
“Spirit-fighters”: those who denied the full divinity of the Holy Spirit (later called Macedonians). →
Pope
“Father”. Used in the 3rd–4th centuries of the bishop of Alexandria (Arius calls Alexander “the Pope”) and of others; in the West from the 6th century increasingly reserved to the bishop of Rome. The Coptic patriarch is still “Pope of Alexandria”. →
Primacy
First place among bishops or churches. Its content — honour, appellate authority, jurisdiction — is the central disputed question between Catholics and Orthodox. →
Prosopon
Face, presentation, person; used by Antiochene theologians of the one “person of union” in Christ. →
Puritans
English Protestants seeking further reform of the Church of England (16th–17th c.). →
Q
A hypothetical lost source of sayings of Jesus used by Matthew and Luke (from German Quelle, source). →
Qnoma
Syriac term in the Church of the East's Christology (“two qnome”), best understood as an individuated nature, not a person. →
Reception
The process by which the Churches accept (or reject) a council's decisions as authentic expressions of the faith. →
Rule of faith
Regula fidei: a summary of essential Christian belief cited by Irenaeus and Tertullian; a forerunner of creeds. →
Sabellianism
Modalism: the view that Father, Son and Spirit are modes or roles of one divine person (after Sabellius, 3rd century). →
Sadducees
Second Temple Jewish party linked to the priestly aristocracy; accepted only the written Torah; rejected resurrection. →
Schism
A break in communion, typically over authority or discipline rather than doctrine — though the two are often intertwined. →
Scholasticism
The method of the medieval schools: questions, authorities, objections and reasoned resolution. →
See
A bishop's seat (Latin sedes) and, by extension, his diocese. →
Septuagint
The Greek translation of the Jewish scriptures made in Alexandria (3rd–1st c. BC), abbreviated LXX; the Old Testament of the early Church. →
Sola fide
“Faith alone”: justification by faith apart from works (Lutheran and Reformed). →
Sola scriptura
“Scripture alone” as the supreme norm of doctrine (Protestant principle). →
Subordinationism
Theologies ranking the Son (and Spirit) below the Father in divinity or being; widespread before Nicaea. →
Supremacy
Universal jurisdiction over all churches and faithful, as defined for the pope at Vatican I (1870). →
Synod
A council of bishops. The “home synod” (synodos endemousa) of Constantinople was a standing synod of bishops present in the capital. →
Synoptic gospels
Matthew, Mark and Luke, which can be “seen together” because of their shared material. →
Theosis
Deification: salvation as participation in the divine life (2 Pet. 1:4), central to Eastern theology and present in Western. →
Theotokos
“God-bearer”: title of Mary affirmed at Ephesus (431), a statement that the one born of her is God the Word. →
Three chapters
The person and works of Theodore of Mopsuestia, writings of Theodoret against Cyril, and a letter of Ibas of Edessa — condemned by Justinian and in 553. →
Transubstantiation
Catholic doctrine that the substance of bread and wine becomes Christ's body and blood (Lateran IV 1215; Trent). →
Ultramontanism
19th-century movement strengthening papal authority “beyond the mountains” (Rome). →
Uniatism
Pejorative Orthodox term for the method of bringing parts of Eastern Churches into union with Rome; renounced as a method at Balamand (1993). →
