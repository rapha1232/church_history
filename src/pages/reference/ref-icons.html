How to read an icon

An icon is not a portrait or an illustration but an image for prayer, painted to a tradition of types, colours and gestures. Once you know the conventions, most icons can be read like a text: who is shown, which moment, and what it means.

Why icons

The earliest Christian images are in the Roman catacombs and the house-church at Dura-Europos in the third century. Painted panels of Christ and the saints survive from the sixth century, most famously at St Catherine’s Monastery on Sinai, which escaped the destruction of the iconoclast period (c. 730–843). The Second Council of Nicaea (787) defended images on the ground of the Incarnation: because the invisible God became visible in Christ, Christ can be depicted. It distinguished the honour or veneration (proskynesis) given to an image from the worship (latreia) due to God alone, and, following Basil the Great, taught that the honour given to the image passes to the one it shows. See Iconoclasm and the Western reaction and Nicaea II.

Reading the image

Gold
The gold ground is not a sky or a wall but uncreated light, the glory of God. Figures do not stand in a place so much as in eternity.
Light
There are few cast shadows. Light seems to come from within the figures, and faces are built up from dark to light in successive layers.
Perspective
Buildings and furniture are often drawn in “inverse” perspective, widening away from the viewer, so the scene opens toward the one who prays rather than receding from them. Buildings in the background mean the scene takes place indoors; a red cloth draped between them says the same.
Faces and bodies
Eyes are large and calm, noses long and thin, mouths small and closed: the senses turned toward God. Saints face the viewer, or are shown in three-quarter view; usually only figures who have turned from God, such as Judas or the demons, are drawn in profile.
Inscriptions
An icon is named. ΙϹ ΧϹ (IC XC) abbreviates the Greek for Jesus Christ; ΜΡ ΘΥ (MP ΘY) stands for Mother of God; in Christ’s halo the three letters Ο ὬΝ (ho ōn, “the One who is”) recall God’s name to Moses (Exodus 3:14). Saints are named in full, often with a title.
Hands
Christ blesses with the fingers held to form the letters IC XC, or with two fingers raised and the thumb joined to the other two (the older form kept by the Old Believers). A hand raised with the palm outward is a speaking or witnessing gesture; open hands raised at the sides are the ancient posture of prayer (orans).
Colours
The meanings are conventions, not a fixed code, and they differ between schools. Christ commonly wears a red or brown tunic under a blue mantle: divinity clothed in humanity. The Mother of God wears the reverse, a blue tunic under a deep red veil (maphorion) with three stars, for her virginity before, during and after the birth of Christ. White is used for the transfigured and risen Christ, and for the clothes of angels.
Scrolls and books
A prophet holds an open scroll with his prophecy; an evangelist a closed or open Gospel book; a bishop the Gospel and a stole with crosses (omophorion); a monk a scroll with a saying from his teaching.

Christ

  • Pantocrator (“Ruler of all”): Christ half-length, blessing with his right hand and holding the Gospel book in his left. The sixth-century icon at Sinai, one of the oldest to survive, has a face that is subtly different on each side, often read as his two natures. In a domed church he looks down from the dome.
  • The Image Not Made by Hands (Mandylion): the face of Christ alone on a cloth, from the legend of King Abgar of Edessa. It is traditionally the first icon a painter learns.
  • Emmanuel: Christ as a boy with a high forehead, the eternal Word as a child.
  • Christ the Great High Priest and Christ Enthroned: Christ in a bishop’s vestments or on a throne, often at the centre of the Deesis.
  • Deesis (“prayer”): Christ enthroned between the Mother of God and John the Baptist, who turn toward him in intercession. It is the central row of a large iconostasis.

The Mother of God

Almost every icon of Mary shows her with the Child, because she is honoured as Theotokos, the one who bore God (Ephesus, 431).

  • Hodegetria (“she who shows the way”): Mary holds the Child on one arm and points to him with the other hand. Tradition traced the type to a portrait by the evangelist Luke kept in Constantinople. The icon of Kazan is a Hodegetria.
  • Eleousa (“of tenderness”): the Child presses his cheek against his mother’s. The Vladimir icon, painted in Constantinople in the twelfth century and later taken to Russia, is the most famous.
  • Platytera (“wider than the heavens”): Mary with arms raised in prayer and the Child in a medallion before her. It fills the apse above the altar, because she contained in her womb the one whom the heavens cannot contain.

The great feasts

The festal row of an iconostasis shows the twelve great feasts of the year with Pascha above them. Each scene has a fixed composition with details that carry the meaning.

Nativity
Christ in a manger shaped like a tomb, swaddled like a body for burial, in a dark cave: he is born into the darkness of the world to die. The ox and the ass come from Isaiah 1:3. Joseph sits apart, often tempted to doubt by an old man, while midwives wash the Child.
Theophany
Christ stands in the Jordan, the water drawn like a dark cave or a tomb; the Father’s voice is a ray from a segment of heaven and the Spirit descends as a dove.
Transfiguration
Christ in white within a mandorla of light, Moses and Elijah beside him, and the three disciples thrown to the ground below.
Crucifixion
Christ hangs not in agony but in the stillness of death, his head bowed; Adam’s skull lies in the cave under Golgotha.
Resurrection (Anastasis)
The Gospels do not describe the moment of rising, so the Byzantine icon shows the descent into Hades: Christ stands on the broken gates of death, keys and locks scattered, and pulls Adam and Eve from their tombs by the wrist.
Pentecost
The apostles sit in a semicircle, Paul among them though he was not there, a sign of the whole Church. In the dark arch below, an old crowned king, “Cosmos”, holds a cloth with twelve scrolls: the world waiting for the apostles’ preaching.
Dormition
Mary lies on her bier among the apostles; Christ stands behind holding her soul as a small child in white, reversing the image of the Nativity.

East and West

The West shared the same images until the twelfth century; the mosaics of Rome, Ravenna and Sicily are icons in all but name. From the thirteenth century Western painting moved toward natural space, shadows and portraiture, and statues became common, which the Orthodox Churches avoid in worship. The Frankish theologians of the Libri Carolini (c. 790) accepted images as decoration and teaching but rejected their veneration. The Reformed churches removed images from worship; Lutherans kept many. Today icons are painted and prayed with in many Catholic and Protestant churches as well. The Eastern Catholic Churches keep the Byzantine tradition, and Maronite churches combine Syriac, Byzantine and Latin images.

On the theology of icons: the decree of Nicaea II (787); John of Damascus, On the Divine Images; Leonid Ouspensky and Vladimir Lossky, The Meaning of Icons.
