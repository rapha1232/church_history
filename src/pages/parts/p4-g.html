Christianity and Islam

2 minPreviously: Study F — Marian doctrines

Starring

  • Origins. Islam emerged in 7th-century Arabia, a region with Christian communities (Najran; Christian Arab tribes such as the Ghassanids) and Jewish communities. The Qur'an honours Jesus (ʿĪsā) as prophet and , born of the virgin Mary. It denies his divinity and, in the usual reading of 4:157, his crucifixion, and it rejects the Trinity as understood by its hearers. Scholars debate which Christian groups and texts were known in the Qur'an's milieu.
  • Early encounters. John of Damascus treated Islam as a Christian "heresy" (On Heresies 100). Arabic Christian theology developed (Theodore Abu Qurrah; ʿAmmār al-Baṣrī). The Timothy I debated Caliph al-Mahdi (c. 782); the account of the dialogue survives.1
  • Life under Islamic rule. Dhimmī status (XIII.3). Conversion over centuries; historians debate its pace (Richard Bulliet's "conversion curve" models). Christians participated in the translation movement (V.1).2
  • Conflict and exchange. Crusades and Reconquista; the Ottoman advance and the sieges of Vienna (1529, 1683); Latin translations of Arabic science and philosophy (X.3); Francis and al-Kamil (X.4).
  • Modern relations. Colonialism and mission; Nostra aetate 3 (1965) on esteem for Muslims; A Common Word (2007), an open letter from Muslim scholars to Christian leaders; the Document on Human Fraternity (Abu Dhabi, 2019), signed by Pope Francis and the Grand Imam of al-Azhar. Persecution of Christians in some Muslim-majority states, and of Muslims in some Christian-majority contexts, remains a reality (XV.7).

In depth

Christians and Muslims: encounters and arguments

Islam emerged in the seventh century in an Arabian environment that included Christians and Jews. The Qur'an honours Jesus (Isa) as a prophet and Messiah born of the virgin Mary, but denies that he is God's son and, in most readings, that he was crucified (4:157). Christian communities of Syria, Iraq and Egypt came under Muslim rule within a decade of the Prophet's death; they were the majority population in many regions for centuries afterwards.

Early Christian responses treated Islam as a Christian heresy, as John of Damascus did in his Fount of Knowledge, or engaged it in Arabic debate: the Timothy I of the Church of the East debated the caliph al-Mahdi around 781, and Christian translators such as Hunayn ibn Ishaq made Greek philosophy and medicine available in Arabic.

Western Europe met Islam chiefly through war (Spain, the Crusades, the Ottoman advance) but also through scholarship: Peter the Venerable of Cluny commissioned the first Latin translation of the Qur'an (1143), and Muslim philosophers shaped scholastic theology. Francis of Assisi met Sultan al-Kamil in Egypt in 1219.

Modern relations were transformed by Vatican II's Nostra aetate (1965), which said that the Church 'regards with esteem' Muslims who 'adore the one God', and by initiatives such as the Muslim scholars' letter A Common Word Between Us and You (2007) and the Document on Human Fraternity signed by Pope Francis and the Grand Imam of al-Azhar in Abu Dhabi (2019).
