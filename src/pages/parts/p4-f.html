In depth

How the Marian doctrines developed

The New Testament gives Mary a modest but significant role: the annunciation, the birth narratives, Cana, the cross and the upper room. Second-century writers, especially Justin and Irenaeus, developed the parallel between Eve and Mary: Eve's disobedience was undone by Mary's obedience. The Protoevangelium of James (c. 150) told of her birth and childhood and asserted her virginity during and after the birth of Jesus.

The title , 'God-bearer', was in use in Alexandria by the early fourth century and was confirmed at Ephesus (431) as a statement about Christ: the one born of Mary is God the Word in the flesh. Her perpetual virginity became general belief and was assumed by later councils. Feasts of her Dormition (falling asleep) spread from Jerusalem and were fixed on 15 August in the Byzantine Empire around 600.

In the West the question of whether Mary was conceived without original sin was long debated: Bernard of Clairvaux and Thomas Aquinas did not accept it; Duns Scotus defended it. Pius IX defined the Immaculate Conception in 1854 (see the definition), and Pius XII defined the Assumption of Mary body and soul into heavenly glory in 1950 (Munificentissimus Deus), leaving open whether she died first.

The Orthodox venerate Mary intensely and believe in her Dormition and bodily translation, but do not accept the Immaculate Conception, which they see as unnecessary given their understanding of original sin, nor papal definitions. Luther, Zwingli and Calvin all accepted her perpetual virginity and honoured her as Theotokos; later Protestants largely dropped Marian devotion, though ecumenical dialogues (for example the Anglican–Roman Catholic Mary: Grace and Hope in Christ, 2005) have revisited it.
