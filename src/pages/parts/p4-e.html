The history of Christian worship

3 min

The earliest Christian worship joined a common meal with the Lord's Supper, prayer and teaching.

Starring

  • Eucharist. The moved from a meal in households (II.8) to set prayers led by a bishop or presbyter. The Didache and Justin (2nd c.) are the earliest witnesses (III.8). In the 4th century came great basilicas and fixed anaphoras: in Syria, the Liturgies of St James and of Addai and Mari; in Byzantium, those of St Basil and St John Chrysostom; in Alexandria, St Mark; in Rome, the Roman Canon. Latin became Rome's liturgical language in the 4th century. Medieval Western developments included private Masses, elevation of the host (c. 1200) and infrequent lay communion; Lateran IV required communion once a year (X.5). The reformers restored the vernacular and communion in both kinds. Trent and Pius V's Missal (1570) standardised the Roman Rite, until Vatican II's reform (1969/70).1
  • Initiation. Adult catechumenate and Easter baptism (3rd–4th c.). Later infant baptism became general, and the West separated confirmation from baptism. The Rite of Christian Initiation of Adults (1972) restored the catechumenate. Believers' baptism was revived by and Baptists (XI.4).
  • The liturgical year. Easter is the oldest feast; its date was disputed (Quartodecimans, VII.1; Nicaea, IV.6). Christmas on 25 December is attested at Rome by 336 (the Chronograph of 354). Epiphany is older in the East. Lent took shape in the 4th century. Saints' days grew from martyrs' anniversaries (III.2). The Gregorian calendar (1582) divided the dating of Easter between East and West.2
  • Daily prayer. The Liturgy of the Hours developed from monastic and cathedral offices. Cranmer simplified it into Morning and Evening Prayer.
  • Music. Psalmody; Ephrem's Syriac hymns; Byzantine chant; Gregorian chant (Carolingian standardisation, 9th c.); polyphony (Notre-Dame, 12th–13th c.); Luther's chorales; metrical psalms (Geneva); English hymnody (Watts, Wesley); spirituals and gospel music; Taizé; contemporary worship music.
  • Architecture and images. House-churches (Dura-Europos, III.8); basilicas; Byzantine domed churches; Romanesque and Gothic (X.8); Reformed meeting-houses; Baroque (XII.2); modern liturgical architecture. On images, see VIII.3 and VI.7.
  • Vestments. Vestments derive from late Roman secular dress, such as the alb (tunic) and the chasuble (from the cloak paenula), and became distinctive from about the 6th century. Colour sequences were standardised in the West in the medieval period. Reformed churches adopted the academic gown.

In depth

From house meals to the great liturgies

The earliest Christian worship joined a common meal with the Lord's Supper, prayer and teaching. By around 155 Justin Martyr describes a recognisable pattern for Sunday: readings from the apostles and prophets, a sermon, common prayers, the kiss of peace, the presider's thanksgiving over bread and wine mixed with water, communion, and a collection for the needy.

After 313 Christians built basilicas, and worship became public and ceremonial. Regional families of liturgy took shape: the Roman, Gallican and Mozarabic in the West; the Byzantine, whose Divine Liturgies of John Chrysostom and Basil are still used; and the West Syrian, East Syrian, Coptic, Ethiopian and Armenian rites. The church year developed around Easter, with Christmas added in the fourth century.

In the medieval West the Mass was in Latin, and ordinary people often received communion rarely, perhaps once a year after 1215. The Reformers introduced worship in the vernacular, congregational singing and frequent preaching: Luther's German Mass (1526), Calvin's Geneva liturgy and the Book of Common Prayer (1549). The Council of Trent responded with a standard Roman Missal (1570).

The twentieth-century liturgical movement led to the Second Vatican Council's constitution Sacrosanctum Concilium (1963), which permitted the vernacular and encouraged 'full, conscious and active participation'. Many Protestant churches revised their liturgies in parallel, while Pentecostal worship, with its spontaneity and music, spread worldwide.
