In depth

Persecuted and persecutors: a sober reckoning

Pre-Constantinian persecution was real but intermittent. Most historians estimate the number of martyrs in the hundreds or low thousands rather than the hundreds of thousands once claimed; the Great Persecution of 303–311 was the most severe. Its memory shaped Christian identity profoundly.

Within decades of gaining power, Christians used it against others. Theodosian laws from the 380s penalised heretics and pagan sacrifice. In 415 a Christian mob in Alexandria murdered the philosopher Hypatia. Augustine, after initially opposing it, came to defend imperial coercion of the Donatists, a precedent later cited to justify force against dissent.

The medieval and early modern record includes the Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229), the inquisitions (from the 1230s, and the Spanish from 1478, whose executions modern research puts in the low thousands), the expulsions and massacres of Jews, the wars of religion culminating in the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648), and the witch trials, which killed perhaps 40,000 to 60,000 people, mostly women, between about 1450 and 1750.

Christian voices for tolerance also emerged: Sebastian Castellio after the execution of Servetus in 1553, Roger Williams in Rhode Island, John Locke's Letter Concerning Toleration (1689), and the Second Vatican Council's Declaration on Religious Liberty (1965). In the twentieth century Christians were again among the most persecuted groups, under Soviet, Nazi and other regimes.
