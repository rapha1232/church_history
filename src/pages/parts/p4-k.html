Transmission and preservation

3 minPreviously: Study J — Church and state

Early Christians adopted the codex, the ancestor of the book, while most literature was still copied on scrolls.

Starring

  • The codex. Christians adopted the codex (book) rather than the scroll unusually early. Most 2nd–4th century Christian manuscripts are codices.1
  • Translations. Each major translation carried Christianity into a new culture:
    • Old Latin and the Vulgate (Jerome, late 4th c.);
    • Syriac (the Peshitta) and Coptic;
    • Gothic (Ulfila, 4th c.);
    • Armenian (5th c.) and Ge'ez;
    • Georgian;
    • Old Church Slavonic (9th c.);
    • Arabic (from the 8th–9th c.);
    • later the vernacular Bibles of the Reformation and modern missions. Scripture has been translated in full into over 700 languages. That figure is reported annually by Bible societies and was not verified for this edition.
  • Libraries and monasteries. Caesarea (Origen, Pamphilus, Eusebius); Constantinople; Irish and Anglo-Saxon monasteries; the Carolingian scriptoria and minuscule script (most surviving classical Latin texts depend on Carolingian copies); St Catherine's, Sinai; Mount Athos; the Ethiopian monasteries; the Syriac manuscripts of Deir al-Surian (Egypt).
  • Movement between cultures. Greek philosophy passed through Syriac and Arabic into Latin (X.3). Byzantine scholars brought Greek texts to Italy (15th c.). Printing (from the 1450s; XI.1).
  • Rediscoveries. The Didache (1873); Nag Hammadi (1945); the Dead Sea Scrolls (1947) (I.4); the Sinai "new finds" (1975). Digitisation projects, such as the Codex Sinaiticus Project (2009) and the Vatican Library, make manuscripts widely available today.2
  • Losses. Destruction in wars and conquests: Constantinople 1204 and 1453, Baghdad 1258; deliberate burnings, such as Diamper (1599) and Lollard books; and destruction in 20th-century conflicts, including Iraq and Syria since 2003.

In depth

How the texts reached us

Early Christians adopted the codex, the ancestor of the book, while most literature was still copied on scrolls. Papyrus fragments such as P52, a scrap of John's Gospel usually dated to the early or mid second century, and the Chester Beatty and Bodmer papyri of around 200 are among the earliest witnesses. The great parchment codices Sinaiticus and Vaticanus date from the fourth century.

About 5,800 Greek manuscripts of the New Testament survive, plus thousands in Latin, Syriac, Coptic and other languages, and countless quotations in the Fathers. They differ in hundreds of thousands of small ways, mostly spelling and word order; a few variants, such as the ending of Mark and the story of the woman taken in adultery (John 7:53–8:11), are significant and are noted in modern Bibles.

Monasteries preserved much of ancient literature, Christian and classical. Cassiodorus's Vivarium in sixth-century Italy, the Irish and Northumbrian scriptoria, and the Carolingian reforms with their clear 'minuscule' script were vital links. In Byzantium the ninth-century switch to minuscule script saved many texts by recopying them.

Printing changed everything. The Gutenberg Bible appeared around 1455, and Erasmus published the first printed Greek New Testament in 1516. Modern textual criticism, from Lachmann and Tischendorf, who brought Codex Sinaiticus to light between 1844 and 1859, to the Nestle–Aland editions, compares manuscripts systematically. The discoveries at Nag Hammadi (1945) and Qumran (1947) and today's digitized manuscripts continue to add to what we know.
