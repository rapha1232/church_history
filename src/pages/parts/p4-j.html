In depth

Models of church and state

Christianity has lived under almost every possible relation to political power. Before Constantine it was an illegal minority that nonetheless prayed for the emperor (Romans 13; 1 Timothy 2). After Theodosius it became the established religion of the Roman Empire, and the Byzantine ideal of 'symphony' (Justinian, Novel 6) described emperor and priesthood as two gifts of God working in harmony.

In the West, Gelasius I's letter of 494 distinguished the 'sacred authority of bishops' from the 'royal power' (VII.4). The medieval papacy built on this to claim superiority over rulers, culminating in Unam sanctam (1302). Kings and emperors in turn claimed authority over their churches, and the investiture conflict (X.1) was fought over this boundary.

The Reformation produced several models: Lutheran territorial churches under princely authority; Calvin's Geneva, where church and magistrate were distinct but cooperated in discipline; the Church of England under royal supremacy; and the Anabaptist rejection of the sword altogether. The principle cuius regio, eius religio (1555) made the ruler's faith that of the territory.

Modern models include separation (the United States, 1791; France's law of 1905 on laïcité), concordats (Napoleon in 1801; the Lateran Treaty of 1929), established national churches (England, Denmark, until 2000 Sweden), the Orthodox pattern of national churches closely tied to national identity, and authoritarian control (the Soviet Union, China). Vatican II's Dignitatis humanae (1965) accepted religious freedom as a right grounded in human dignity, which ended Catholic support for confessional states in principle.
