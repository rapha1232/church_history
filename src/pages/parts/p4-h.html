Starring

  1. Jewish origins. Jesus, his disciples and Paul were Jews. Christianity's scriptures, worship and hope come from Second Temple Judaism (Vol. I; II.4).
  2. Differentiation. A long process of separation, not one event (II.7).
  3. The adversus Iudaeos tradition. From Barnabas, Justin and Melito, through John Chrysostom's sermons Against the Jews (386–87), to the medieval charge of "deicide". Augustine's "witness" doctrine both protected Jews and justified their subordination.1
  4. Medieval violence and legislation. The 1096 massacres; Lateran IV (X.4); blood libels, starting at Norwich in 1144; the Talmud burned in Paris (1242); expulsions; Spanish conversos and the (X.5); ghettos (Venice, 1516; Rome, 1555).2
  5. Reformation. Luther's 1543 writings (XI.2). In the Dutch Republic and England (readmission in 1656) Jews were tolerated.
  6. Modern antisemitism and the Holocaust. Racial antisemitism; Church responses (XV.1).
  7. Reconciliation. Nostra aetate 4 (1965); John Paul II at the Rome synagogue (1986) and the Western Wall (2000); The Gifts and the Calling of God Are Irrevocable (2015); Protestant rejections of supersessionism; Jewish responses such as Dabru Emet (2000).3

In depth

Christianity and Judaism: from rivalry to dialogue

Christianity began as a movement within Judaism, and its Scriptures include the Jewish Bible. As the two separated (II.7), Christian writers developed a 'teaching of contempt': the claim that the Jews had killed Christ, were rejected by God and had been replaced by the Church (supersessionism). Augustine's view that Jews should be preserved as witnesses to the truth of Scripture, in a subordinate state, shaped medieval policy: Jews were protected from forced conversion in principle by papal bulls (Sicut Judaeis, from about 1120), but subjected to restrictions, accusations and recurrent violence (X.4).

The Reformation did not end this. Luther's late treatise On the Jews and Their Lies (1543) urged burning synagogues; it was later exploited by the Nazis. In Catholic Europe, ghettos were established, beginning in Venice (1516) and in Rome by Paul IV (1555).

After the Holocaust, many churches concluded that Christian anti-Judaism had prepared the ground for modern racial antisemitism. The Seelisberg conference (1947) proposed ten points for Christian teaching. Vatican II's Nostra aetate (1965) stated that what happened in Christ's passion 'cannot be charged against all the Jews, without distinction, then alive, nor against the Jews of today', and that the Jews should not be presented as rejected or accursed. John Paul II visited the Rome synagogue in 1986 and prayed at the Western Wall in 2000. The Vatican and Israel established diplomatic relations in 1993.

Protestant churches made similar statements, including the Evangelical Church in the Rhineland (1980). Jewish scholars responded in Dabru Emet (2000). Questions still under discussion include mission to Jews, the theological meaning of the State of Israel, and how Christians should read New Testament texts critical of 'the Jews'.
